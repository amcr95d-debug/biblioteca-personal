Pictorial Apocalypse:
Blake's "Great Red Dragon and the Woman Clothed with the Sun"

Terence Allan Hoagwood

BLAKE's watercolor, "The Great Red Dragon and the Woman Clothed with the Sun," illustrates an episode from Revelation xii; as usual, however, Blake does not pictorialize that narrative, but instead transforms its symbols and their values. I shall argue that two principles embodied forcefully in this design derive from apocalyptic tradition: aesthetically, pictorial form furnishes a fresh conceptualization of a narrative theme, even altering the values embedded in the narrative. Ontologically, Blake presents an "intellectual vision" (to quote one of his letters to William Hayley)[1]; that is, the painting expresses Blake's absolute idealism according to which "Mental Things are alone Real."[2] These principles make up the theoretical framework within which the minute particulars of Blake's painting unfold their significance; these principles in turn have a place in a coherent tradition of apocalyptic literature and commentary, as I shall show, but Blake modifies in important ways the radical Protestantism that was his heritage.

One pictorial element by which Blake effects a transformation is the stationing of bodies in the design. In the biblical text, the "woman clothed with the sun . . . being with child cried, travailing in birth, . . . and the dragon stood before the woman" (Rev. xii, 1,2,4). In Blake's painting, the woman shows no pain, is not crying, and shows a radiant confidence missing from the biblical account (see fig.). Further, the dragon is not "before" the woman, but rather directly above her. Her apparent vigor, vertical posture, and her placement below the dragon, rather than beside him, together embody a recurring motif in Blake's portrayals of visionary experience. In his illustrations, a seer with visionary power is commonly portrayed beneath the vision that he sees; thus in "Isaiah Foretelling the Destruction of Jerusalem" (a pencil drawing on wood block), the speaking prophet also gestures upward, where his own conceptualization is displayed in the upper portion of the picture-space. In both designs, the seer's vision is portrayed as a horizontal mass above the seer's head.

This positioning of bodies is one of Blake's pathos formulae for visionary composition; that is, it represents "a limited range of body configurations and placements of the human form in space which communicates a definable range of mental and emotional states," as Jenijoy La Belle says.[3] Specifically, Janet A. Warner calls attention to the recurrent form of hovering figures with outstretched arms: Warner suggests that this positioning of bodies develops "connotations of divinity separated in some way from the whole man or human form"; the divine quality of humanity is thus in danger of estrangement. In the "Great Red Dragon," the "hovering figure is . . . taken to its ultimate Satanic form," says Warner, and its implications are intrapsychic: "these are two aspects of the same psyche."[4]

That language of identification leads, however, to a misrepresentation of Blake's design: to say that "the positions of woman and dragon reflect each other" is to assign them the same ontological status, whereas in this painting, as in his biblical illustrations generally, the hovering figure is rather an imagined concept belonging to the mentality of the seer positioned below. Long ago Joseph A. Wicksteed noticed this important motif in Blake's Job illustrations: "we see into the spiritual life of Job. For Blake represents the inner by the higher, 'What is Above,' he says, 'is Within.' "[5] Wicksteed's instance is the second illustration in the Job series, and Kathleen Raine has also called the deity portrayed there, above Job, the "God Within."[6] In fact, throughout the Illustrations of the Book of Job Blake portrays Job's vision of the divine above his head; these images of God are thus exteriorizations of elements within the mentality of Job. Often these visions of deity are represented as a horizontal mass, as the dragon is shown in the watercolor.

Another common element among the Job designs and the "Great Red Dragon" is the satanic character of the seer's conceptualization. In the sixth illustration in the Job series, for example ("And smote Job with sore Boils"), the figure spread above is nominally Satan; in what amounts to a gnostic revision of the Bible, Blake repeats this same vision, with the same posturing, in illustration 11 ("With Dreams upon my bed"), but here he places the God of Job, Elohim, in the role of Satan. In the printed illustration and also in the watercolor version that belongs to the same series as the "Great Red Dragon" ("Job's Evil Dreams"), the satanic tormentor has both the face of Elohim and also the stone tablets of the Moral Law; he is wrapped round by a serpent, exactly as Satan is in the watercolor, "Satan Watching the Endearments of Adam and Eve." In Revelation xii the dragon is named "that old serpent, called the Devil, and Satan"; Blake's biblical illustrations, thus, link the satanic figures in Job and in Revelation, assigning them to the same placement within his visionary configuration. A symbolic idiom common to both the Job designs and the "Great Red Dragon" is not even hinted in the Bible: this is the portrayal of the seer's visionary idea as a horizontal mass above the seer's head.

Another watercolor, "Job Confessing His Presumption to God Who Answers from the Whirlwind," also belongs to the same series of biblical paintings, which was commissioned by Thomas Butts.[7] In this case a parallel strategy is even more obvious: whereas in the printed version (no. 13 in the Job engravings) the Lord is before Job, as the dragon is before the woman in Revelation, here in the watercolor Blake moves the Lord immediately above Job's head, spreads him horizontally, and stretches his arms, just as he moves and positions the dragon in the contemporary painting of that biblical episode. Often Blake humanizes the visionary process conspicuously; the exteriorization of mental forms is presented as a psychological process, so that Blake's transformation of the biblical narratives is at once idealistic and naturalistic. In all of the Job illustrations, for example, the face of God is identical with the face of Job;[8] as the visionary is, so is the vision; "As the Eye—Such the Object" (p. 645). Pictorially, Blake reveals that a perceiver endows the visionary object with its character; the visionary act constitutes the visionary object.

Using the same visual formulation, Blake occasionally achieves a more optimistic implication than what appears in the satanic visions, and he portrays this optimism in two ways. He sometimes shows the visionary refusing a satanic vision: in "Christ Refusing the Banquet Offered by Satan," for example (a watercolor illustration to Paradise Regained), it is Christ who is the vertical seer; the horizontal figure above him is Satan, obviously, but Christ's averted gaze and his manual gesture—pushing against the vision—display his rejection of the satanic vision. Job, in contrast, submits: in Job 6, for instance, the smitten Job does press outward with his palms, but he is stretched in a horizontal and highly vulnerable position, subject to Satan utterly. Again in Job 11 and in the parallel watercolor, "Job's Evil Dreams," Job's palms press ineffectually against the vision that torments him, even while his posture—again horizontal and vulnerable—subjects him helplessly to that evil projection.

In other cases, Blake uses the visionary formulation in designs still more optimistic than "Christ Refusing." On plate 76 of Jerusalem (a full-page design without text), a radiant and cruciform Christ is placed above Albion, who gazes upward into Christ's face and whose posture imitates Christ's. Here man and his vision of God are again identified, but here both visionary and vision are vertical; further, the movement implied is upward, and the vision is brilliant, while in the Job illustrations (notably in "Behemoth and Leviathan," in the Butts series of watercolors) the gazes are downward and the vision is dark with destructive power. Clearly the same visionary configuration can embody very different conceptualizations, dark and satanic (like Job's) or brilliant and redemptive (like Albion's in Jerusalem 76). In both sorts of cases, however, the visionary stationing is the same, Blake placing the seer's projection in the upper portion of the picture-space; this variety in Blake's usage of the form invites us to contrast the alternative projections of his different human seers.

If we recall even that much about Blake's other designs, we can recognize instantly certain implications of the "Great Red Dragon" watercolor. Like Isaiah (and also like St. John in the watercolor, "Angel of the Revelation"), the "Woman Clothed with the Sun" beholds the dragon as a vision displayed horizontally above the seer. Like the serpent-wrapped tormentor which is Job's projection in "Job's Evil Dreams," the vision is satanic. Like Christ in "Christ Refusing," the woman resists the satanic vision with a posture of opposition, her palms turned outward and her arms spread in an arc exactly opposite to the dragon's. She displays the brilliant light and the upward movement that Blake uses again in plate 76 of Jerusalem, where Albion imitates and internalizes the vision of Christ. Here, the light source is the woman, and the dragon she envisions is dark above her; in contrast, the redeeming vision in Jerusalem 76 shows light emanating from above, from the object of vision itself. Significantly, the posture of inclusion belongs to the design of the Christian God of forgiveness, while the posture of opposition is dramatized in the design of the dragon accuser. Both, however, display the act of envisioning these idealizations with the same visual formula.

In fact, Blake habitually uses that visionary stationing to contrast these two visions of God. In the "Great Red Dragon," as in the design on plate 6 of Jerusalem, a bat-winged state of mind (horizontal) is presented as a contrary to the vertical visionary. In the text of Jerusalem, this bat-like vision, the Spectre, speaks of punishment and of "a Law of Sin" (7:50). Los, the vertical visionary, replies: "0 that I could abstain from wrath!" (7:59); he repudiates the wrathful vision specifically by invoking the Lamb of God, and by expressing the Christian principle of "mutual forgiveness" (7:59-60, 66). Pictorially and verbally, the doctrines of wrath and forgiveness are thus contrasted, and Christian humanism prevails: "the terrible Spectre fell shuddring before him" (8:21), vengeance falling away before mercy, and Los cries to the fallen Spectre, "arise!" (9:31).

Of course "arise" is an important directional word for the designs: plate 76 of Jerusalem uses the movement upward as well as the human embodiment of Christ's position to portray a Christian humanism. On plate 6 of Jerusalem and in the "Great Red Dragon," the downward gaze of the bat-winged beast and the upward gaze of the visionary symbolize their perspectives: wrath and destruction look downward, like Job looking at Behemoth and Leviathan. The Christian prophet, upright, typically looks upward, like John beholding the Angel of the Revelation and like the "Woman Clothed with the Sun."

Describing his own design, the "Last Judgment," Blake articulates verbally this theological contrast: "Thinking as I do that the Creator of this World [Elohim in Genesis and Job] is a very Cruel Being & being a Worshipper of Christ I cannot help saying the Son 0 how unlike the Father " (p. 565). Such contrast engages the mental and perceptual powers of Blake's audience in a mental fight, and the terms of conflict are theological, Blake opposing the cruel God of the Dragon and Serpent against the "mutual forgiveness" embodied in Christ. The 1795 color print, "Elohim Creating Adam," shows the cruel and massively winged God, with the face of Job's evil dream, engaged in the destructive work of making the worm-wrapped matter of man; his contrary, Christ, speaks most eloquently in Jerusalem, where he repudiates both materialism and wrath: "I am in you and you in me, mutual in love divine" (4:7). This same theological opposition is portrayed in the "Great Red Dragon," where the radiant "Woman Clothed with the Sun" rises on her heart-shaped wings, against the massive and stony form of the dragon in the sky.

These theological implications in Blake's painting express his direct relationship with the larger context of eighteenth-century commentaries on the Book of Revelation, just as his pictorial conceptualization extends a tradition of biblical illustration that I shall describe subsequently. Before turning to these larger contexts, however, I shall dwell a moment on another relevant sketch element in the "Great Red Dragon," because it embodies another of Blake's philosophical themes and it binds his theology to his philosophy of mind. It is no less than amazing that an artist who speaks of a "distinct, sharp . . . bounding line" as enthusiastically as Blake does (A Descriptive Catalogue, p. 550) should sketch figures as vague and ambiguous as those in the bottom right of his painting.[9] Two or perhaps three heads are dimly visible, almost as parts of the stone or cloud beneath the woman. David Bindman has suggested that these figures represent earth "opening up and swallowing the flood" which, according to the scriptural account, "the serpent cast out of his mouth."[10] To interpret the figures in that way, however, would require seeing the dragon's jagged lightning as a flood of water; further, it manifestly does not come from his mouth, the vague figures show fear and deathliness rather than positive helpfulness, and the entire scene obviously does not occur when the scriptural flood takes place, when the woman has been hidden "from the face of the serpent": instead, she stares directly in his face at short range.

This episode, then, is not that of the flood but rather the apocalyptic moment narrated, for instance, in The Four Zoas, where human souls "cry out to be delivered" under the "Dragon form"; "John saw these things Reveald in Heaven / On Patmos Isle & heard the Souls cry out" (p. 385).

The visual ambiguity of these figures, moreover, is precisely Blake's strategy for depicting materialism: he portrays human forms turning into earth or the elements. Thus on plate 11 of The Marriage of Heaven and Hell, Blake's illumination shows human beings turning into earth and water; on plate 3 of Jerusalem, a human form falls into the dead trunk of a tree, and another human form becomes the tree itself. The Marriage of Heaven and Hell places the relation in a theological framework: "The ancient Poets animated all sensible objects with Gods or Geniuses," but then "a system was formed, which some took advantage of & enslaved the vulgar by attempting to realize or abstract the mental deities from their objects: thus began Priesthood." For Blake, "All deities reside in the human breast" (Marriage, plate 11); this theology is a corollary of his principle, "All Things Exist in the Human Imagination" (Jerusalem 69:25). The externality of matter and of deity are tyrannical illusions. To succumb to them is to die, to turn to stone, like those figures in the "Great Red Dragon," where Blake depicts what in The Four Zoas he had called the "stony form of death a dragon of the Deeps" (p. 389). Materialism and the concomitant God of externality form therefore one vision, whose effect on human beings who entertain it is depicted in the watercolor.

That Blake's exegesis takes a pictorial form should not surprise his Christian audience: the Apocalypse had always attracted artists. Albrecht Dürer's set of woodcuts on the Apocalypse (1498) is one influential group of designs, and it is also that artist's first major work. In the first edition of Luther's New Testament (published in Wittenberg by Melchior Lotther in 1522 and conventionally called the "September Bible"), the Book of Revelation is singled out as the only book with large, full-page illustrations, including one of the "Great Red Dragon and the Woman Clothed with the Sun," which resembles Dürer's design in many important respects. In the first edition of Luther's complete Bible (published in Wittenberg by Hans Lufft in 1534), the Book of Revelation is illustrated again, with twenty-six pictures rather than the twenty-one of the September Bible. This time, the illustration of the "Great Red Dragon and the Woman Clothed with the Sun" loses the resemblance to Dürer's design, in fact reversing the position of the figures. Hans Holbein the Younger executed a set of twenty-one woodcuts illustrating the Book of Revelation (these illustrations appear in the New Testament of 1523, published in Basle by T. Wolff). Holbein's "Great Red Dragon and the Woman Clothed with the Sun" clearly belongs to the tradition of Dürer and the September Bible, combining elements from both for a design at once more complex and dynamic.[11]

Those among early printed editions of the English Bible which include illustrations represent the Book of Revelation disproportionately. One important instance is the 1534 printing of William Tyndale's translation of the New Testament: of the thirty-nine woodcuts included in this volume, twenty-two illustrate the Book of Revelation, and these illustrations are larger than the rest.[12]

This obvious preference among illustrators has an exegetical basis: Martin Luther, among others, notices that the Book of Revelation is a pictorial prophecy: "there is no prophet in the Old Testament, to say nothing of the New, who deals so out and out with visions and figures" as John does in the Apocalypse.[13] John's prophecy "is purely visionary and consists only of 'pictures,' remaining 'a concealed and dumb prophecy' until the pictures, once interpreted, are made to yield up hidden meaning."[14] Similarly, Henry Hammond writes of the visions in Revelation that they appeared to St. John as "Symbols or visible Hieroglyphs."[15] Aptly, too, Leslie Tannenbaum observes that Samuel Horsley "reiterated the traditional idea that biblical prophecy took the form of 'emblematic pictures' that were presented by the Divine Spirit to the prophet's imagination";[16] what is more, the pertinent emblematic tradition is that described by Sambucus, for instance, in the Emblemata of 1566: its aim is "to suggest symbols and set forth something rather obscure which requires clarification and cogitation."[17] It is of this emblematic tradition, associated with biblical prophecy by Samuel Horsley, that Paulo Giovio writes in 1599: "the picture is the body and the text is the soul."[18]

Giovio's distinction suggests what Blake's work also makes obvious: the pictorial presentation cannot simply reproduce the concepts of the verbal work, but must transform them: "Between word and image, between what is depicted by language and what is uttered by plastic form, the unity begins to dissolve; a single and identical meaning is not immediately common to them," says Michel Foucault, whose argument may be applied here to Blake's transformations of scriptural themes—"By its own plastic values painting engages in an experiment that will take it farther and farther from language, whatever the superficial identity of the theme."[19] Specifically, Blake's painting, taking as its subject the act of vision, directly engages its viewer in that act, and Blake allows this property of the visual medium to affect his theme. Presenting a portrayal of visionary imagination as a constitutive act of the beholder, as a human psychological process, Blake simultaneously involves his own audience in exactly that act of visualization. Blake's audience must see the portrayal of vision; John's audience can only hear of it after the fact, as John reports, "I John saw these things" (Rev. xxii, 8). Each beholder of Blake's design is immediately involved in the vision; John's reader must rely on the hearsay authority of the visionary, being denied direct access to the vision itself.

Arising from the visual medium, this fact of audience engagement returns us to the theme of conceptualization. Blake insists repeatedly on the conversion of tangible to intellectual terms. Thus he says, in his preface to his illuminated prophecy, Milton, "I will not cease from Mental Fight," emphasizing what he says again in A Vision of the Last Judgment: "the Writings of the Prophets illustrate . . . conceptions of the Visionary Fancy by their various sublime & Divine Images as seen in the Worlds of Vision" (p. 555). Explicitly here, Blake identifies the act of conceptualization as the subject of the prophetic work; his visual art, interpreting the scriptural passage even while illustrating it, serves at once as an instance of that intellectual act and also as a commentary on it.

That dual function of prophecy, as vision and as commentary, was also traditional. Sir Isaac Newton, for example, had observed that each biblical prophecy comprises vision and concept, picture and interpretation.[20] Prior to Newton's commentary, Joseph Mede had popularized an understanding of the prophecy's structure as partly pictorial and partly verbal: some visions there were "painted in certain shapes," and some were written in language; vision and exposition are "joyned together."[21] This structural concept is obviously germane to the composite art of Blake's illuminated poems, which culminate in the greatest of his works, Jerusalem, a poem which is itself a visionary interpretation of the Apocalypse. In the "Great Red Dragon" as well, a dual intent is important: like his other biblical paintings, this one is partly a portrayal of scriptural imagery (an illustration strictly) and partly a vision at once conceptual and new.[22]

According to Thomas Goodwin (1639), the Book of Revelation is itself both a picture and a concept: the prophecy embodies a "Portrait of the Holy Ghost's Mind."[23] Robert Lowth writes that biblical prophecy employs chiefly visual imagery, but it is "imagery . . . transferred from certain and definite objects to express indefinite and general ideas."[24] Similarly, William Jones points out that the Bible applies "all visible objects to a figurative use."[25] Committing his own visionary art to these ideational ends, Blake also exalts the ontological value of such mental forms: the "Permanent Realities of Every Thing" exist in "This World ." To illustrate the given prophecy would be the work of memory; Blake's work, he says, is the work of inspiration (A Vision of the Last Judgment, pp. 555, 554).

That conceptual basis of his art helps to define for Blake a place within his eighteenth-century context. Specifically, the traditional opposition of idealistic (and Augustinian) interpretations against earthly (and chiliastic) interpretations reappears in the eighteenth century with a special focus on the American and French revolutions. Blake uses his philosophical idealism to unify these contrary visions of the biblical prophecy. Joseph Priestley, for example, writes that the American and French revolutions were "distinctly and repeatedly foretold in [biblical] prophecies."[26] James Bicheno argues that the French Revolution "is undoubtedly the theme of prophecy."[27] The sermons of John Willison (d. 1750) were published long after his death, with the title, A Prophecy of the French Revolution (1793). According to these chiliastic interpretations, the seven-headed beast is a type of the political tyrant, and the Whore of Babylon is his ecclesiastical counterpart. Thus was Antichrist among the seventeenth-century Puritans,[28] and thus implied Dürer, much earlier, when he portrayed bishops, not devils, overcome by heavenly angels in the War in Heaven. Blake implies this same identification when he writes that "The Beast & the Whore rule without controls" (p. 611). Consistently with his stated doctrine on the Book of Revelation—that "Mental Things are alone Real" (A Vision of the Last Judgment, p. 565)—Blake goes on to intellectualize the beast and the dragon, and in fact all prophetic symbolism. He finds authority in (for example) John Wesley's Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament, where Wesley explicates the dragon as an idea (hatred of faith).[29] Blake also found support for his idealist reading in Swedenborg, who states in one of his commentaries on the Apocalypse that "every truly spiritual meaning is abstracted from the idea of persons, places, and times."[30] Blake opposes the interpretations of Isaac Newton, who argues that the dragon is specifically a "type of the numerous armies of Arabians invading the Romans." Whereas Newton identifies the four beasts of the Apocalypse as geographical regions in Mesopotamia,[31] Blake locates them "in every Man" (The Four Zoas, p. 300).

Typically, in the eighteenth century, it is the conservative and the orthodox who argue for utterly de-temporalized interpretations; Charles Walmesley's Catholic commentary on the Apocalypse is thus devoted to "the great and awful Truths . . . of Religion," and not to radical politics.[32] But Blake's idealism (like that of John Smith, the Cambridge Platonist) allows him to locate the "Prophetical scene or Stage" in the Imagination, not in terrestrial matter and affairs.[33] At the same time, Blake's radicalism endows his vision with particular purpose; ideational realities are susceptible of many manifestations, and the beast is thus the ally of the bishop; Blake's apocalyptic "Song of Liberty" ends with the cry that "Empire is no more" (p. 45).

Thus Blake's radical idealism marries the heaven of spiritual vision with the hell of earthly wars and revolution. Accordingly, the "Great Red Dragon" embodies two of the themes of Blake's life-work and of his century: first, "Human thought is crushed [or threatened] beneath the iron hand of Power" (Milton, 25:5), and this is the political dimension of the watercolor; second, redemption (both political and spiritual renewal) is to be achieved by visionary means: "All of us on earth are united in thought" (p. 600); it is "Thought [that] chang'd the infinite to a serpent" (Europe, 10:16), and which changed a mental power of the universal Man to a "stony form of death [a] dragon of the Deeps." And thus we can recover infinity: "Man is All Imagination God is Man & exists in us & we in him" (p. 664). The "Great Red Dragon" in the watercolor is a vision, then, like the materialistic Elohim who created Adam; it is the action of the "Woman Clothed with the Sun" first to perceive this vision, and then to oppose and to overcome it. This rendition of the Apocalypse of John is therefore no rendition at all, but a new work that challenges the old, embracing its imagery to transform it. The Christian prophecy provides for Blake's work what he ascribed to apocalyptic art: not a master text suitable for imitation, but rather an imagistic and symbolic language, suitable for the sublimer task, the "Visions of Regeneration" (Jerusalem, 24:45).

The Kekulé Problem

Where did language come from?

Cormac McCarthy

11:49 AM GMT-5 on April 17, 2017 • Nautilus

Illustration by Don Kilpatrick III

Cormac McCarthy is best known to the world as a writer of novels. These include Blood Meridian, All the Pretty Horses, No Country for Old Men, and The Road. At the Santa Fe Institute (SFI) he is a research colleague and thought of in complementary terms. An aficionado on subjects ranging from the history of mathematics, philosophical arguments relating to the status of quantum mechanics as a causal theory, comparative evidence bearing on non-human intelligence, and the nature of the conscious and unconscious mind. At SFI we have been searching for the expression of these scientific interests in his novels and we maintain a furtive tally of their covert manifestations and demonstrations in his prose.

Over the last two decades Cormac and I have been discussing the puzzles and paradoxes of the unconscious mind. Foremost among them, the fact that the very recent and "uniquely" human capability of near infinite expressive power arising through a combinatorial grammar is built on the foundations of a far more ancient animal brain. How have these two evolutionary systems become reconciled? Cormac expresses this tension as the deep suspicion, perhaps even contempt, that the primeval unconscious feels toward the upstart, conscious language. In this article Cormac explores this idea through processes of dream and infection. It is a discerning and wide-ranging exploration of ideas and challenges that our research community has only recently dared to start addressing through complexity science.

— David Krakauer
President and William H. Miller Professor of Complex Systems, Santa Fe Institute

I call it the Kekulé Problem because among the myriad instances of scientific problems solved in the sleep of the inquirer Kekulé's is probably the best known. He was trying to arrive at the configuration of the benzene molecule and not making much progress when he fell asleep in front of the fire and had his famous dream of a snake coiled in a hoop with its tail in its mouth—the ouroboros of mythology—and woke exclaiming to himself: "It's a ring. The molecule is in the form of a ring." Well. The problem of course—not Kekulé's but ours—is that since the unconscious understands language perfectly well or it would not understand the problem in the first place, why doesn't it simply answer Kekulé's question with something like: "Kekulé, it's a bloody ring." To which our scientist might respond: "Okay. Got it. Thanks."

Why the snake? That is, why is the unconscious so loathe to speak to us? Why the images, metaphors, pictures? Why the dreams, for that matter.

A logical place to begin would be to define what the unconscious is in the first place. To do this we have to set aside the jargon of modern psychology and get back to biology. The unconscious is a biological system before it is anything else. To put it as pithily as possibly—and as accurately—the unconscious is a machine for operating an animal.

All animals have an unconscious. If they didn't they would be plants. We may sometimes credit ours with duties it doesn't actually perform. Systems at a certain level of necessity may require their own mechanics of governance. Breathing, for instance, is not controlled by the unconscious but by the pons and the medulla oblongata, two systems located in the brainstem. Except of course in the case of cetaceans, who have to breathe when they come up for air. An autonomous system wouldn't work here. The first dolphin anesthetized on an operating table simply died. (How do they sleep? With half of their brain alternately.) But the duties of the unconscious are beyond counting. Everything from scratching an itch to solving math problems.

Did language meet some need? No. The other five thousand plus mammals among us do fine without it.

Problems in general are often well posed in terms of language and language remains a handy tool for explaining them. But the actual process of thinking—in any discipline—is largely an unconscious affair. Language can be used to sum up some point at which one has arrived—a sort of milepost—so as to gain a fresh starting point. But if you believe that you actually use language in the solving of problems I wish that you would write to me and tell me how you go about it.

I've pointed out to some of my mathematical friends that the unconscious appears to be better at math than they are. My friend George Zweig calls this the Night Shift. Bear in mind that the unconscious has no pencil or notepad and certainly no eraser. That it does solve problems in mathematics is indisputable. How does it go about it? When I've suggested to my friends that it may well do it without using numbers, most of them thought—after a while—that this was a possibility. How, we don't know. Just as we don't know how it is that we manage to talk. If I am talking to you then I can hardly be crafting at the same time the sentences that are to follow what I am now saying. I am totally occupied in talking to you. Nor can some part of my mind be assembling these sentences and then saying them to me so that I can repeat them. Aside from the fact that I am busy this would be to evoke an endless regress. The truth is that there is a process here to which we have no access. It is a mystery opaque to total blackness.

There are influential persons among us—of whom a bit more a bit later—who claim to believe that language is a totally evolutionary process. That it has somehow appeared in the brain in a primitive form and then grown to usefulness. Somewhat like vision, perhaps. But vision we now know is traceable to perhaps as many as a dozen quite independent evolutionary histories. Tempting material for the teleologists. These stories apparently begin with a crude organ capable of perceiving light where any occlusion could well suggest a predator. Which actually makes it an excellent scenario for Darwinian selection. It may be that the influential persons imagine all mammals waiting for language to appear. I don't know. But all indications are that language has appeared only once and in one species only. Among whom it then spread with considerable speed.

There are a number of examples of signaling in the animal world that might be taken for a proto-language. Chipmunks—among other species—have one alarm-call for aerial predators and another for those on the ground. Hawks as distinct from foxes or cats. Very useful. But what is missing here is the central idea of language—that one thing can be another thing. It is the idea that Helen Keller suddenly understood at the well. That the sign for water was not simply what you did to get a glass of water. It was the glass of water. It was in fact the water in the glass. This in the play The Miracle Worker. Not a dry eye in the house.

The invention of language was understood at once to be incredibly useful. Again, it seems to have spread through the species almost instantaneously. The immediate problem would seem to have been that there were more things to name than sounds to name them with. Language appears to have originated in southwestern Africa and it may even be that the clicks in the Khoisan languages—to include Sandawe and Hadza—are an atavistic remnant of addressing this need for a greater variety of sounds. The vocal problems were eventually handled evolutionarily—and apparently in fairly short order—by turning our throat over largely to the manufacture of speech. Not without cost, as it turns out. The larynx has moved down in the throat in such a way as to make us as a species highly vulnerable to choking on our food—a not uncommon cause of death. It's also left us as the only mammal incapable of swallowing and vocalizing at the same time.

The sort of isolation that gave us tall and short and light and dark and other variations in our species was no protection against the advance of language. It crossed mountains and oceans as if they weren't there. Did it meet some need? No. The other five thousand plus mammals among us do fine without it. But useful? Oh yes. We might further point out that when it arrived it had no place to go. The brain was not expecting it and had made no plans for its arrival. It simply invaded those areas of the brain that were the least dedicated. I suggested once in conversation at the Santa Fe Institute that language had acted very much like a parasitic invasion and David Krakauer—our president—said that the same idea had occurred to him. Which pleased me a good deal because David is very smart. This is not to say of course that the human brain was not in any way structured for the reception of language. Where else would it go? If nothing else we have the evidence of history. The difference between the history of a virus and that of language is that the virus has arrived by way of Darwinian selection and language has not. The virus comes nicely machined. Offer it up. Turn it slightly. Push it in. Click. Nice fit. But the scrap heap will be found to contain any number of viruses that did not fit.

There is no selection at work in the evolution of language because language is not a biological system and because there is only one of them. The ur-language of linguistic origin out of which all languages have evolved.

Influential persons will by now of course have smiled to themselves at the ill-concealed Lamarckianism lurking here. We might think to evade it by various strategies or redefinitions but probably without much success. Darwin of course was dismissive of the idea of inherited "mutilations"—the issue of cutting off the tails of dogs for instance. But the inheritance of ideas remains something of a sticky issue. It is difficult to see them as anything other than acquired. How the unconscious goes about its work is not so much poorly understood as not understood at all. It is an area pretty much ignored by the artificial intelligence studies, which seem mostly devoted to analytics and to the question of whether the brain is like a computer. They have decided that it's not, but that is not altogether true.

Of the known characteristics of the unconscious its persistence is among the most notable. Everyone is familiar with repetitive dreams. Here the unconscious may well be imagined to have more than one voice: He's not getting it, is he? No. He's pretty thick. What do you want to do? I don't know. Do you want to try using his mother? His mother is dead. What difference does that make?

To put it as pithily as possibly—and as accurately—the unconscious is a machine for operating an animal.

What is at work here? And how does the unconscious know we're not getting it? What doesn't it know? It's hard to escape the conclusion that the unconscious is laboring under a moral compulsion to educate us. (Moral compulsion? Is he serious?)

The evolution of language would begin with the names of things. After that would come descriptions of these things and descriptions of what they do. The growth of languages into their present shape and form—their syntax and grammar—has a universality that suggests a common rule. The rule is that languages have followed their own requirements. The rule is that they are charged with describing the world. There is nothing else to describe.

All very quickly. There are no languages whose form is in a state of development. And their forms are all basically the same.

We don't know what the unconscious is or where it is or how it got there—wherever there might be. Recent animal brain studies showing outsized cerebellums in some pretty smart species are suggestive. That facts about the world are in themselves capable of shaping the brain is slowly becoming accepted. Does the unconscious only get these facts from us, or does it have the same access to our sensorium that we have? You can do whatever you like with the us and the our and the we. I did. At some point the mind must grammaticize facts and convert them to narratives. The facts of the world do not for the most part come in narrative form. We have to do that.

So what are we saying here? That some unknown thinker sat up one night in his cave and said: Wow. One thing can be another thing. Yes. Of course that's what we are saying. Except that he didn't say it because there was no language for him to say it in. For the time being he had to settle for just thinking it. And when did this take place? Our influential persons claim to have no idea. Of course they don't think that it took place at all. But aside from that. One hundred thousand years ago? Half a million? Longer? Actually a hundred thousand would be a pretty good guess. It dates the earliest known graphics—found in the Blombos Cave in South Africa. These scratchings have everything to do with our chap waking up in his cave. For while it is fairly certain that art preceded language it probably didn't precede it by much. Some influential persons have actually claimed that language could be up to a million years old. They haven't explained what we have been doing with it all this time. What we do know—pretty much without question—is that once you have language everything else follows pretty quickly. The simple understanding that one thing can be another thing is at the root of all things of our doing. From using colored pebbles for the trading of goats to art and language and on to using symbolic marks to represent pieces of the world too small to see.

One hundred thousand years is pretty much an eyeblink. But two million years is not. This is, rather loosely, the length of time in which our unconscious has been organizing and directing our lives. And without language you will note. At least for all but that recent blink. How does it tell us where and when to scratch? We don't know. We just know that it's good at it. But the fact that the unconscious prefers avoiding verbal instructions pretty much altogether—even where they would appear to be quite useful—suggests rather strongly that it doesn't much like language and even that it doesn't trust it. And why is that? How about for the good and sufficient reason that it has been getting along quite well without it for a couple of million years?

Illustration by Don Kilpatrick III

Apart from its great antiquity the picture-story mode of presentation favored by the unconscious has the appeal of its simple utility. A picture can be recalled in its entirety whereas an essay cannot. Unless one is an Asperger's case. In which event memories, while correct, suffer from their own literalness. The log of knowledge or information contained in the brain of the average citizen is enormous. But the form in which it resides is largely unknown. You may have read a thousand books and be able to discuss any one of them without remembering a word of the text.

When you pause to reflect and say: "Let me see. How can I put this," your aim is to resurrect an idea from this pool of we-know-not-what and give it a linguistic form so that it can be expressed. It is the this that one wishes to put that is representative of this pool of knowledge whose form is so amorphous. If you explain this to someone and they say that they don't understand you may well seize your chin and think some more and come up with another way to "put" it. Or you may not. When the physicist Dirac was complained to by students that they didn't understand what he'd said Dirac would simply repeat it verbatim.

The picture-story lends itself to parable. To the tale whose meaning gives one pause. The unconscious is concerned with rules but these rules will require your cooperation. The unconscious wants to give guidance to your life in general but it doesn't care what toothpaste you use. And while the path which it suggests for you may be broad it doesn't include going over a cliff. We can see this in dreams. Those disturbing dreams which wake us from sleep are purely graphic. No one speaks. These are very old dreams and often troubling. Sometimes a friend can see their meaning where we cannot. The unconscious intends that they be difficult to unravel because it wants us to think about them. To remember them. It doesn't say that you can't ask for help. Parables of course often want to resolve themselves into the pictorial. When you first heard of Plato's cave you set about reconstructing it.

To repeat. The unconscious is a biological operative and language is not. Or not yet. You have to be careful about inviting Descartes to the table. Aside from inheritability probably the best guide as to whether a category is of our own devising is to ask if we see it in other creatures. The case for language is pretty clear. In the facility with which young children learn its complex and difficult rules we see the slow incorporation of the acquired.

I'd been thinking about the Kekulé problem off and on for a couple of years without making much progress. Then one morning after George Zweig and I had had one of our ten hour lunches I came down in the morning with the wastebasket from my bedroom and as I was emptying it into the kitchen trash I suddenly knew the answer. Or I knew that I knew the answer. It took me a minute or so to put it together. I reflected that while George and I had spent the first couple of hours at cognition and neuroscience we had not talked about Kekulé and the problem. But something in our conversation might very well have triggered our reflections—mine and those of the Night Shift—on this issue. The answer of course is simple once you know it. The unconscious is just not used to giving verbal instructions and is not happy doing so. Habits of two million years duration are hard to break. When later I told George what I'd come up with he mulled it over for a minute or so and then nodded and said: "That sounds about right." Which pleased me a good deal because George is very smart.

The unconscious seems to know a great deal. What does it know about itself? Does it know that it's going to die? What does it think about that? It appears to represent a gathering of talents rather than just one. It seems unlikely that the itch department is also in charge of math. Can it work on a number of problems at once? Does it only know what we tell it? Or—more plausibly—has it direct access to the outer world? Some of the dreams which it is at pains to assemble for us are no doubt deeply reflective and yet some are quite frivolous. And the fact that it appears to be less than insistent upon our remembering every dream suggests that sometimes it may be working on itself. And is it really so good at solving problems or is it just that it keeps its own counsel about the failures? How does it have this understanding which we might well envy? How might we make inquiries of it? Are you sure?

Robert Musil, in his Man Without Qualities, wrote that a Soul is "That which crawls away and hides whenever someone mentions algebra." According to his friend, Elias Canetti, Musil "felt at home and seemed natural among scientists," as distinct from most "people against whom his only defense was silence." These sentiments might be attached to Cormac McCarthy, hence the astonishment of many to his recent article in this magazine, "The Kekulé Problem," where his scientific imagination set out for a wide-ranging constitutional in the territories of linguistic provenance before returning to the sanctuary of our mountain community.

The region Cormac sought to explore was the intersection of organic and cultural evolution as revealed by that remarkable human instrument—combinatorial grammar. It is the enigma of the material brain—in almost all particulars indistinguishable from those of our nearest primate cousins—acquiring through the unknown mechanics of culture an ability that enables the gifts of poetry, prose, mathematics, and material and temporal transcendence.

My brother Dennis has brought me a fat stack of comments on "The Kekulé Problem" as published in Nautilus. A few of these I thought might be commented on in turn.

I haven't read the William Burroughs book that several people mentioned in which apparently language is compared to a virus. The only Burroughs book I've read is Naked Lunch. One reader seemed to know that that is just what I would say. Bloody McCarthy lies about everything. Naked Lunch was supposedly so named by Jack Kerouac. When Burroughs wanted to know what it meant, Kerouac said that it was that frozen moment when everybody sees what's on the end of the fork. Or so the story.

Sarah wrote with a number of questions that are my questions too.

I would not have thought that the distinction between useful and necessary—as regards language—would pose a difficulty for the average bear. The fact that language has found its way into the portfolio of but a single species should be all that one needs to know in order to properly categorize it. But apparently not. Some of the biolinguistic people do admit that the mission upon which they are bent is looking increasingly intractable. But the notion that language is a human invention—like parcheesi or marmalade—seems to be distressing to them. Resistance to reasonable ideas in science and elsewhere is often influenced more by what we know than what we don't. Entrenched ideas can be difficult to dislocate.

There is nothing in nature to suggest that a sound can be representative of an object.

One reader writes: "The vast majority of dreams and reveries don't involve major problems in the history of science." Baffling. Who would suggest such a thing?

The difference between the conscious and the unconscious is not a function of language and the paper suggests no such thing.

I would have thought that everyone knows why we have diverse languages. It's why we can't read Old English. It's why Finns and Hungarians can't converse.

Some were exercised about the paper's claim that the pictorial arts preceded language in our evolutionary history. How do we know that? Here's how we know that: The pictorial is a first order representation. A picture of a deer can be understood to be a deer without further explication. But the word deer represents another category. It is a second order representation. It cannot be understood on its own. The naming of things is a wholly artificial construct. There is nothing in nature to suggest that a sound can be representative of an object. Before you leap to your computer to argue please reflect on this. It is what takes place at the well in The Miracle Worker. The word water is water and it is so solely because we say it is.

Children raised in a feral state do not learn to speak. Do they learn to draw?

Some readers wrote about the subconscious. I don't know what that is.

What is true is that the world has a great deal to tell us while we have nothing at all to tell it.

The evolution of the eye has been frequently trotted out by the intelligent designers as an example of the designated. The teleological. Some very thoughtful evolutionary biologists think that there is something missing in the present Darwinian schema. Darwin himself is among them. The eye has been constructed—and several times over—in the absolute absence of any idea of such an apparatus—by simply deleting anything not conducive to eyehood in what we have as we go along. There are other ways of putting this. Some of them fairly brutal.

Moving along. Language being so useful it would most likely have showed up—if it were biological—a number of times in a number of species by now. Everything else did. You probably don't even need to be all that smart. Granted that even dull humans are fairly bright on the general scale of intelligence, still you have to dredge fairly deep to find people who can't talk. Feeding and dressing oneself may actually be more difficult. Until fairly recently it was at least conjectured that faculties such as vision might well have a single ancestor. The thinking was that anything so unique was unlikely to appear with any great frequency. Today our view is pretty much the opposite and we have hard evidence for the evolution of vision—our popular example—quite a number of times. What we have no evidence for is language evolving a number of times. The reason being of course that language is not a biological innovation. It is a human invention, and human inventions are magical in that they give life to what heretofore had no existence. Our good working ideas have the capacity to direct our lives in a manner indistinguishable from any other reality. There's more. The way in which the reality of the world becomes incorporated into our being, while poorly understood, is the salient fact of our existence. It is not true of course that we are born blank slates. What is true is that the world has a great deal to tell us while we have nothing at all to tell it.

A good part of the work that our linguists at SFI have been engaged in consists in tracing the histories of the world's languages. We believe that these languages share a common origin. We believe in an Ur language. We believe that language is based on an idea. The idea of representation. And although this seems a simple enough notion it apparently is not. And while there is every evidence that language is an idea there is no evidence that it was thought of more than once. Which of course is all that would be required.

They are the languages of this world but they are not—that we know of—languages of the universe.

No one seemed particularly interested in Helen Keller. Or the question of how her unconscious managed to communicate with her. It could neither speak to her nor draw pictures. Isn't that tantamount to saying that for all practical purposes she had no unconscious? Something missing in this scenario.

The universe in its billions of years remains a creation of total silence and total blackness. The incendiary explosions of the novae can be no more than optical constructions and no matter what your view of the nature of reality they can have no existence in the absence of an eye or something very like it. And the likelihood of such an instrument coming into being anywhere other than in the natural history of the earth seems more than vanishingly slim. The truth is that there is limited evidence for the existence of the visual. (What? What's he saying?) To what might it be compared? That which is seen is pretty much left to speak for itself. As is that which is said.

There is more to be considered concerning the structure of language. Our understanding of the world at large is formed by our experience of that world in a way which is difficult to exaggerate. This is Nietzsche on the subject, even if he doesn't go far enough:

However far human knowledge may extend or however objective that knowledge may appear to be it is nevertheless largely only our own life stories.

We've little reason to assume that the common structure of language—which all human languages share—is either the most effective or indeed the only form which language can take. The fact that all languages can be translated one into the other should tell us something about the common nature of their histories. The structure of these languages—their syntax and grammar and their general form—more than suggests that they have a single origin. But it further elicits the question as to whether or not this is a structure which enjoys an independent standing. Or whether other forms might be not only possible but even preferable. If intelligent beings from other parts of the universe should attempt to converse with us would their language be translatable? Would it share enough of our notions of how to go about describing the world for us to correlate it? Our languages in their form and in their structure are a single language. They are the languages of this world but they are not—that we know of—languages of the universe. We've no reason to believe that there is, or could be, such a thing. We might further consider that the form of language and its usage have at once influenced our view of reality as indeed has our experience of the world continued to influence our language. There is little evidence for selection in the shaping of language. A good part of what we experience appears in the form of frozen accident. As indeed does a good part of human experience in general.

El Apocalipsis de la Mente

Un Análisis Visionario del Gran Dragón Rojo de William Blake

1. Introducción y Contexto de la Obra

Entre 1803 y 1810, el polifacético artista y poeta inglés William Blake fue comisionado por su principal mecenas, Thomas Butts, para ejecutar una monumental serie de más de cien acuarelas destinadas a ilustrar diversos pasajes de la Biblia (1, 2). Este generoso patrocinio financiero resultó de vital importancia en una época en la que el establishment artístico británico marginaba a Blake, tildando sus visiones sistemáticamente de locura (3). Dentro de este vasto proyecto de ilustración bíblica, Blake ejecutó un ciclo muy enfocado de cuatro pinturas al agua inspiradas en el Libro del Apocalipsis, detallando los catastróficos conflictos celestiales atestiguados por San Juan en Patmos (1, 3). En el centro de este ciclo se yergue la sobrecogedora figura del Gran Dragón Rojo, un monstruo de múltiples cabezas que el texto bíblico identifica explícitamente con Satanás (1, 3).

Fiel a su temperamento herético, Blake no pretendía ilustrar de manera literal o sumisa las Escrituras. Su arte actuaba como una deconstrucción intelectual, un proceso mediante el cual los símbolos tradicionales eran transformados para encarnar una profunda rebelión contra la tiranía dogmática (1). El Dragón no representaba un mal exterior real o físico, sino una colosal fuerza intrapsíquica, un símbolo polivalente de opresión dogmática y límites perceptivos (1, 3). Para Blake, la imaginación humana era una fuerza divina pura, y el acto de crear arte era la mayor actividad espiritual posible. En este sentido, la tiranía del Dragón representa la supresión de esta libertad imaginativa por parte de los dogmas de las iglesias institucionales: una lucha cósmica cuyo verdadero escenario no es el cielo físico, sino el pecho humano (1, 3).

2. Ontología Espacial: 'Lo que está arriba está dentro'

El análisis formal de la composición visual de las acuarelas pone de manifiesto una alteración deliberada y metódica por parte de Blake respecto a la descripción literal del texto bíblico (1). En el capítulo 12 de Apocalipsis, se relata que el dragón se colocó delante de la mujer que estaba a punto de dar a luz, con el fin de devorar a su hijo recién nacido (1, 3). No obstante, en ambas acuarelas del Gran Dragón y la Mujer Vestida de Sol, Blake posiciona al dragón directamente arriba de la mujer, ocupando la mitad superior de la composición de manera amenazante, con las alas extendidas de borde a borde (1). La mujer yace horizontalmente a sus pies, de espaldas sobre una delgada luna creciente (1, 3).

Según el erudito Terence Allan Hoagwood, este arreglo responde al concepto de pathos formula (fórmula de patetismo) de Aby Warburg: configuraciones espaciales estandarizadas de la anatomía humana diseñadas para transmitir e invocar estados psicológicos y afectivos precisos (1). En el lenguaje visual de Blake, el visionario o perceptor se sitúa verticalmente en la parte inferior, contemplando con asombro una masa horizontal que flota sobre su cabeza y que representa su propio estado mental exteriorizado (1). Esta constante estructural, analizada inicialmente por Joseph Wicksteed en las ilustraciones de Blake para el Libro de Job, se resume en el axioma hermético: 'What is Above is Within' (Lo que está arriba está dentro) (1).

Bajo esta clave de lectura, el monstruoso Gran Dragón Rojo no representa un demonio externo físicamente real. Por el contrario, es la proyección psicorreligiosa de la propia mente de la mujer (1). El dragón encarna al 'acusador de los pecados': el miedo irracional, la culpa internalizada y el terror punitivo instituidos por la moral dogmática y el racionalismo opresivo (1, 3). Blake hace evidente este paralelismo al comparar al dragón con el Satán-Elohim de la ilustración 11 de Job ('Con sueños en mi cama'), quien flota horizontalmente sobre Job en la misma posición exacta, sosteniendo las tablas de piedra de la Ley Moral y envuelto en una gigantesca serpiente (1). El dragón es, por tanto, una exteriorización de la tiranía moral que paraliza y asfixia la imaginación del ser humano (1).

"Al posicionar la visión horizontalmente sobre la cabeza de la perceptora, Blake revela un proceso psicológico naturalista: el perceptor dota al objeto visionario de su propio carácter. El acto de percepción constituye el objeto mismo... El dragón es el espectro de la razón moralizante que intenta devorar el nacimiento de la libertad interna." — Terence Allan Hoagwood, 1983

3. La Deconstrucción Gnóstica y el Demiurgo

La singular reinterpretación que Blake realiza de las Escrituras entronca de forma asombrosa con las antiguas doctrinas de la herejía gnóstica del siglo II (2). El historiador E.P. Thompson enfatiza que el dualismo gnóstico, que concebía a un segundo Dios o demiurgo como creador del mundo de la materia, servía a Blake como un andamio conceptual ideal para su feroz oposición a la teología ortodoxa (2). En una célebre conversación con Henry Crabb Robinson en 1825, Blake declaró explícitamente que 'la Naturaleza es la obra del Diablo' (2). Al citar Robinson el Génesis para defender la creación divina del mundo material, Blake rebatió de inmediato que el Dios del Génesis no era Jehová, sino los 'Elohim', deidades inferiores identificadas con la materia y la ley (2).

En la cosmología mitológica de Blake, este creador ciego de la materia recibe el nombre de Urizen (juego de palabras con 'Your Reason' o 'Horizonte'), a quien ridiculizaba poéticamente como 'Nobodaddy' (1, 3). Urizen es el demiurgo tiránico que escribe las leyes opresivas en su 'Libro de Bronce' e impone 'Un comando, un Dios, una Ley' (3). El Gran Dragón Rojo del Apocalipsis es el brazo ejecutor de este demiurgo; representa la fuerza de coerción de la Ley Moral en contraposición directa con el Evangelio de Jesús, el cual, según Blake, consiste estrictamente en el Perdón de los Pecados y carece por completo de preceptos morales restrictivos (2). Adorar al Dios ortodoxo de la ley y el castigo es, para Blake, adorar a Satán mismo bajo el nombre de Dios (2).

4. Lecturas Psicoanalíticas: el Ello y la Sombra

Las implicaciones del ciclo del Gran Dragón Rojo rebasan el ámbito teológico y anticipan con una intuición asombrosa los fundamentos del psicoanálisis moderno de Sigmund Freud y Carl Gustav Jung (1, 3). Para Blake, el mundo exterior era una proyección de la psique humana ('Todas las deidades residen en el pecho humano' [3]); sus acuarelas y poemas proféticos constituyen detallados mapas de la mente inconsciente humana y de sus fragmentaciones (2).

La Dimensión Freudiana (El 'Ello' o Id): Desde una perspectiva psicoanalítica clásica, el Gran Dragón Rojo representa de forma ejemplar la irrupción indómita del Ello (Id): la sede primitiva de los instintos sexuales, los impulsos destructivos y las energías reprimidas de la libido que escapan al control de la conciencia (3). El cuerpo del dragón en la acuarela del Brooklyn Museum se destaca por una musculatura atlética hipertrofiada, erguido con las piernas abiertas de espaldas al espectador (1). Su rostro oculto sugiere que encarna lo desconocido, los temores innombrables, las ansiedades y las pulsiones reprimidas hechas carne (3). Al yacer la mujer embarazada vulnerable a sus pies, se visualiza el dominio que las fuerzas instintivas del Ello pretenden imponer sobre el Yo consciente (Ego) y su vulnerabilidad constitutiva (1, 3).

La Dimensión Junguiana (La Sombra y el Sueño del Alma): Para Carl Gustav Jung, quien sentía una profunda atracción por el sistema mitológico blakeano como reflejo del proceso de individuación, el Dragón representa el arquetipo de la Sombra: el lado oscuro del propio Yo que debe ser reconocido e integrado para evitar la disociación destructiva de la personalidad (3). En la versión de la National Gallery de Washington, los brazos extendidos de la mujer vestida de sol y las alas batientes del dragón se curvan en una simetría perfecta, casi especular (3, 212). Esta correspondencia geométrica demuestra que ambos personajes no son fuerzas totalmente dispares o enemigas externas, sino estados coexistentes del sueño de una sola alma (10, 32). La mujer y el dragón representan la dualidad arquetípica de la mente: luz y oscuridad, consciente e inconsciente. La fuerza del color rojo del dragón alude al impulso creador de la libido, una energía que, si se reprime moralmente de forma absoluta en lugar de ser integrada mediante el perdón y la moderación, puede llegar a devorar y consumir la psique entera del sujeto (3).

"Psicológicamente hablando, el Dragón Rojo es un agente de caos creador y de recuerdos pesados y solidificados que niegan nuestro renacimiento interno. La tríada de personajes ilustra un conflicto íntimo: el dragón del pasado ataca a la virgen (el presente), quien puja dolorosamente por dar a luz al niño que representa el futuro y el nuevo Ser (Self)." — Credo Quia Absurdum, 2022

5. Simbología Polivalente del Reptil

La serpiente y el dragón son símbolos recurrentes en la producción artística y literaria de Blake, y sus significados son inherentemente fluidos y polivalentes (2). El renombrado mitógrafo S. Foster Damon compiló en su diccionario de símbolos de Blake la complejidad polifacética del reptil, que lejos de limitarse a una encarnación del mal absoluto, fluctúa según el contexto de la obra (2):

  • El Culto a la Naturaleza: Las espirales infinitas de la serpiente representan los ciclos monótonos y repetitivos del mundo de la materia (Urizenic Ulro), en los cuales el hombre se encuentra encadenado (2).
  • La Hipocresía Sacerdotal y el Dogma: El reptil encarna la represión y la moralidad exterior de la iglesia institucionalizada. Blake identifica la 'espada flamígera' que custodia el Edén con la Ley de Moisés y la serpiente sacerdotal (2).
  • La Energía Vital Reprimida: El color rojo del dragón alude a la energía corpórea. Cuando la razón usurpa el trono de la imaginación, la energía se convierte en un dragón hirviente de los abismos (2).

Para Blake, el desprendimiento de esta envoltura material se lograba mediante la crucifixión mística (2). En sus obras gráficas como 'Miguel profetizando la Crucifixión' o en las ilustraciones de 'The Grave', la cabeza de la serpiente yace clavada bajo los pies de Cristo en la cruz (2, 359). Al clavar el reptil a la madera, Cristo no derrota a un enemigo externo, sino que se desprende de la 'envoltura mortal' (mortal coil) de la materia pecaminosa y la ley restrictiva de Urizen, restaurando al ser humano en su forma divina espiritual infinita (2).

6. Antropología y Mitología Comparada

Durante el final del siglo XVIII y principios del XIX, la cultura intelectual británica se vio inundada de textos de mitografía comparada que intentaban unificar el folclore pagano de todo el mundo con la historia de las Escrituras hebreas (2, 3). Blake estuvo expuesto directamente a esta corriente. Durante su aprendizaje con el grabador James Basire, colaboró en las ilustraciones para el influyente compendio de Jacob Bryant, A New System; or, An analysis of ancient mythology (1774), el cual afirmaba que todos los mitos provenían de las tribus sobrevivientes al diluvio de Noé (3). La imaginación sincretista de Blake se apropió de estas tesis arqueológicas y antropológicas de su tiempo (2).

De esta asimilación brota la asombrosa identificación blakeana entre el misticismo de los druidas celtas y los patriarcas bíblicos (2). En su obra épica tardía Jerusalén, Blake proclama que 'Toda la historia comienza y termina en las costas rocosas del antiguo druida de Albión', sosteniendo que Abraham, Noé y Sem eran druidas y que los monumentos megalíticos de Stonehenge y Avebury eran templos patriarcales primigenios (2, 292). Sin embargo, la resolución que Blake hace del origen del mito difiere radicalmente de la sociología racionalista de su contemporáneo Giambattista Vico (3). Mientras que Vico argumentaba que los mitos eran respuestas producidas socialmente ante los fenómenos de la naturaleza, Blake subvertía el materialismo ilustrado al insistir en que las deidades no provenían del exterior físico: eran personificaciones poéticas creadas por el Genio Poético o la imaginación del ser humano (3). El error fatal de la humanidad residió en separar estas deidades del sujeto perceptor y proyectarlas al cielo exterior, permitiendo que el clero construyera un sistema de opresión dogmática (3). En consecuencia, el Gran Dragón Rojo no habita en una dimensión externa de condenación mística; su fuego abrasador y su furia opresora residen de manera exclusiva dentro del pecho y el corazón humano (3, 237).

"Los poetas antiguos animaron todos los objetos sensibles con Dioses o Genios, nombrándolos con las propiedades de bosques, ríos y montañas... Hasta que se formó un sistema que algunos aprovecharon para esclavizar al vulgo, intentando abstraer a las deidades mentales de sus objetos originales: así comenzó el Sacerdocio, pronunciando que los Dioses habían ordenado tales cosas. Así olvidaron los hombres que todos los dioses residen en el pecho humano." — William Blake, El Matrimonio del Cielo y el Infierno, Placa 11

A Psychologically Rich Life:
Beyond Happiness and Meaning

Shigehiro Oishi & Erin C. Westgate

Psychological Review © 2021 American Psychological Association

Psychological science has typically conceptualized a good life in terms of either hedonic or eudaimonic well-being. We propose that psychological richness is another, neglected aspect of what people consider a good life. Unlike happy or meaningful lives, psychologically rich lives are best characterized by a variety of interesting and perspective-changing experiences. We present empirical evidence that happiness, meaning, and psychological richness are related but distinct and desirable aspects of a good life, with unique causes and correlates. In doing so, we show that a nontrivial number of people around the world report they would choose a psychologically rich life at the expense of a happy or meaningful life, and that approximately a third say that undoing their life’s biggest regret would have made their lives psychologically richer. Furthermore, we propose that the predictors of a psychologically rich life are different from those of a happy life or a meaningful life, and report evidence suggesting that people leading psychologically rich lives tend to be more curious, think more holistically, and lean more politically liberal. Together, this work moves us beyond the dichotomy of hedonic versus eudaimonic well-being, and lays the foundation for the study of psychological richness as another dimension of a good life.

Keywords: happiness, meaning in life, psychological richness

Aristotle opens the Nicomachean Ethics by considering various candidates for the good life: a life of pleasure, a life of honor, a life of wealth or health or eminence. Or, as Aristotle himself argues persuasively, a life led in accordance with virtue and excellence (i.e., areté, Thomson, 1955). Of these, only two have survived the test of time: the pleasurable life (despite Aristotle’s admonitions) and the eudaimonic life (i.e., Aristotle’s preferred contender). In the millennia since Greek philosophers debated the question, Aristotle’s conception of "the good life" has proliferated into many fine-tuned theories of well-being (see Vittersø, 2016, for a comprehensive review). However, the strong dichotomy between these two prevailing models—hedonic versus eudaimonic well-being—continues to dominate psychological research on the good life. Yet this dichotomy limits psychology by overlooking many lives that do not fit neatly within it. In this article, we move beyond the eudaimonic–hedonic divide to suggest a third contender for a good life: a psychologically rich life—or a life characterized by a variety of interesting and perspective-changing experiences. Together with happiness and meaning, we suggest that psychological richness constitutes an element of a life well lived.

We define a good life as a life well lived from the perspective of the person living it, as opposed to a purely objectivist perspective (i.e., whether most people would recognize it as such; see Sumner, 1996, for a philosophically grounded discussion on subjective versus objective theories of a good life). Psychologists have advanced many broad theories of subjective well-being (see Diener et al., 2018, for review), ranging from dispositional and genetic (Lykken & Tellegen, 1996) to motivational and need-fulfillment accounts (Emmons, 1986; Higgins, 2011; Ryan & Deci, 2001; Ryff, 1989). These existing theories address many fundamental questions, from how to explain stable individual differences in well-being, and the temporal processes (e.g., adaptation) underlying them, to the types of life events and conditions facilitating it (Luhmann et al., 2012; Wilson & Gilbert, 2008). Many models focus on social cognition, from the role of attention and appraisal in shaping well-being (Kurtz, 2008; O’Brien & Ellsworth, 2012) to the judgments people draw about their own and others’ happiness (Gilbert, 2006; Kahneman, 1999; Robinson & Clore, 2002; Schwarz & Strack, 1999). Other theories have focused specifically on the benefits of positive emotion (Fredrickson, 2001) and increasing happiness (Dunn et al., 2014; Lyubomirsky, 2008; Van Boven & Gilovich, 2003). However, none of these theories address the central question of what it means to live a good life, or the type of ideal life people imagine for themselves. In other words, because they do not ask what kind of life people want to live, we do not know if current concepts of happiness and meaning comprehensively cover the possibilities.

To preview, we argue that a psychologically rich life is one such life that people desire, which is unaccounted for by current conceptualizations of well-being. A psychologically rich life, we will show, is related to (but distinct from) a happy or a meaningful life, with distinct causes and consequences (see Besser & Oishi, 2020, for a philosophical overview). To be clear, we do not claim that psychological richness is wholly independent of happiness or meaning; as empirical evidence shows, the three often correlate positively with one another. Rather, we argue that it is beneficial to treat them as three interrelated but distinct aspects of a good life. Nor do we claim that a psychologically rich life is better or preferable to a happy or a meaningful life. Rather, our aim is simply to open up the conceptual space; as part of this, we do not claim there can be only three dimensions to a good life. Our goal here is only to introduce a possible third dimension; there could in fact be many more such, a possibility we discuss in detail later.

This approach argues that psychological richness, happiness, and meaning constitute three elements of a subjectively well-lived life (see Table 1). As such, our model provides a more comprehensive account than the extant dichotomous model governing much of well-being research: namely, the distinction between hedonic and eudaimonic well-being. We review this work below, before drawing on classic work in literature and philosophy to make the case for why it is insufficient to account for the full range of human experience. Finally, we introduce empirical evidence for psychological richness as a dimension of the good life distinct from happiness and meaning.

The Existing Dichotomy of a Good Life: Hedonic Versus Eudaimonic Well-Being

Previous work has conceptualized well-being in terms of either hedonic (e.g., Diener et al., 2018; Kahneman, 1999) or eudaimonic well-being (e.g., Ryan & Deci, 2001; Ryff, 1989; Waterman, 1993). Hedonic well-being focuses on feelings of positive affect and life satisfaction. Eudaimonic well-being focuses on a sense of optimal functioning and realizing potential (Vittersø, 2016). Although this dichotomy has moved the empirical study of well-being forward over the past three decades (see Besser-Jones, 2014; Haybron, 2013; Ryan & Deci, 2001; Tiberius, 2008 for review), it has also diverted research attention away from other forms of well-being. Below, we review these two prevailing conceptualizations—the happy life and the meaningful life—and introduce a third currently missing possibility: the psychologically rich life.

A Happy Life

We define a happy life as one characterized by high life satisfaction and a high positive-to-negative affect ratio (i.e., frequent positive affect paired with relatively infrequent negative affect, such as anger or sadness). Several decades of research shows that a happy life (i.e., hedonic well-being) is characterized by pleasantness, comfort, safety, security, and stability (Diener et al., 2018; see Table 1).

People leading happy lives tend to be blessed with material and relational wealth. For instance, income is modestly but consistently associated with greater happiness and life satisfaction (e.g., Kahneman & Deaton, 2010; Killingsworth, 2021). People with higher socioeconomic status (SES) report greater life satisfaction (e.g., Anderson et al., 2012), as do those with greater job security (Helbling & Kanji, 2018). In particular, having cash on hand (or in savings), rather than money in stocks and investments, predicts perceived financial security, which in turn predicts higher life satisfaction (Ruberton et al., 2016). Finally, marital satisfaction consistently predicts happiness (e.g., Carr et al., 2014), as does regular interaction with friends and neighbors (e.g., Helliwell & Putnam, 2004). In short, a happy life is attained via stable economic and interpersonal conditions.

However, even when objective conditions are not conducive to happiness, people are sometimes able to reconstrue them to be so (Taylor & Brown, 1988). For instance, optimism protectively buffers against cancer, bereavement, and even war (Carver et al., 2010). Pursuing gain, as opposed to trying to prevent loss, is associated with happiness (Elliot & Thrash, 2002; Higgins et al., 2014), as is gratitude (Emmons & McCullough, 2003). Whereas unhappy people desire the unattainable, happy people come to terms with less-than-perfect options (Lyubomirsky & Ross, 1999). Happy people are not bothered when others do better than them, whereas unhappy people are (Lyubomirsky & Ross, 1997). As William James (1950) famously stated, "To give up pretensions is as blessed a relief as to get them gratified" (p. 311). Indeed, the smaller the gap between aspirations and realities, the higher people’s satisfaction (Campbell et al., 1976). Accordingly, satisficing, as opposed to maximizing, is associated with happiness (Schwartz et al., 2002). Prolonging pleasurable experiences (via savoring) offers another such strategy (Bryant & Veroff, 2007; Kurtz, 2008).

In short, a happy life is characterized by stable interpersonal, financial, and political environments (Diener et al., 2018), and is facilitated by certain mindsets, including optimism, gratitude, savoring, satisficing, and approach orientations. Not surprisingly, most people want a happy life; Voltaire reportedly claimed that happiness is the only thing worth living for (Tatarkiewicz, 1976). Empirical studies agree; 69% of respondents across 42 countries rated happiness as "extremely important" (7 on a 7-point scale), even more so than health and money (Diener, 2000). However, a happy life does not fully capture what it means to live a good life. Happy people tend to be prosocial and make numerous contributions to society via, for instance, donating and volunteering (Dunn et al., 2014; Lyubomirsky et al., 2005). Yet, happiness is sometimes associated with system justification, resistance to change, and insensitivity to inequality and social injustice (Butz et al., 2017; Napier & Jost, 2008; see, however, Kushlev et al., 2020). In this sense, a happy life can be a complacent life (Ryff, 1989; Ryff & Singer, 1998). We contrast this, below, with a meaningful life.

A Meaningful Life

We define a meaningful life as the subjective self-appraisal that one’s life and experiences have meaning. There is a great deal of conceptual diversity in the literature on how to define such meaning (see Martela & Steger, 2016; Steger, 2009). We highlight three elements common to many existing definitions: (1) significance, or the subjective sense that one’s life matters (George & Park, 2014), (2) coherence, the extent to which life roles and experiences fit together into a coherent whole (Baumeister, 1991; Heine et al., 2006), and (3) purpose, the perception that one’s life has direction and contributes to something greater than the self (Recker et al., 1987). As such, individuals leading meaningful lives tend to be actively engaged and committed to societally important causes (Wolf, 1997). Indeed, Aristotle defined the eudaimonic person as "one who is active in accordance with complete virtue … not for some unspecified period but throughout a complete life" (Book I, I100b27–I101a20; italics added). Good deeds must be repeated to build lasting contributions; for instance, routines are positively associated with meaning in life (Heintzelman & King, 2019), perhaps because they assist in doing so.

We should note that meaning in life, on its own, does not fully reflect all elements of eudaimonic well-being. For instance, some theorists emphasize self-realization, rather than meaning, as its defining characteristic (Vittersø, 2016). Others, such as Waterman (1993), focus on personal expressiveness, which is closely related to authenticity (Schlegel et al., 2009). Likewise, self-determination theorists consider intrinsic motivation (and accompanying satisfaction of autonomy, competence, and relatedness needs) to be critical (Ryan & Deci, 2001; Ryan et al., 2008). Finally, Ryff (1989) conceptualizes eudaimonia as the confluence of all the above: self-acceptance, autonomy, environmental mastery, positive relationships with others, purpose, and personal growth. Despite this conceptual diversity, measures of eudaimonic well-being all correlate highly with each other. For instance, autonomy, competence, and relatedness all correlate strongly with meaning in life (rs = .55–.71 in Martela et al., 2018). Likewise, purpose in life correlates strongly with Ryff’s overall psychological well-being (r = .75, Garcia-Alandete, 2015), as does Waterman et al.’s (2010) eudaimonic well-being (r = .63). In short, people who engage in activities that foster authenticity and satisfy intrinsic motivations (such as autonomy and competence), who practice self-acceptance, and who pursue positive relationships and personal growth also tend to lead meaningful lives.

Beyond the Dichotomy: Examples From Literature and Philosophy

Although a happy life and a meaningful life represent ideals for many people (Kashdan et al., 2008; King & Napa, 1998), they overlook at least one important dimension of a good life and, in doing so, struggle to account for the full range of human experiences. In Nietzsche and Kaufmann (1978) Thus Spoke Zarathustra, the title character casts happiness and meaning as banal, observing that others "would laud and lure me into a small virtue; they would persuade my foot to the ticktock of a small happiness. I walk among this people … they have become smaller, and they are becoming smaller and smaller; but this is due to their doctrine of happiness and virtue" (pp. 168–169). Instead of "small" happiness and meaning, Zarathustra advocated the life of the wanderer: "Peak and abyss … are now joined together. You are going your way to greatness … your ultimate danger has become your ultimate refuge" (p. 152). Taking Zarathustra’s advice to heart, we explore this third ideal, and an alternative to happiness and meaning: the psychologically rich life. A psychologically rich life is best characterized by a variety of interesting and perspective-changing experiences. A significant reason neither a happy life nor a meaningful life captures the full range of human motivation is that both happy and meaningful lives can be monotonous and repetitive. In Either/Or, Kierkegaard (2004) proposes the "aesthetic" life as an antithesis to the "ethical" life. The ethical life, he warns, can be monotonous, dreadful, and boring, and lauds the value of the arbitrary as an antidote: "One should therefore always keep an eye open for the accidental, always be expeditus if anything should offer" (p. 240). According to Kierkegaard, a married person with a secure, well-respected job and children may have a happy and (in many respects) meaningful life, but not necessarily a life rich in diverse perspective-changing experiences. Although most people choose such a conventional, secure, social, and well-respected life, others like James Joyce’s (2003) alter ego Stephen Dedalus choose the esthetic wanderer’s life instead—unconventional, unstable, and uncompromising.

Consider another example: The character Goldmund in Hermann Hesse’s (1984) Narcissus and Goldmund. Both Narcissus and Goldmund are students in a monastic cloister; Narcissus stays in the cloister and devotes himself to a life of orderly contemplation and virtue, leading an Aristotelian eudaimonic (or "ethical") life. In contrast, Goldmund flees to live as a vagabond. Toward the end of the novel, Narcissus reflects upon his own life compared to Goldmund’s:

"Seen from the point of view of the cloister, his own [Narcissus’] life was better, righter, steadier, more orderly, more exemplary … It was much purer, much better than the life of an artist, vagrant, and seducer of women. But, seen from above, with God’s eyes—was this exemplary life of order and discipline … any better than Goldmund’s life?" (p. 297).

Narcissus wonders whether the dramatic life Goldmund led was not also a good life. We think many would say it was (and report evidence to this effect later). Goldmund’s life is a psychologically rich one, despite its lack of material goods or comforts.

How do we account for such lives within empirical psychology? We argue the current dichotomy between eudaimonic and hedonic well-being overlooks a third possibility: the psychologically rich life. To remedy this oversight, we introduce the concept of psychological richness and hypothesize that the features, causes, and consequences of a happy life, a meaningful life, and a psychologically rich life systematically differ, as laid out in Table 1.

First, we theorize that a psychologically rich life is characterized by variety, interestingness, and perspective change; in contrast, a happy life is characterized by comfort, joy, and stability, and a meaningful life by purpose, significance, and coherence. Thus, we predict that different resources will differentially facilitate happiness, meaning, and psychological richness. For instance, we hypothesize that curiosity, spontaneity, and energy will facilitate a psychologically rich life; strong moral principles and religiosity will facilitate a meaningful life; and stable relationships, time, money, and positive mindsets will facilitate a happy life. Finally, we predict that these three aspects of a good life ultimately contribute to different life outcomes—whereas happiness leads to personal satisfaction, and meaning to societal contribution, psychological richness leads to wisdom.

To be clear, although some people’s lives may be higher on one dimension than others, happiness, meaning, and richness represent three components or dimensions of the good life, rather than independent types of life, strictly speaking. Presumably, some lucky people may lead a life high in all three dimensions (e.g., a happy, meaningful, and psychologically rich life), while others may be high in just two dimensions (e.g., a life that is meaningful and happy, but not psychologically rich), and others high in only one dimension (or none). We elaborate on each of these predictions below before reporting empirical support for our claims in the second half of the article.

The Causes and Facilitators of a Psychologically Rich Life

We predict that certain situational and individual characteristics are more likely to yield psychologically rich experiences, which in turn make up a psychologically rich life.

Situational Characteristics

Novelty, complexity, and changes in perspective should give rise to psychologically rich experiences, which in turn form the building blocks of a psychologically rich life. As such, certain situations may be more likely to facilitate psychological richness than others. We generated a preliminary list of such features via qualitative data outlining the contours of a psychologically rich experience. In two focus groups (12 undergraduates and 10 graduate student/postdoctoral researchers, respectively), members described a happy experience, a personally meaningful experience, and a psychologically rich experience. These groups converged on a description of psychologically rich experiences as those involving novelty, variety/complexity, and a change in perspective.

For instance, a 19-year-old undergraduate from a wealthy suburb of Washington D.C. described attending a professional wrestling event as a particularly rich experience. Her first time at such an event, she did not know much about professional wrestling; she went in with stereotypic expectations of fake violence and cheesy drama. To her surprise, she discovered professional wrestlers to be inspiring role models for children because, unbeknownst to her, the World Wrestling Entertainment (WWE) is heavily devoted to children’s charities. She laughed, cheered, felt outraged and pained, and ultimately found herself deeply moved by the experience. She returned home with a changed perspective.

Unexpectedness (e.g., WWE’s charitable work), novelty (e.g., first time at a wrestling event), complexity (e.g., not simple choreographed violence/drama), and a change in perspective (e.g., "Now I see why so many kids adore WWE wrestlers") made this experience much richer than a typical outing. In particular, this last feature—a personal change in perspective—appears integral to the experience of richness. Emotion theorists have argued that cognitive restructuring intensifies emotional experiences (Clore, 2011); the greater the cognitive distance between one’s original and revised mental representations, the more intense the emotion. This kind of cognitive restructuring—abandoning old mental representations in response to new experiences—is very like the perspective change required of psychologically rich experiences. This is particularly clear when we compare another student’s experience which, although novel and complex, was not rich. This second focus group member noticed a male student in her apartment complex’s lounge studying shirtless. He was not conventionally handsome, and she began to wonder why he was shirtless in a public space, as the lounge was not particularly warm. Although this experience was novel, certainly surprising, and somewhat complex (i.e., his behavior was unusual and mystifying), it did not change her perspective in any way. Likewise, she did not experience diverse emotions; and thus, we theorize that her experience was not a psychologically rich one.

In short, a subjective sense of surprise, novelty, complexity, and perspective change seem critical to the experience of psychological richness. This is consistent with work on another construct quite similar to richness: interest. Berlyne (1949) concluded in his historical survey that interest was integral to the "everyday discussion of human behaviour" (p. 184), and later (1960) theorized that novelty, complexity, conflict, and uncertainty were key determinants of both interest and curiosity. Conflict occurs when a stimulus evokes multiple incompatible responses. For instance, new coworkers provoke psychological conflict, because they evoke both approach and avoidant responses (e.g., "Should I talk to them, or avoid them?"). Finally, Berlyne (1971, 1974) distinguished between judgments of interest and pleasure, illustrating that complexity increases pleasure, but only up to a point; in contrast, interest continues to increase in response to even very high levels of complexity. Such interest is thought to support basic behavioral and cognitive processes necessary for survival (e.g., Izard, 1991; Tomkins, 1962/2008). Tomkins (1962/2008) theorized that interest–excitement is a core feature of the human motivation that "enables the individual sustained attention to complex objects" (p. 186). That is, he argues that perception, thinking, and behavior all require sustained interest, without which human beings would not thrive, and that such interest is activated by change and novelty (Izard, 1991).

More recently, Silvia and colleagues expanded existing work by applying appraisal theories to interest (e.g., Silvia, 2005, 2008; Turner & Silvia, 2006; see also Harackiewicz et al., 2016; Litman, 2004; Sansone et al., 1992). Interest, according to Silvia, occurs when a person feels they have the capacity to make sense of novel and/or complex stimuli. For instance, paintings, poems, and polygons high in novelty and complexity (e.g., abstract art) are rated as more interesting (but less enjoyable) than simpler or familiar stimuli, but only if people have the capacity to understand them. Thus, things that are enjoyable are not necessarily interesting, and things that are interesting are not necessarily enjoyable. Likewise, we suggest that happy experiences are not necessarily interesting or psychologically rich, and conversely that psychologically rich experiences are not necessarily pleasant.

In short, prior work on how novelty and complexity foster interest laid the foundation for our current concept of psychological richness. One major difference is that previous work focuses primarily on interesting stimuli or short-term affective states (i.e., feeling interested). We are interested in not just momentary elicitors of psychological richness, or even short-term subjectively rich experiences, but rather the long-term outcomes of many such accumulated rich experiences. Thus, we argue that a psychologically rich life consists of interesting experiences in which novelty and/or complexity are accompanied by profound changes in perspective.

Person Characteristics

Psychological richness is a function of the person, as well as the situation, for instance, curious people open to experience who should be more likely to lead complex and psychologically rich lives. Thus, we predict that openness to experience, characterized by "vivid fantasy, artistic sensitivity, depth of feeling, behavioral flexibility, intellectual curiosity, and unconventional attitudes" (McCrae, 1996, p. 323) should foster psychological richness. Curiosity in particular, we predict, should lead people to explore a broad range of interests (Kashdan et al., 2018), which may in turn trigger perspective shifts of the sort theorized to produce psychological richness. Notably, people who experience intense emotions also tend to lead more complex lives (Larsen & Diener, 1987). For instance, their social networks tend to be broader, shallower, and more complex. That is, instead of hanging out with the same friends or working on goals in a single life domain, affectively intense people hang out with wider groups of individuals and work on diverse goals—and view these diverse life events and goals as personally important (Schimmack & Diener, 1997).

To be clear, we do not suggest that simply experiencing intense emotions and events is itself sufficient for a psychologically rich life. Rather, such events create a need for accommodation—how people make sense of and integrate these events is critical. For instance, people explain similar life stories in many different ways (Bauer et al., 2008; McAdams, 2001; McLean et al., 2007). We suggest narrative complexity and accommodative processing are two important themes in such stories. Narrative complexity is the degree to which a story contains multiple roles and perspectives, motivations, complex emotional experiences, and contradictory aspects of the self. Higher scores indicate "successful or harmonious integration of differentiated perspectives, motivations, states, or self-aspects" (McAdams et al., 2004, p. 768), and are associated with openness to experience (r = .52, p < .001) and extraversion (r = .24, p < .01). Thus, we expect that people leading psychologically rich lives will tend to have complex life narratives. Accommodative processing, like cognitive restructuring, is the process by which people revise their "ordinary frame of reference to make sense of and integrate" extraordinary events (Perlin & Li, 2020, p. 2), and is thought, ultimately, to result in psychological maturity and wisdom (e.g., Helson & Roberts, 1994; King et al., 2000).

In sum, we hypothesize that openness to experience, curiosity, and affect intensity serve as fuel for the psychologically rich life. Because curiosity and openness encourage individuals to pursue and appreciate novel, complex, and potentially perspective-changing experiences, they constitute dispositional factors that facilitate the psychologically rich life.

The Consequences of a Psychologically Rich Life

Just as psychologically rich lives have unique facilitating causes, so too do they have distinct consequences. We hypothesize that although a lifetime of happiness gives rise to personal satisfaction, and a lifetime of a meaningful life to societal contribution, a lifetime of psychologically rich experiences should give rise to wisdom. Wisdom is a complex concept. Indeed, as Sternberg (1990) stated: "To understand wisdom fully and correctly probably requires more wisdom than any of us have" (p. 3).

One popular psychological definition of wisdom is factual and procedural knowledge about the fundamental pragmatics of life, which involve insight into life matters and good judgment and advice about difficult problems (Baltes & Staudinger, 2000; Grossmann, 2017). Critically, wisdom goes beyond conventional knowledge, and is accompanied by awareness that one’s insight is neither definitive nor universal. Wisdom is thus characterized by breadth and depth of knowledge along with humble and relativist mindsets. Such knowledge and mindsets are often "hard won from engagement with life, and therefore … not exclusively cognitive but involv[e] a broader experience" (McKee & Barber, 1999, p.151). Individuals who have led a psychologically rich life presumably have done so via broad life experiences, in which they have encountered (and entertained) a variety of perspectives, and recognized life’s complexity. Accordingly, a psychologically rich life should be associated with holistic thinking styles (Choi et al., 2007) and attributional complexity (Fletcher et al., 1986), which should in turn should give rise to wisdom. Thus, on their deathbed, a person who has led a happy life might say, "I had fun!" A person who has led a meaningful life might say, "I made a difference!" And a person who has led a psychologically rich life might say, "What a journey!"

Novel Predictions

In addition to introducing psychological richness, and arguing for its inclusion as an aspect of a good life, our model generates several novel predictions, which we outline in detail below.

  • Prediction #1: Psychological richness is distinct from happiness and meaning. We present empirical evidence that a happy life, a meaningful life, and a psychologically rich life are distinct (but related) constructs.
  • Prediction #2: Psychologically rich lives stem from distinct causes. We predict that psychologically rich lives stem from a variety of interesting and perspective-changing experiences, and are facilitated by curiosity, time, and energy. We present evidence that the experiences and personality traits that characterize people with psychologically rich lives are distinct from those that characterize meaningful or happy lives.
  • Prediction #3: Psychologically rich lives predict distinct outcomes. We theorize that psychological richness predicts exploration and wisdom. For example, psychological richness should predict the degree to which college students take challenging courses and endorse progressive political attitudes. We present preliminary evidence for the unique predictive value of psychologically rich lives, relative to meaningful or happy lives.
  • Prediction #4: The psychologically rich life is a good life. We present evidence that people desire psychological richness and consider it an aspect of a good life.

Empirical Evidence for the Three Dimensions of a Good Life

In the first half of the article, we reviewed examples from literature, psychology, and philosophy suggesting the existence of a third dimension of a good life: psychological richness. As a first step toward a programmatic investigation, we next present empirical evidence for the key predictions outlined above, namely, that a psychologically rich life is distinct from a happy or meaningful life, according to both self-report and informants and third-party observers (e.g., obituary studies). We then examine the personality and situational characteristics of people leading psychologically rich lives, and show that they are distinct from those leading meaningful or happy lives. Finally, we present evidence that psychological richness uniquely predicts political attitudes, emotional intensity, and challenge-seeking, and that it is a desirable and important part of people’s self-described concept of a good life.

Psychological Richness Is Distinct From Meaning and Happiness (Prediction #1)

We begin by reviewing evidence that meaningful and happy lives are distinct and can be additionally distinguished from psychologically rich lives.

Psychometric Evidence

We asked a large sample of college students (n = 583) to report the extent to which their lives possessed the following characteristics: happy ["happy","enjoyable","comfortable","unstable" (reversed), "sad" (reversed)], meaningful ["meaningful","fulfilling","purposeful","meaningless" (reversed), "disorganized" (reversed)], and psychologically rich ["interesting","dramatic","psychologically rich","uneventful" (reversed), "monotonous" (reversed)]. A 3-factor model, in which richness, happiness, and meaning represented three separate factors, was the best fit, and fitted reasonably well. In other words, richness, happiness, and meaning represented three distinct factors (see Table 2).

We formally tested this model against competing alternatives in a series of confirmatory factor analyses where psychological richness was not distinct from happiness or meaning. First, we tested a general 1-factor model, in which richness, happiness, and meaning formed interchangeable components of a "good life." This model fitted poorly. Next, we tested a 2-factor model, comparing a psychologically rich life to the combination of a happy life and a meaningful life (i.e., equating happiness and meaning). This 2-factor model did not fit well either—nor did a different 2-factor model, combining a psychologically rich life and a happy life (i.e., equating happiness and richness). Finally, we tested a 2-factor model, comparing a happy life to the combination of a psychologically rich life and a meaningful life (i.e., equating richness and meaning). Once again, combining factors resulted in unacceptable fit indices. Overall, even the best-fitting 2-factor model fitted significantly worse than the 3-factor model: Δχ²(2) = 201.55, p < .001. In short, combining psychological richness with meaning, or with happiness, yielded a poorer fit than a 3-factor model representing psychological richness as a distinct construct.

We replicated the 3-factor structure in two additional independent samples (Ns = 348, 405; see Table 2). In the latter study (Oishi et al., 2021), in addition to the 15-item Good Life Scale (GLS) described above, we added additional measures in the form of the Satisfaction with Life Scale (Diener et al., 1985), Meaning in Life Questionnaire’s (Steger et al., 2006) presence subscale, and the Psychologically Rich Life Questionnaire (Oishi et al., 2019). Here each latent factor was indexed by the two scale scores (e.g., a happy life factor was indexed by the SWLS and the 5-item happy life subscale of the GLS). As can be seen in Table 2, the fit was excellent. In sum, a series of confirmatory factor analyses in three independent samples found that a 3-factor model, in which a psychologically rich life, a happy life, and a meaningful life are three distinct factors, fitted participants’ data best. Psychometrically, psychological richness cannot be reduced to an aspect of meaning or happiness.

Multitrait–Multimethod Approach

However, the above results rely solely on self-report. Thus, in the next studies, we incorporated informant reports to test convergent and discriminant validity using a multitrait–multimethod approach (Oishi et al., 2019). First, we found the 17-item Psychologically Rich Life Questionnaire (see appendix) was highly reliable (Cronbach’s α = .87–.93 across four studies), and 2-week test–retest reliability was high (r = .80). Self-reported psychological richness was positively correlated with informant reports, r = .35, p < .001 (see Table 3; 36% family members, 64% close friends). Furthermore, self-reported psychological richness was significantly more strongly correlated with informant reports of psychological richness than with informant reports of life satisfaction, positive affect, or meaning in life. In other words, the strongest agreement was between how psychologically rich an individual rated their own life and how rich their close friends and/or family rated it, suggesting that such ratings converged on the same construct. Finally, the key "monotrait–multimethod" coefficient was higher than the respective "multitrait–multimethod" coefficients, providing evidence of convergent and discriminant validity (Oishi et al., 2019, study 1).

Finally, we examined the association between psychological richness and a common measure of eudaimonic well-being, namely, Ryff’s (1989) six subscales, among 1,214 American adults. Self-reported psychological richness was moderately correlated with (but not identical to) self-acceptance (r = .54), purpose in life (r = .47), positive relations (r = .47), personal growth (r = .46), environmental mastery (r = .40), and autonomy (r = .27, p < .001). In all, across three factor-analytic studies and four psychometric validation studies we found that psychological richness is related to (but distinct from) happiness and meaning in life, according to both the participants themselves and people who know them well.

Obituaries

However, the majority of the above studies rely on college students, who are not only not representative, but also have lived out only a small portion of their lives. To address these methodological issues, we coded obituaries (Oishi & Choi, 2020, study 1). An obituary succinctly summarizes a person’s entire life, providing an ideal data source for testing whether a person’s life maximizes all three dimensions of a good life—happiness, meaning, and richness, all together—or only one or two. Three research assistants (blind to hypothesis) independently coded all obituaries published in the New York Times in June 2016, using 12 adjectives (i.e., "interesting," "dramatic," "eventful," and "psychologically rich," for the psychologically rich life; "happy," "comfortable," "secure," and "pleasant" for the happy life; "meaningful," "fulfilling," "sense of purpose," and "devotion" for the meaningful life). Once again, interrater agreement was substantial (intraclass correlations for psychologically rich life = .72; happy life = .68; meaningful life = .64). Whereas all three dimensions were moderately correlated in the college student self-report samples, this was not the case for the obituary codings. The more psychologically rich an obituary, the less happy the person’s life was, r (99) = −.26, p = .01, and the more meaningful r = .37, p < .001. Happiness and meaning in life were not correlated, r = −.08, p = .43.

One psychologically rich life from the New York Times is Dr. Simon Ramo, an aerospace engineer who died at the age of 103. Dr. Ramo authored 62 books, developed intercontinental ballistic missiles (ICBM), and became the oldest person to receive a patent, at the age of 100. After receiving his PhD in physics and engineering, he worked for General Electronic and Hughes Aircraft until, fed up with management, he quit to launch his own company. He was quickly awarded a government contract to develop advanced missile technology, which he did in secrecy from his headquarters in a former Catholic church. His life was rated as not only psychologically rich, but happy and meaningful as well.

Like Simon Ramo, many of the people featured in the New York Times obituary section were quite extraordinary. Thus, we turned to a local newspaper in Charlottesville, Virginia, where we coded an additional 116 obituaries detailing the lives of ordinary people who passed between June and August of 2016 (Oishi & Choi, 2020, study 2). Consider Paula Zo, who was rated as leading a particularly rich life. Ms. Zo died at the age 62 in June 2016. Although she suffered from postpolio syndrome and experienced disability due to multiple illnesses, Ms. Zo spent her life advocating for foster children in her job as a social worker. She loved theater, and starred in a number of local plays and commercials, including her self-authored one-person play "Crazy Mama." At the time of her passing, Ms. Zo was working on a second autobiographical comedy about her life as "a handicapped wheelchair bound woman." Consistent with the New York Times obituaries, Virginians who led psychologically rich lives were rated as less happy, r [114] = −.26, p = .005. Psychological richness and meaning were unrelated in this sample (r = .04, p = .67); happiness and meaning only marginally so (r = .16, p = .09). Thus, in obituaries from a local Virginian paper, richness, happiness, and meaning were once again distinct.

Does this generalize beyond Americans? To find out, we coded 111 obituaries from a major Singapore newspaper, Straight Times (Oishi & Choi, 2020, study 3). Leading a psychologically rich life was unrelated to a happy life (r = .02, p = .88), but positively associated with a meaningful life (r = .42, p < .01); happiness and meaning were also positively correlated (r = .37, p < .01). An example of a psychologically rich life is Louis Cha, who died at age 94 in Hong Kong. Cha launched his career as a journalist in Shanghai, before transferring to Hong Kong, where he authored an enormously popular series of martial arts history novels from 1955 to 1972. During this time, he also penned anti-Mao articles, which landed him on an assassination list; he escaped by fleeing to Singapore for nearly a year. These same political views resulted in a blanket ban of his works in China, which lasted until 1984. Despite his popularity, Cha never authored another book after 1972. Instead, he continued to re-read and revise his own novels, which he found deeply moving. He would cry upon re-reading his favorite characters’ breakup (the very same breakup that he himself had authored). Like Paula Zo, Louis Cha clearly experienced major challenges and setbacks in his life. Fleeing assassination is not a marker of a happy life. Yet, both Mr. Cha and Ms. Zo led extremely interesting, psychologically rich lives. Taken together, an internal meta-analysis of the obituaries from the New York Times, the Daily Progress (Virginia), and Straight Times (Singapore) overall finds psychological richness to be distinct from happiness in people’s lives (meta-analytic correlation = −.17), and correlated only moderately with meaning (meta-analytic correlation = .28). Happiness and meaning were only weakly correlated (meta-analytic correlation = .21).

In sum, across ten studies (three factor-analyses, four psychometric scale validation studies with both self- and informant reports, and three obituary studies), we found convergent support for our prediction that the three dimensions of a good life—happiness, meaning, and psychological richness—are related but separable from one another.

The Sources of a Psychologically Rich Life: Who Experiences Richness and Why (Prediction #2)

Below, we present further evidence that these dimensions stem from distinct sources. We begin by noting individual differences in who leads a psychologically rich life, before reporting longitudinal and experimental evidence for what creates psychologically rich experiences.

Personality and Individual Differences

Who has a psychologically rich life? Across seven studies, a psychologically rich life was best predicted by openness to experience, meta-analytic r = .47, and extraversion, meta-analytic r = .44 (see Table 4 for details). These effects held among American undergraduates, American and Indian mTurk workers, and a nationally representative sample of Americans (Oishi et al., 2019, Studies 1–4), and were replicated among Korean adults, and University of Florida and University of Virginia undergraduates. Tendencies toward fantasy, artistic sensitivity, and unconventional attitudes are thus associated with a psychologically rich life, but far from redundant with it. In contrast, a happy life was most strongly associated with extraversion, followed by conscientiousness, and low neuroticism, consistent with Steel et al. (2008). A meaningful life was associated fairly evenly with all Big Five traits.

Finally, although happiness was significantly associated with self-reported socioeconomic status (SES), r = .30, a psychologically rich life was only modestly associated with SES, income, or education, all rs < .19, and a meaningful life only weakly associated, r = .13. Taken together, these initial studies suggest that openness and extraversion are associated with a psychologically rich life, whereas extraversion, conscientiousness, and high SES appear to be key to a happy life.

Life Experiences Associated With a Psychologically Rich Life

Because a psychologically rich life is characterized by unexpectedness, novelty, complexity, and perspective change, some life experiences should be particularly conducive to it. Studying abroad is a case in point. Study abroad places a student in unfamiliar environments for an extended period of time, forcing them to live differently than they did at home. Thus, we predicted that studying abroad should increase psychological richness.

To test this, we recruited college students studying abroad, as well as students from the same university interested in study abroad but who had stayed on campus. We followed both groups via 12 weekly activity surveys, and assessed the three types of good life at the beginning and end of the semester (Oishi et al., in press, study 3). Initially, there were no group differences in psychological richness (measured by the 17-item PRLQ), life satisfaction, or meaning in life. By the end of the semester, however, the study-abroad students reported significantly higher psychological richness than those who remained on campus. This pattern was unique to psychological richness; there was no corresponding increase in happiness or meaning, which remained unchanged among the study-abroad students across the semester. Furthermore, these effects remained significant even after controlling for demographics and individual differences, including openness to experience and the Big Five. Thus, changes in psychological richness were not due to preexisting differences in personality or background, and extended beyond self-reports: People who knew the students well also rated the study-abroad students’ lives to be subsequently psychologically richer than of those who stayed on campus (Oishi et al., in press, study 4).

What about the study abroad led to psychologically richer lives? The increase in psychological richness was explained in part by study-abroad students’ weekly engagement in artistic activities. In another 14-day diary study assessing daily activities as well as psychological richness (Oishi et al., in press, study 1), participants reported greater psychological richness on days when they took short excursions. Like studying abroad, such short trips injected novel atypical experiences into everyday life.

Although not everyone is able to live or study abroad, people often voluntarily seek out similar novel experiences through popular means such as escape rooms (Cha et al., 2020). The goal in an escape room is to solve a series of puzzles to escape from one or more (often themed) rooms. In an initial study, we recruited individuals who had just exited an escape room. Consistent with our novelty hypothesis, people who had more previous experience with escape rooms reported less psychological richness after exiting. Individuals who "escaped" successfully reported being happier (and found the game more meaningful) than those who failed to escape. Interestingly, however, psychological richness was unrelated to the outcome (i.e., successful escape vs. failure). Instead, the more difficult people found the escape room, the more psychological richness (and less happiness) they reported, consistent with our prediction that complex challenging experiences should also be psychologically rich. This is consistent with our obituary studies (Oishi & Choi, 2020), where we likewise coded the number of challenging and dramatic life events people experienced, such as tragedies (e.g., bereavements) and divorce. Obituaries of people who experienced more dramatic life events were rated as psychologically richer (but less happy); dramatic life events were unrelated to meaning.

In sum, life experiences that are novel, challenging, and perspective-changing seem to contribute to a psychologically rich life, whether they are "big" experiences like studying abroad or divorce or "small" everyday experiences, like short trips or an escape room. However, because none of the studies above are experimental, they cannot establish causal direction.

Experimental Evidence for Psychologically Rich Experiences

Fortunately, we have experimental evidence from a line of studies exploring causal mechanisms for what makes certain experiences richer than others. We predict that novelty, complexity, and a change in perspective are key to psychologically rich experiences. Figure-ground drawings hit all three criteria, as they literally depict different images depending on one’s focal perspective. In a series of studies (Oishi, Choi, & Axt, 2020), we showed participants four figure-ground illusions where (for half of the participants) the image seen depended on whether one focused on the foreground (e.g., a moon) or background (e.g., a face; see Figure 1 for an example). The other half viewed modified versions of the same figure-ground illusions altered so only the foreground image was visible (control).

In study 1 (N = 299), participants viewed and described each of the four drawings, one at a time, and then indicated their current mood (e.g., happy, sad) and evaluations of the drawings (e.g., how interesting, boring). As expected, participants in the figure-ground condition reported substantially higher psychological richness after viewing the figure-ground drawings than those in the control condition, d = 1.38 (see Figure 1). However, positive mood in the figure-ground condition did not differ: d = .13. That is, the figure-ground drawings evoked more psychological richness without evoking more happiness.

We replicated these effects in a second study (N = 343), testing whether prompting participants to see both hidden images (the prompt "What do you see?" followed by another prompt "What else do you see?") further increased psychological richness. The prompt did not matter; in both conditions, participants viewing the figure-ground illusions reported substantially higher psychological richness than those in the control condition, d = 1.06 and 1.08, respectively. Once again, there was no difference in positive mood between conditions, |d| ≤ .05.

These findings suggest that duality, conflict, and complexity are associated with visual experiences of psychological richness. And, consistent with the correlational studies summarized earlier, this was true only for psychological richness; positive hedonic experiences (pleasant vs. unpleasant, happy vs. unhappy) were not associated with duality. Thus, the visual illusion studies provide initial causal evidence that psychological richness is distinct from happiness.

Such effects are not limited to visual experiences. Oishi, Choi, and Axt (2020, study 5) tested whether surprising information can make auditory experiences richer as well, among 238 college students who watched a video of world class pianist Nobuyuki Tsujii playing Chopin’s Etude Opus 10. Beforehand participants learned a little background about the musician: that "Nobuyuki Tsujii is a Japanese pianist," "He began his formal study of piano at age 4," and "At age 10, he debuted with the Century Orchestra, Osaka." However, in the experimental "perspective change" condition (randomly assigned), participants read four additional words: "He was born blind." As a result, participants in the perspective change condition (who knew the pianist was blind) found Tsuji’s performance psychologically richer than those in the control condition, who did not (d = .26, p = .05). But although this knowledge made the experience richer, it did not make it more pleasurable; there was no difference in positive affect between conditions (d = .10, p = .44). In short, information that induced a change of perspective increased psychological richness without increasing pleasure.

In sum, individual differences and situational features combine to facilitate psychological richness. Correlational and experimental evidence across a wide range of samples and contexts, including both lab and field studies, support the role of novel, complex, perspective-changing experiences as uniquely important to psychological richness. People high in openness to experience are more likely to report leading psychologically rich lives, perhaps because they are more likely to engage in such experiences. These patterns were very different for meaning and happiness, suggesting that the ingredients that make up a psychologically rich life are distinct from those that comprise a happy or meaningful life.

The Three Dimensions of a Good Life Have Unique Predictive Value (Prediction #3)

Above we present evidence that psychological richness is distinct from meaning and happiness in life, and stems from different sources. In addition, we (Oishi et al., 2021) have also tested whether the three dimensions of a good life uniquely predict important psychological variables, such as political liberalism, attributional complexity, curiosity, risk taking, and related constructs. For instance, people leading psychologically rich lives may be more open to changing current political structures and systems. Therefore, we simultaneously entered a happy life, a meaningful life, and a psychologically rich life as predictors of system justification (Kay & Jost, 2003) among over 500 college students (Oishi et al., 2021, study 1). Whereas a happy life and a meaningful life were positively associated with system justification (e.g., Butz et al., 2017; Napier & Jost, 2008), leading a psychologically rich life was negatively associated. That is, those leading happy and/or meaningful lives tend to prefer to maintain social order and the status quo, whereas those leading psychologically rich lives seem to embrace social change.

Subsequent studies confirm the system-defying tendencies of psychological richness versus system-justifying tendencies of happiness and meaning (Oishi et al., 2021, studies 2–4). For instance, in a national probabilistic sampling of Americans, a psychologically rich life was associated with political liberalism, whereas a happy life and meaningful life were associated with political conservatism. Such associations could be due to third variables such as openness to experience, which is associated with liberalism (Butz et al., 2017). However, these patterns persisted after controlling for Big Five personality traits and demographics (e.g., age, gender, race). That is, the association between psychological richness and liberalism could not be explained by the link between psychological richness and openness (or other personality traits).

We argue that psychological richness should also result in wisdom, characterized in part by flexibility of thought. Consistent with this, we have found a psychologically rich life to be positively associated with holistic thinking styles (e.g., Choi et al., 2007), attributional complexity (e.g., Fletcher et al., 1986), and lower levels of discrete essentialism (a subscale of essentialism, e.g., Bastian & Haslam, 2006). In short, those leading psychologically rich lives tend to have more complex reasoning styles, consider multiple causes for others’ behavior, and do not believe that a few discrete categories can explain individual differences. These findings remain significant, even after controlling for openness (and other Big Five traits), as well as a happy life, a meaningful life, and demographics.

If psychological richness results in wisdom, as theorized, it may also manifest as a desire to seek out challenges and opportunities for learning. For instance, the degree to which college students took on challenging coursework was uniquely predicted by a psychologically rich life, above and beyond a happy and/or meaningful life (Oishi et al., 2021, study 1). In contrast, happiness and meaning were unrelated to coursework. Furthermore, while students leading a happy and/or meaningful life were oriented toward positive achievement outcomes (i.e., good grades), those leading a psychologically rich life were oriented toward actual learning (i.e., mastery outcomes). These findings are consistent with our "escape room" studies, in which perceived difficulty was associated with psychological richness, whereas objective performance (i.e., success vs. failure) was associated with happiness and meaning.

Consistent with this, participants high in psychological richness also reported greater curiosity, whereas those whose lives were happy and/or meaningful did not (Oishi et al., 2021). "Stretching" curiosity (i.e., appetitive strivings for novelty and challenge) is thought to represent exploration, while "embracing" curiosity (i.e., immersive engagement in specific activities) is thought to represent absorption (Kashdan et al., 2004, 2018). These effects replicate among Korean adults (N = 2,176), where once again, psychological richness predicted both stretching and embracing forms of curiosity, above and beyond Big Five personality and demographics. Finally, we have found that while people leading happy and/or meaningful lives experience positive emotions more intensely and negative emotions less intensely, individuals with psychologically rich lives experience both positive and negative emotions more intensely, consistent with Larsen and Diener’s (1987) theorizing regarding affect intensity. In other words, the psychologically rich life appears to be emotionally intense.

However, the studies detailed above are cross-sectional; causality could run in either direction, despite statistically controlling for potential third variables such as openness to experience (Oishi et al., 2021, studies 2–4). Another limitation is their reliance on self-reports. Thus, in more recent studies we have utilized behavioral measures as well as short-term longitudinal designs. For instance, psychological richness predicts how many balloons people pop in the Balloon Analog Risk Task (Oishi et al., 2021, study 5). Likewise, a psychologically rich life (measured at time 1) prospectively predicts how often participants go on to engage in daily novel (vs. routine) activities over a 2-week period (Oishi et al., 2021, study 6). Most of these relationships remain significant even after additionally controlling for happiness, meaning, openness (and other Big Five personality traits), and demographics.

The above provide preliminary evidence that psychological richness predicts important outcomes related to exploration and motivation for systemic change; and that people whose lives are psychologically rich tend to seek out challenges and value learning, and are less interested in maintaining the status quo. Perhaps as a result, they experience both positive and negative emotions more intensely than those whose lives are not particularly rich. Finally, psychological richness uniquely predicts a behavioral measure of risk taking, as well as everyday engagement in novel and routine activities. Equally important, these associations remain significant even after controlling for many possible third variables.

The Psychologically Rich Life Is a Good Life (Prediction #4)

The evidence thus far suggests that a psychologically rich life is distinct from a happy or meaningful life in terms of predictors, correlates, and outcomes. Still, if nobody actually desires a psychologically rich life (vs. a happy or meaningful life), then it is not a good life, and thus there is no point to expanding the current dichotomous hedonic–eudaimonic model of well-being. Furthermore, the entire concept of a psychologically rich life might simply be a WEIRD (Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, and Democratic) phenomenon (Henrich et al., 2010). Therefore, we asked people to describe their own ideal lives, and report the extent to which they themselves value psychological richness.

We asked participants across nine countries (the United States, Germany, Norway, Portugal, Japan, South Korea, Singapore, India, and Angola; Oishi, Choi, & Koo, 2020, study 1) to describe, in their own words, their ideal life. We have summarized the most frequently mentioned words from four samples in Table 5; family, job/work, and money were by far the most common themes across cultures. There were however some interesting differences. American and Indian participants frequently mentioned free, love, and friends as main ingredients of an ideal life, whereas Japanese participants did not. Likewise, Indian participants mentioned peace and a peaceful life far more frequently than Americans. In contrast, Japanese participants emphasized a long-term focus on old age/retirement age, the self, and hobby, while Angolan participants were especially likely to mention God and stability.

After describing their ideal life, we asked participants to rate the life they had just described using 15 features associated with a happy life (e.g., happy, comfortable, stable), a meaningful life (e.g., meaningful, fulfilling), or a psychologically rich life (e.g., eventful, interesting). Overall, people rated their self-described ideal lives as all three: happy and meaningful, but also psychologically rich.

Finally, we asked participants, if they had to pick just one, whether they would choose a happy life, a meaningful life, or a psychologically rich life. Consistent with Diener (2000), the majority of participants in all countries surveyed preferred a happy life. The second most popular choice was a meaningful life, followed by a substantial minority of participants who chose a psychologically rich life.

In all, there was remarkable consensus across cultures in the percentage of people choosing a psychologically rich life, from 7% to 17% (see Figure 2). A psychologically rich life was most popular in Europe, India, Japan, and Korea. In Japan and Korea, the great majority chose a happy life, with the remaining split evenly between a meaningful life and a psychologically rich life (16% in both Japan and Korea). In India, the United States, and Europe, a meaningful life was more popular, although still not as popular as a happy life, with a minority favoring a psychologically rich life (16% India, 13% United States; 17% Germany, and 15% in Norway and Portugal). Finally, in Singapore and Angola, the majority were evenly split between a meaningful and a happy life, with a small minority choosing a psychologically rich life: 7% in Singapore and 9% in Angola.

Thus, although a happy life was the top preference across nine countries (followed by a meaningful life), a substantial minority would choose a psychologically rich life even at the expense of a happy or meaningful one. In three European countries, two East Asian countries, and one South Asian country (i.e., six of the nine countries studied), a psychologically rich life was chosen by 15%–17% of respondents, a nontrivial portion of the population in these countries.

Of course, it is possible that people may not know what they want, or are unable to verbalize it, especially if the very concept of a psychologically rich life is unfamiliar. Therefore, in follow-up studies, we used an indirect means of getting at the question (Oishi, Choi, & Koo, 2020, studies 2a and 2b). Specifically, we asked participants to think about the event that they regretted most in their lives. Consistent with previous research (Gilovich & Medvec, 1995), people’s most common regrets involved missed educational, career, and relationship opportunities, and mistakes concerning their education, career, family, and romantic relationships. Next, we then asked participants to imagine undoing that regret. If they did so, would their lives have been happier, more meaningful, or psychologically richer (forced choice)? In a national probability sample of Americans (N = 1,611), we found that 28% said their lives would have been psychologically richer if they could undo their life’s biggest regret (far higher than the 13% obtained in our earlier forced choice). The remaining respondents were split, with 35.7% of Americans saying their lives would have been happier and 36.7% saying their lives would have been more meaningful. We replicated this result among Korean adults (N = 680), 35% of whom felt their lives would have been psychologically richer if they could undo the most regretted event of their lives (vs. 16% in forced choice). Once again, the remaining respondents were split, with 27.6% of Koreans saying their lives would have been happier and 37.4% saying their lives would have been more meaningful. That a third of participants in both Korea and the United States regretted most an event that made their lives psychologically less rich suggests that richness is implicitly valued as part of what it means to lead a good life.

In sum, a great majority of people desire a happy life or a meaningful life (Diener, 2000; King & Napa, 1998). Still a minority of individuals do seem to desire a different ideal life, ranging from 7% to 17% (in forced choice paradigms) upward to a third (28% to 35%) using indirect measures. Like Goldmund, Zarathustra, and Stephen Dedalus, some people do choose the life of the wanderer: unstable, unpredictable, but free. Such a life is not easy, nor is it necessarily pleasant. But the empirical evidence described above suggests that some people actively prefer it.

Summary of Empirical Evidence

A psychologically rich life, filled with a wide variety of interesting and perspective-changing experiences, is distinct from a happy life and a meaningful life. Psychometrically, a 3-factor model, in which happiness, meaning, and psychological richness each constitute discrete constructs, fits the data significantly better than 1- or 2-factor models which conflate richness with happiness or meaning. Likewise, people with psychologically rich lives differ in personality from people leading happy or meaningful lives. Openness to experience, in particular, as well as extraversion strongly predicts psychological richness. Finally, leading a psychologically rich life predicts important outcomes above and beyond a happy and/or meaningful life, including system justification, political orientation, attributional complexity, and challenge-seeking.

In sum, the building blocks of a psychologically rich life are different. Particular life experiences and situational factors uniquely contribute to psychological richness, without increasing happiness or meaning. For instance, students’ lives were psychologically richer after a semester studying abroad, but not happier or more meaningful. In experimental work, we likewise find that perspective change uniquely predicts psychological richness. Figure-ground illusions consistently evoke more psychological richness than comparable drawings (but do not increase positive moods). Shifts in perspective increase how psychologically rich (but not how personally meaningful) people find cognitive activities, and perspective-changing information (e.g., learning that a pianist is blind) enriches a musical performance. Finally, perceived difficulty is uniquely associated with psychological richness and (independent of outcomes) predicts how rich, but not how happy or meaningful, people find escape rooms. This is clearly illustrated in the obituary studies: Dramatic (and mostly unpleasant) life events such as unemployment and bereavement are positively associated with richness but negatively associated with happiness.

Remaining Questions and Future Directions

The evidence above provides initial support that psychological richness is distinct from happiness, meaning, and other common constructs in psychology. However, considerable work remains to be done. Here we detail remaining questions about the nature of a psychologically rich life, the challenges of tragedy and adversity, and other related issues.

The Nature of Psychological Richness

Does it matter whether rich experiences are acquired first-hand? Evidence from obituaries (Oishi & Choi, 2020) and students studying abroad (Oishi et al., in press) suggests that relatively rare, dramatic life events should enhance psychological richness. Such experiences might be called experiential richness, stemming from direct, first-hand experience, and may be similar to other positive mood states. For instance, Csikszentmihalyi’s (1990) "flow," or a state of psychological absorption featuring intense focus and concentration in moments of optimal challenge, is similar to momentary experiences of psychological richness. Likewise, rich experiences may contain elements of awe and/or fear, wonder, and amazement (Anderson et al., 2020; Keltner & Haidt, 2003; Piff et al., 2015; Shiota et al., 2007).

But psychological richness could be also experienced vicariously. For instance, in his novel In Search of Lost Time, Marcel Proust (2003) describes his experience of intense events while absorbed in a novel: "These afternoons were crammed with more dramatic events than occur, often, in a whole lifetime. These were the events taking place in the book I was reading" (p. 116). He went on to describe the magic novelists wield, remarking how "for the space of an hour he [the novelist] sets free within us all the joys and sorrows in the world, a few of which only we should have to spend years of our actual life in getting to know, and the most intense of which would never be revealed to us" (p. 117). It is important to explore whether outcomes of experiential richness would be different from those of vicarious richness, stemming from such indirect, second-hand experiences.

Why Do People Desire Different Types of Lives?

We suggest three broad explanations for why some people desire a rich life, whereas others may desire a happy or meaningful life instead: (a) an individual differences/values based approach (i.e., people want a life that exemplifies their values), (b) a compensatory approach (i.e., people desire that which they lack), and (c) a self-justification approach (i.e., people desire that which they already have). One possibility outlined earlier is that personal dispositions and values guide different kinds of people to desire different kinds of lives. For instance, many people value security, stability, and comfort—and thus desire a happy life. Others aspire to make a difference in the world, and seek a meaningful life. Yet others value adventure and harbor a love of learning, desiring a psychologically rich life instead.

Another possibility is that the type of life people want depends on the type of life they are living now. For instance, people may desire that which is missing from their lives, in a compensatory process. For instance, a person living in unstable economic and political conditions might seek stability and a happy life. Similarly, a person whose life is stable and comfortable (but lacks adventure) might desire a psychologically rich life, while a person whose life is exciting (but lacks a clear sense of societal contribution) might desire a meaningful life. Or, the opposite may occur: People may desire more of that which they already have, in a self-justification process. For instance, a person already satisfied with their life might value and desire a happy life (which they already live), while a person whose life is full of purpose might value and desire a meaningful life (which they already live).

A separate but related question is whether people might wish to experience different types of life at different ages or times. For example, one might want to establish a happy life (with its accompanying security) first, before branching out to pursue a meaningful or psychologically rich life. This could explain why a happy life was the most dominant choice in our forced-choice cross-cultural study (Oishi, Choi, & Koo, 2020). It is critical to explore sources of systematic variation in why people prefer some lives to others, and the order in which they wish to experience them.

The Function of a Psychologically Rich Life

Most fundamentally, what is the function of a psychologically rich life? Happiness and meaning are thought to reflect (and reward) behaviors that foster long-term flourishing. For example, both happiness and meaning are associated with many positive outcomes, from health and longevity to strong relationships and creativity (Czekierda et al., 2017; Lyubomirsky et al., 2005). Some theorists have even suggested these benefits confer an evolutionary adaptation for the individual and the group (e.g., Diener et al., 2015).

The Exploitation–Exploration Dilemma

The Clash’s famous song "Should I stay? Or should I go?" poses a fundamental evolutionary challenge (Cohen et al., 2007). All organisms face the trade-off between exploiting the resources available to them and expending time and energy pursuing new resources that may (or may not) materialize. Other species such as birds are known to optimally mix these two different strategies (Krebs et al., 1978), either by switching strategies as their environment changes or via individual differences, such that certain individuals in a population are drawn to exploitation whereas others are drawn to exploration.

We suggest that a happy and/or meaningful life relies on an exploitation strategy, in which people take advantage of familiar environments and deep social ties, more heavily than an exploration strategy. In contrast, a psychologically rich life relies more heavily on an exploration strategy by expanding horizons and broadening social ties. In rapidly changing environments, a psychologically rich life might be most adaptive for learning and accumulating resources, whereas happy and/or meaningful lives might be more advantageous in stable, benign environments. Ideally, people might combine all three, balancing exploitation and exploration strategies as needed.

Fostering Discovery and Learning

A life of curiosity-driven learning helps individuals acquire skills in perspective-taking, empathy, and creativity (e.g., what we might collectively term "wisdom") needed to solve recurrent adaptation problems encountered in navigating complex, shifting physical and social environments (Tooby & Cosmides, 1990). These skills in turn accrue social and cognitive advantages that benefit not only the individual but also the group (e.g., Harvey, 2014). One pay-off of pursuing a psychologically rich life may be increased opportunities for discovery and learning. For instance, one of the strongest predictors of a rich life, openness to experience, is closely associated with what affective neuroscientist Jaak Panksepp (2011) calls the "seeking" system, which regulates the exploratory–investigatory system and motivates exploration and learning critical for long-term fitness (Montag & Panksepp, 2017). Openness to experience is also associated with creativity and problem-solving (Nettle, 2006), which not only allows people "to adjust to novel circumstances and to solve problems that unexpectedly arise," but "can also result in major contributions to human civilization" (Simonton, 2001, p. 2).

Similarly, another strong predictor of the psychologically rich life, extraversion, is closely associated with play (Montag & Panksepp, 2017). Robot learning paradigms suggest that curiosity-driven learning (or play-like learning) is key for healthy cognitive and social development (Oudeyer & Smith, 2016). And Gopnik (2020) has argued that humanity’s extended childhood allows for a prolonged period of exploration and play before the demands of adulthood force individuals to master useful (but repetitive) skill sets.

Coping With Boredom

The pursuit of psychological richness may also represent an antidote (and response) to boredom (Westgate & Wilson, 2018). In a famous quote, Pascal (1660/2006) once observed "that all the unhappiness of men arises from one single fact, that they cannot stay quietly in their own chamber" (pp. 27–28). Boredom leads people to seek out novel experiences, even when those experiences are unpleasant (e.g., electric shocks, unpleasant sounds/images; Bench & Lench, 2019; Havermans et al., 2015). Incorporating unusual activities into the routine of otherwise mundane lives not only reduces boredom but increases psychological richness, as well. Indeed, empirical research finds that a lack of optimal challenge and meaninglessness are at the core of boredom (Van Tilburg & Igou, 2012; Westgate & Wilson, 2018), and that boredom pushes people away from exploitation toward exploration strategies (Geana et al., 2016), which better characterize the psychologically rich life.

Maladaptive responses to boredom are thought to spur a number of societal problems, including gang violence (Eligon, 2016). Interestingly, among the most successful interventions are youth sports programs that introduce sources of psychological richness to people’s lives: reducing boredom (and gang violence) via engagement in novel activities. Such interventions reduce antisocial behaviors and internalizing problems, and improve academic achievement (Spruit et al., 2016). Nor are they confined to sports, spanning not only after-school soccer programs for Californian teens (Buckle & Walsh, 2013), but also arts and theater-based interventions among at-risk youth (Elliott & Dingwall, 2017; Wallace-DiGarbo & Hill, 2006). These interventions reduce boredom by introducing novel activities that youth might not otherwise encounter or turn to. In short, psychological richness (and the activities that facilitate it) may serve as both a response to, and defense against, boredom.

The Role of Tragedy, Adversity, and Other Challenges

Can a person who has suffered a major disaster or tragedy still be said to be leading a good life—that is, a life that is admirable, valuable, and worthwhile—despite their generally lower levels of subjective well-being (Nussbaum, 1986/2001)? Our argument is yes. We suggest that one important function of the psychologically rich life is to cope with tragedy and other adverse events. Difficult moments are inevitable. But by valuing them (and the perspective changes they bring), people may find value in experiences and lives that are not otherwise happy or meaningful. In other words, psychological richness requires valuing not only the bright moments of life, but also the darker moments that make such moments possible.

Aristotle was adamant about the necessity of good fortune for living a eudaimonic life, noting that "in old age even the most prosperous of men may be involved in great misfortunes … nobody calls happy a man who suffered fortunes like [Priam’s] and met a miserable end" (Aristotle, I009b21–I100a9, Thomson, 1955, p. 81). Empirical evidence generally supports him. For instance, 3 months after the Van earthquake in 2011, Turkish residents at the epicenter reported substantially lower life satisfaction than those who lived in Manisa, unaffected by the quake. Sixteen years later, residents who lost their homes in the 1995 Hanshin Awaji earthquake in Kobe, Japan, reported lower life satisfaction, more negative affect, and more health problems than residents whose homes were undamaged (Oishi et al., 2015), and survivors of Hurricane Katrina still report elevated psychological distress 12 years later (Raker et al., 2019). Clearly, such tragedies have long-lasting effects on happiness. However, many victims of natural disasters, and other traumatic events, go on to lead admirable lives in other respects; earthquake victims, for instance, are more likely to ultimately pursue prosocial careers (Oishi, Yagi, et al., 2016). In short, while recovery from tragedies can lead to a greater sense of meaning (Park, 2016), it may also offer opportunities for perspective change, a key aspect of cognitive restructuring (Clore, 2011), and an ingredient for psychological richness.

The Power of Construals

Tragedy, trauma, and other adverse life events are powerful situational forces. However, two individuals who experience the same ups and downs of life may internalize those events very differently. Thus, it is not enough to simply experience tragedy (or even a great variety of interesting/positive events). For such experiences to contribute to psychological richness requires the ability to remember, actively reflect, and integrate those experiences (e.g., resulting in narrative complexity and integrative growth; Bauer et al., 2008; McAdams et al., 2004). A basic prerequisite for such integration is the ability to store and recall memories (Fivush, 2011), which, at an extreme, certain brain injuries might preclude (Damasio et al., 1990). Thus, self-reflection and memory may be prerequisites for transforming experiential richness into psychological richness. For example, sensation seekers may encounter many thrilling experiences, but those thrilling experiences will not necessarily translate into psychological richness if merely unthinkingly "consumed." Similarly, to the extent that a psychologically rich life is defined by diverse experiences, it is likely related to affective complexity (Larsen & Cutler, 1996; Wessman & Ricks, 1966) and emodiversity (Quoidbach et al., 2014). However, the co-occurrence of various emotions (e.g., emotional complexity) need not inevitably produce a subjective sense of psychological richness. One could feel a wide range of emotions—from happiness, pride, and excitement to embarrassment and guilt—without necessarily experiencing psychological richness (e.g., one could simply feel torn and ambivalent; see Donahue et al., 1993).

Processing tragic, dramatic, or surprising experiences by making sense of them may be a key component. Consider Pennebaker and colleagues’ influential research on the effect of writing on recovery from traumatic events (see Pennebaker, 1997, for review). Writing about a traumatic event not only improves health, but does so via people’s ability to form a narrative or coherent story that explains the trauma they have experienced (see also Kross & Ayduk, 2011). Thus, although tragedies and adversity may offer opportunities for revising mental narratives and structures to accommodate them (Perlin & Li, 2020), actually engaging in such accommodative processing may be necessary to ultimately experience such events as psychologically rich.

In sum, the same processes that promote psychological richness may also contribute to coping with tragic and traumatic events. It is thus critical to identify the individual, situational, and developmental factors that facilitate resilience (Masten, 2001) and adaptive narratives (Pennebaker, 1997) in the short term, as well as long-term psychological richness.

Cultural Variations

Like any psychological concept, what it means to live a good life is culturally construed. For instance, what it means to be "well" differs across cultures (e.g., Kitayama & Markus, 2000). In the United States, well-being is fostered via mutual praise and reaffirmation of autonomy. In contrast, in other cultures well-being might be fostered via mutual sympathy for shared predicaments and reaffirmation of roles. Just as there are diverse modes of the self (Markus & Hamedani, 2007), so too are there many views of the good life (see Table 5). Consider how students from Angola, Japan, Norway, and the United States described their own conceptions of the good life. A 22-year-old Angolan woman focused on the structural resources that allow people to flourish; in an ideal life she would "have the right to health and health care, a favorable education, universities with qualified teachers, stuffed libraries, employment with salary able to meet my needs, a shelter, have time and money for leisure." An 18-year-old American man also focused on resources, but from an individualistic perspective—his ideal life would be "on a beach with lots of money and a happy family. I would not want to worry about working as I would have been successful enough so I would not have to work anymore. I could fly around anywhere I wanted at any time." In contrast, students from Japan and Norway focused on social relationships. An 18-year-old Japanese man noted that his ideal life "doesn’t have to be luxurious, as long as not unhappy. Preferably a family." And, in the words of a Norwegian woman, "The ideal life includes close and secure relationships, psychological growth, mastery, and a continuous curiosity and learning about life."

These examples (see Table 5) illustrate individual and cultural variation in what people desire from a good life, ranging from basic needs satisfaction to family and close relationships, to personal growth, freedom, and peace (see Tsai, 2007, on ideal affect). Psychological richness, meaning, and happiness touch on only some of these themes. Just as what it means to be well differs across individuals and cultures, what it means for a life to be interesting and psychologically rich is likely to differ across individuals and cultures. Documenting, quantifying, and predicting these variations is the next major step in understanding the good life.

Beyond the Three Dimensions of a Good Life

In this article, we have focused on extending the hedonic–eudaimonic dichotomy to include psychological richness. However, there is no reason to assume that there are only three dimensions to a good life. If a good life is defined broadly as a life worth living, and if values (defined as superordinate goals, or guiding life principles) represent the types of lives worth aspiring to, there could be as many forms of a good life as there are distinct values. For instance, Vernon and Allport (1931) proposed six values: theoretical, economic, esthetic, political, social, and religious. Thus, we could derive six types of a good life: an intellectual life, a materially wealthy life, an artistic/creative life, an influential life, a loving/caring life, and a religious life. Likewise, Schwartz (1992) argued for the existence of ten universal values: power, achievement, hedonism, stimulation, self-direction, universalism, benevolence, conformity, tradition, and security. From these, we could derive ten corresponding forms of the good life (a powerful/influential life, a successful life, an enjoyable life, a stimulating life, etc.). The same logic can be extended to other sets of values (e.g., Rokeach, 1973), as well as virtues and character strengths (e.g., wisdom and knowledge, courage; Peterson & Seligman, 2004). Work on character strengths has tended to empirically classify strengths as belonging either to the "head" (e.g., creativity, curiosity, love of learning, appreciation of beauty) or the "heart" (e.g., love, forgiveness, kindness, spirituality; Park & Peterson, 2010). Interestingly, this "head versus heart" classification is consistent with Aristotle’s own claim that two types of virtues exist: intellectual and moral (Thomson, 1955). Among these are many candidates (e.g., courage, modesty, patience) that might map onto other forms of the good life, and ultimately go on to comprise a comprehensive model of a good life that goes beyond happiness, meaning, and psychological richness.

A Psychologically Rich Life: Toward a More Inclusive Understanding of the Good Life

A key contribution of our work is widening the conceptualization of a good life beyond the extant 2-factor model of hedonic versus eudaimonic well-being (King & Napa, 1998; Ryan & Deci, 2001). Many important literary figures—Muriel Barbery’s (2008) Renée, Rabin Alameddine’s (2013) Aaliya, Hermann Hesse’s (1932) Goldmund—do not lead happy lives. Nor do they lead meaningful ones. Nevertheless, they appreciate moments of ineffable beauty, Proustian moments of elongated time and esthetics, and lead lives full of inner and experiential richness. The psychologically rich life is an attempt to capture such lives, which—according to existing definitions in psychology—simply lack happiness and meaning. In a sense, they and their kind are forgotten; overlooked and dismissed in the extant literature on well-being. Instead, we offer a new means to understanding them as individuals leading good lives—psychologically rich lives, full of interesting, complex, novel first- and second-hand experiences that, although not always pleasant, foster valuable changes in worldview and perspective.

Our findings suggest that many people’s lives are psychologically rich, even if they are not particularly happy or meaningful, and that these lives have subjective value. This recognition is a major benefit of conceptualizing a psychologically rich life as a type of good life to be valued and pursued.

Second, by exploring psychological richness as a new dimension of a good life, we shed light on what it means to lead a happy life. A happy life is characterized by comfort, safety, and stability; happy people tend to have stable romantic relationships (Diener & Seligman, 2002) and secure, well-paying jobs (Drobnič et al., 2010; Helbling & Kanji, 2017). In a sense, then, a happy life is somewhat conventional. A surprising correlation between political conservatism and happiness (Napier & Jost, 2008) might be in part explained by conservative individuals’ emphasis on marriage, stability, and security, in general. Such stability is, of course, antithetical to seeking out the kind of rich varied experiences required to live a psychologically rich life, which require venturing beyond what is known and certain to grapple with (often uncomfortable) new ideas and perspectives.

In addition to adding a new dimension to well-being research, understanding the psychologically rich life could provide new empirical answers to fundamental questions such as "What is the role of art?" and "Why do people watch sad films?" Some esthetic experiences are not pleasant, but they are interesting (Berlyne, 1971; Silvia, 2008); for instance, people seek out sad movies when they want to gain insight into the human condition (Parrott, 2014; see also Tamir, 2016). And quite possibly, such experiences might not increase happiness but could increase psychological richness. For instance, Eskine et al. (2012) found that participants experienced geometric abstract art as more sublime when induced to feel fear than when experiencing happiness or physiological arousal. Such unusual moods might likewise serve to enhance interest and richness. In the past, it has been difficult to pinpoint the benefits of art, literature, music, and sports on well-being. One possibility is that we have been looking for benefits in the wrong places, by focusing exclusively on happiness and positive affect (see Dodell-Feder & Tamir, 2018, for effects on social cognition). In the future, we could quantify the benefits of exposure to literature, music, sports, and so on using psychological richness instead.

Likewise, other important and societally valuable experiences have not always been associated with hedonic well-being. For instance, racial diversity in Facebook friendship networks is inversely associated with hedonic well-being among White American college students (Seder & Oishi, 2009). That is, students with more diverse friends reported lower life satisfaction and positive affect than those with racially homogeneous friendship networks. Likewise, immigrants tend to report lower hedonic well-being (du Plooy et al., 2019). These studies paint a bleak picture of diversity and immigration for conventional well-being. We suggest this interpretation is short-sighted; the negative correlates reported in past work might have been associated with an increase in psychological richness, had it been assessed.

Similarly, education is not generally associated with life satisfaction or positive affect after controlling for income (Frey & Stutzer, 2002). But higher education is not a happiness-generating activity. It requires hard work, effort, and disquieting moments of doubt and uncertainty; such challenges may be desirable for learning but are not always pleasant. Thus, traditional measures of well-being may have simply failed to capture the positive aspects of learning. Unlike happiness, our conception of richness allows for moments of discomfort and unpleasant emotion. Research on a psychologically rich life suggests that a good life may not always be pleasant, and that there is value in leading lives that investigate different perspectives. Recognition of a psychologically rich life as another form of a good life may explain why people sometimes seek out such experiences (e.g., studying foreign languages, reading James Joyce’s Ulysses) at the expense of their own comfort. The ability to make sense of such behaviors is a benefit of psychological richness.

Conclusion

The extant literature centers on a dichotomy: hedonic versus eudaimonic well-being (e.g., King & Napa, 1998; Ryan & Deci, 2000). It is high time to move beyond it. Friedrich Nietzsche and Kaufmann (1978) in Thus Spoke Zarathustra dismissed happiness and meaning, promoting the life of the wanderer instead. To be sure, we are not dismissing happiness or meaning. A happy life, full of enjoyment, stability, and comfort, is a good life, as is too a meaningful life. However, just as Hermann Hesse (1984) wondered whether his character Goldmund’s life—unpleasant and painful at times, but eventful, dramatic, and interesting—was also a good life, so too do we. In short, we argue that psychological richness should join happiness and meaning as important components of well-being.

Empirical research shows that psychologically rich lives are uniquely associated with curiosity, openness to experience, and willingness to defy systems. Complexity and difficulty fuel psychologically rich experiences, and informant and self-reports converge in suggesting that psychological richness grows over time in response to perspective-changing life experiences such as studying abroad. Finally, not only do people across cultures view a psychologically rich life as a good life, but also a small but consistent minority would even choose it at the expense of a happy or meaningful life.

Our concept of psychological richness expands our understanding of a good life to include people like Goldmund, Louis Cha, and survivors of natural disasters and other tragic life events, leaving them no longer forgotten by psychological science. In doing so, the addition of psychological richness broadens, deepens, and enriches empirical research on a good life.

Colin DeYoung: Personalidad, Neurociencia y la Teoría Cibernética del Big Five

Entrevista con el psicólogo Colin DeYoung sobre personalidad, cerebro y psicopatología

Transcripción editada y traducida
Pregunta: ¿Cómo describirías tu investigación a alguien que no es del campo?

Esa es una pregunta divertida, porque tengo un par de historias que cuento con regularidad. En una fiesta o en un bar, suelo decir que soy psicólogo y, cuando preguntan qué tipo de psicología estudio, respondo que personalidad. Entonces la gente me pregunta una de dos cosas: o bien «¿estás analizando mi personalidad ahora mismo?», a lo que siempre miento y digo que no (aunque, claro, todos analizamos la personalidad de los demás cuando conocemos a alguien); o bien preguntan si la personalidad puede cambiar, lo cual es un tema que interesa mucho a la gente y que también ha cobrado importancia en la psicología de la personalidad en las últimas décadas.

Si la conversación se profundiza, digo que me interesa la estructura y las fuentes de nuestras personalidades: qué elementos son importantes, de dónde vienen, cómo los genera el cerebro. Hago mucha investigación neurocientífica y de neuroimagen, y me interesan los sistemas cerebrales implicados en los distintos rasgos de personalidad y cómo esos rasgos pueden ser factores de riesgo para problemas de salud mental. Y si la charla se pone muy técnica, termino hablando de cibernética, que es el estudio de los principios que rigen los sistemas orientados a metas, con procesos de retroalimentación que permiten a los organismos rastrear su posición en relación con un objetivo.

Pregunta: ¿Podrías contarnos más sobre tu teoría cibernética del Big Five?

Ese fue mi intento de crear una «teoría del todo» para la personalidad. Desde la universidad me interesaba integrar no solo las descripciones conductuales, sino también la neurociencia. Leí un libro de Carver y Scheier sobre la autorregulación del comportamiento, que fue mi introducción a las ideas cibernéticas. Ellos llevaron la cibernética a la psicología social, pero luego también aplicaron ese enfoque a las diferencias individuales.

La idea central es que todo lo que hacemos, toda la información que procesamos, está orientado y moldeado por las metas que tenemos. Todos los organismos persiguen metas para sobrevivir y reproducirse. Con el tiempo fui desarrollando una explicación coherente de todo lo que los psicólogos consideran personalidad. La personalidad no son solo los cinco grandes rasgos; es cualquier patrón persistente en la conducta y la experiencia a lo largo del tiempo. La teoría se llama «Teoría Cibernética del Big Five», porque los cinco grandes son las dimensiones principales de covariación entre todas las diferencias más específicas de la personalidad. Son especiales porque, si evalúas a las personas en cien características diferentes y buscas cuáles tienden a aparecer juntas (mediante análisis factorial), obtienes algo que se asemeja mucho a los cinco grandes.

Lo fascinante es preguntarse por qué se agrupan así. ¿Cuáles son los mecanismos subyacentes que causan esa agrupación? La mayor parte de la investigación en personalidad se ha centrado en predecir resultados (éxito laboral, satisfacción en las relaciones, etc.), pero a mí me interesaba ir en dirección opuesta: ¿de dónde viene la personalidad? Ahí es donde entran los mecanismos cibernéticos y su base neural.

Pregunta: ¿Hemos encontrado correlatos neurales que expliquen estas agrupaciones? Por ejemplo, los circuitos emocionales de Panksepp… ¿ves algo similar en el Big Five?

Sí, definitivamente. Panksepp tenía una tarea más fácil porque se centraba solo en las emociones, pero la personalidad incluye más que eso: patrones de pensamiento, metas, etc. Sin embargo, donde más hemos progresado en neurociencia es con la extraversión y el neuroticismo, porque reflejan diferencias básicas en sensibilidad a la recompensa (extraversión) y sensibilidad al castigo (neuroticismo). La recompensa y el castigo son procesos psicológicos fundamentales que hemos estudiado en profundidad en otras especies, y los circuitos están muy conservados en los mamíferos. Aunque el cerebro humano es proporcionalmente más grande, las estructuras subcorticales donde se procesan estas motivaciones son muy similares a las de una rata. Por eso, el cerebro de una rata es probablemente un mejor modelo del cerebro humano que la idea que algunos científicos tienen de cómo funciona el cerebro humano, porque el cerebro es el objeto más complicado del universo conocido.

Pregunta: ¿Cómo se relaciona esto con el sistema nervioso, el estrés, el cortisol, la dopamina… esa dinámica de aproximación vs. evitación?

Exactamente. La extraversión representa diferencias en las tendencias de aproximación, impulsadas por la sensibilidad a la recompensa, y el neuroticismo se refiere a la tendencia a experimentar emociones negativas, desencadenadas por la amenaza y el castigo. Pero hay que matizar lo de sistema nervioso simpático y parasimpático: ambos se activan tanto en la aproximación como en la evitación; el simpático te pone en acción («lucha o huida»), mientras que el parasimpático se ocupa del reposo y la digestión. La excitación positiva también activa el simpático.

La dopamina se ha descrito como la señal del coste del esfuerzo: te dice cuándo merece la pena hacer algo y te energiza para avanzar. Pero no es la «química del placer»; los opioides endógenos producen el placer. La dopamina produce deseo. De hecho, si bloqueas el sistema opioide mientras das anfetaminas, la persona siente la excitación y el querer, pero no el placer. Así que la dopamina es más la química de la esperanza o del deseo, no del placer.

Pregunta: ¿Qué nos dice esto sobre la estabilidad de los rasgos y la interacción naturaleza vs. crianza?

La respuesta fácil es que son ambas cosas. Todos los rasgos conductuales estudiados muestran influencias genéticas y ambientales. La genética establece la probabilidad de cómo será nuestra personalidad, pero hay muchas oportunidades para que las experiencias (estrés, trauma, etc.) la moldeen a lo largo del desarrollo, desde el útero hasta la vejez. La personalidad cambia en la edad adulta, aunque es difícil encontrar eventos vitales específicos que causen cambios sistemáticos en la mayoría de las personas. Algunos son el primer empleo o la primera relación seria, que producen cambios predecibles. Pero otros, como tener hijos, no muestran un patrón regular porque afectan a cada persona de manera distinta: unos se vuelven más responsables y otros se desorganizan por completo. La investigación actual está empezando a mirar las diferencias individuales en el cambio, no solo el cambio promedio.

Pregunta: ¿Crees que los cambios tecnológicos (como las redes sociales) pueden provocar cambios de personalidad a nivel generacional?

Absolutamente. Las tecnologías suelen tener efectos disruptivos enormes en las sociedades y el desarrollo humano. Por ejemplo, la imprenta provocó décadas de guerra. En cuanto a los rasgos, hay estudios que muestran cambios históricos en la asertividad de las mujeres (un componente de la extraversión) a lo largo del siglo XX: alta en los años 30-40 (Gran Depresión y Segunda Guerra Mundial), baja en los 50-60 (vuelta a la familia nuclear), y alta de nuevo en los 70-80 (movimiento feminista). Así que sí, los cambios sociotecnológicos pueden moldear la personalidad. Por ejemplo, estudiar en el extranjero aumenta la apertura a la experiencia. La exposición a mucha información a través de internet podría aumentar la apertura, pero también podría ser abrumadora y desencadenar respuestas de amenaza. Lo desconocido tiene una naturaleza ambivalente: es a la vez prometedor y amenazante.

Pregunta: ¿Cómo se relacionan los trastornos mentales con los extremos del Big Five?

Casi todos los rasgos del Big Five son factores de riesgo para la psicopatología. La estructura de los síntomas de los trastornos mentales es muy similar a la estructura de la variación normal de la personalidad. Por ejemplo, el neuroticismo se corresponde con los problemas internalizantes (ansiedad, depresión); la baja extraversión se llama «desapego» y es un riesgo para la depresión y el aislamiento social; la alta extraversión (especialmente la asertividad) puede llevar a problemas con drogas, conducta antisocial, juego… ambos extremos tienen riesgos.

La apertura extrema es un riesgo para la psicosis, aunque hay un componente intelectual que protege y otro más estético/fantasioso que aumenta el riesgo. La conciencia baja (desinhibición) se asocia con impulsividad y falta de perseverancia; la conciencia alta puede llevar a trastornos compulsivos, anorexia, etc. La amabilidad baja (antagonismo) se relaciona con problemas de explotación y falta de empatía.

Pero tener un rasgo extremo no significa tener un trastorno. La clave es el «disfuncionamiento cibernético»: cuando la persona no puede perseguir sus metas importantes de manera efectiva. Por ejemplo, hay personas con alucinaciones auditivas diarias (psíquicos) que no están trastornadas porque funcionan bien en su vida. La mera presencia de síntomas no es suficiente; tiene que haber deterioro en el funcionamiento.

Pregunta: ¿Qué cambios propondrías para el diagnóstico psiquiátrico, como el enfoque dimensional del HiTOP?

El DSM es categórico y no se ajusta a la realidad. No hay categorías discretas; hay espectros. El modelo HiTOP (Taxonomía Jerárquica de la Psicopatología) propone un sistema dimensional: se evalúa el perfil de la persona en distintos espectros (desapego, trastorno del pensamiento, etc.) y se identifica dónde hay elevaciones. La ventaja es que los tratamientos funcionan mejor cuando se adaptan a estos perfiles, y sabemos que los mismos tratamientos sirven para varios trastornos que comparten una base dimensional.

El problema es que la medicina opera por tradición y autoridad, no por ciencia. Aunque los clínicos ya usan un enfoque dimensional de forma intuitiva, el sistema oficial es difícil de cambiar. Pero al menos la idea de espectro se está filtrando al público, aunque a veces se malinterpreta (por ejemplo, «estar en el espectro» del autismo, cuando en realidad todos estamos en algún punto del espectro).

Pregunta: ¿Algún comentario final sobre el futuro de la investigación o la IA?

No voy a tocar el tema de la IA porque es una caja de Pandora, pero sí creo que pronto tendremos inteligencia artificial general (AGI). Los modelos de lenguaje ya tienen el «pensamiento rápido»; solo falta añadir un módulo de razonamiento lógico y causal. También creo que las máquinas podrían ser conscientes, aunque eso es más difícil de lo que algunos piensan.

En mi campo, la neurociencia de la personalidad sigue avanzando. Ahora tenemos estudios con muestras grandes que permiten estudiar diferencias individuales con más potencia. Me interesa especialmente la amabilidad (empatía y teoría de la mente), la apertura (creatividad y procesos asociativos) y la conciencia (priorización de metas). Hay un sinfín de preguntas sobre el cerebro y la personalidad, y no creo que me aburra nunca.

If I were to come up with a single phrase for what I think the full story is; the thesis of Utena if you will, it would be this: “gender as a structure reproduces itself violently: especially through romance and sex, especially against children, especially within families – and, at its core, all three of these at once.”

Before I explain that with the depth it deserves, I want to get into a couple of disclaimers. Your standard massive spoiler warning first of all, and the same set of content caveats that the show itself requires. I also want to clarify that I’m only going to really be considering the show itself in this essay: I don’t think a single version of Utena besides the show has come close to achieving what it has done.

The movie is a wildly sensationalized, if cathartic, retelling of the show that skips over far too much to be able to stand, or even be coherent, on its own merits. Everything is turned up to twelve, and a massive amount is lost in the noise – it is an absolutely beautiful end effort, both visually and emotionally, but it is shallow and sorely lacking in context, nuance, and narrative integrity when looked at alone.

Going to be straight-up here and admit I just hate the manga. It plays everything straight that the show criticized, getting character after character entirely wrong – or at the very least, entirely different. It felt insulting to read, shockingly surface-level. Even in the more recent anniversary chapters, it feels as though some driving force was seriously lost along the way; only aping at the show at its best, and at its worst actively undermining the show’s arguments.

I’m speaking strongly here. It’s a rather bold claim to say “no non-show version of Utena actually understands Utena,” but I’m saying it anyway. In this essay I will be regarding both the movie and the manga as entirely extracanonical, and rarely if ever going to them for corroborating or conflicting information.

So what’s missing from the other iterations? Why do I feel like they fall so flat?

It’s not a function of length, though length contributes to it. It is that Utena as a story and as a thesis comes together in a single point. Understanding the most important things the series has to say depends on one link of a quite convoluted chain; one that, when adaptations lack it, makes them lose a huge portion of the core focus. While you get fragments of it elsewhere – Adolescence’s discussion on The Road and by which motions it is surpassed, or the manga’s focus on the concept and mythology behind a story – the true weight of Utena, its hard core, only falls together in one place.

It’s no secret that the anime had far greater levels of character development for people like Juri, Miki, and even Saionji, while the movie and manga leave out people like Wakaba and Mikage (and most of the black roses, predictably) almost entirely. But even the character count isn’t the issue here – none of these are part of the thesis I mentioned; they are only supplements. Cool supplements, incredibly moving supplements; but in my conception, side content nonetheless. So what is this missing link, if you will?

The short answer is that it’s obviously Nanami, who is completely absent but for a stray photograph or contextless cow joke everywhere but the show. The thesis I stated before – gendered abuse within the family – is really impossible to illustrate fully without her. Utena is very predominantly, if not primarily, about sibling incest.

I feel like the subject of incest in Utena is often recognized, but often glossed over – out of reflexive disgust, confusion, misidentification, or higher prioritization of the previously mentioned issues. This is partially for good reason; people can be very uncomfortable and/or unfamiliar with talking about and discussing it. However, I do believe it’s true, no matter how difficult it is to look at.

Utena is unique in that it posits sibling incest not only as an example of abuse, but abuse-in-archetype. A distilled and concentrated form of the concept; a kind of definition. What does this mean, properly, for the show itself? We’ll get into it in its full details in the next part, but a quick overview couldn’t hurt.

The core story and argument of Utena is told in five necessary characters: Akio, Anthy, Utena, Touga, and Nanami. I remember Ikuhara identifying the first three as true main characters, and the last two are natural and unavoidable counterparts. Together these five make up a rather didactic character gallery through which Utena presents its world.

Utena has a very defined notion of The World. What the world – the world of Ohtori – is, how it functions, and how it maintains itself. Ohtori’s power structures, its channels and darker machinations masked by a pristine story-based ruling law. A mechanism, defined and acted out by people. The reason why Nanami is so crucial in understanding this is that while the other four characters are series staples, she is necessary to round out the non-Utena quartet; to complete the circle. She is in some ways a counterpart to Utena here – similar in position, but within the mechanism, instead of foreign to it.

This is how the world of Ohtori is built. When you boil it down – take out the extraneous parts and flourishes and tangents, when you cut it down to the real core, you see that it is a very clear kind of dynamic: a very old brother and sister pair raising up a younger one. It is the same dynamic – only that one is in its latest stages of long-term abuse, and the other we are able to take shape for the very first time.

Akio and Anthy together compose the mythological archetype of Ohtori’s central power structure – the sibling pair. They are heavily implied to be magical, otherworldly almost, and stemming from the foundations of the world. Recalling the show’s version of the Tale of the Rose – one if its several ruling myths – the world has long since fallen completely into darkness. Primordially, past memory. The Himemiyas are living leitmotifs.

Touga and Nanami are the natural counterpart to said archetype. Touga is well established as Akio’s right-hand boy, and as student council president is the direct analogue of chairman on the student echelon of Ohtori’s society. Nanami is as Anthy once was – a young girl who loves just a bit too strongly, and is made to suffer for it. If Anthy has no heart to be broken, Nanami has a heart big enough for two people, and we can already see this being acted on. Touga is following directly in Akio’s footsteps – sometimes clumsily, sometimes getting hurt himself in the process – but steadfastly nonetheless. And, for a long time, Nanami is fooled into coming along.

Within Touga and Akio we see a strange kind of intimacy and alignment in goals – more than anything, their relationship is an apprenticeship. We see the interlocking gears of male power; how it is retaught and reinforced generationally, and the mindsets on both ends that conduct its channels so well. It is a strange balance between grooming and complicity; learned behavior and inherent self, and can be surprisingly subtle at times. We see Touga learn from the best how to master the social positioning and manipulation practices necessary to control the world’s currents, and he takes to it well.

Meanwhile, in Nanami and Anthy, we see a deep, self-defeating resentment. Their dislike for each other often edges into real hatred, but different flavors from each of them. Nanami is in many ways a younger Anthy, and Anthy’s self-hatred certainly runs deep enough to project itself onto other people. Nanami, on the other hand, comes from a place of more naïve, confused threatenedness – convinced Anthy will usurp her position, and still not having the words to articulate why she feels that. Ohtori undermines desperately necessary forms of female solidarity. Competitiveness, jealousy, tenuousness-of-self, and eventual horrible understanding all mark their relationship, which I find to be one of the most overlooked ones in the show.

Anthy and Akio are the endpoints of what Nanami and Touga could become. This pair of pairs is a deliberate choice, and one that asks for parallels to be made of it – it asks us to look at how abuse begins and continues, along with its effects over time; from the very beginning to the very end. Within this compound gear we can see the formulation, perpetuation, and stabilization of the concept of abuse in Ohtori, as well as its very human cost.

Akio and Anthy exemplify how the story was written, and Touga and Nanami reflect a modern retelling of that same story. Touga and Akio perpetuate the story’s power generationally, and Nanami and Anthy together prevent the story from being acted against.

And Utena, at the last, comes to break that story entirely. It shatters at the root.

Chapter 3: part two, painting in blood

(See the end of the chapter for notes.)

I don’t like explicit content warnings very much, especially when presenting what I’m going to cover as clearly as this, and I’ve said before that this entire series of essays requires the same kinds of caution that the series did. Even so, I really do feel like I need to reiterate it here: this was rough to write and I expect it to be rough to read. We get graphic in this one.

Utena bears a certain question at its core, and we see our main characters answer it: “What is incest?”

Utena herself goes “Haha, well, uhmm… what?” and really isn’t in the best place to answer this question just yet. We’ll come back.

Akio and Anthy — City Built of Flesh

It is nearly impossible to get to a concrete truth of just what Akio and Anthy are.

There is something like an obfuscating haze in front of them; a metaphysical veil. Something that keeps us as viewers from getting close enough to really see. Their entire shared being is incredibly ambiguous, with the series presenting consistently contradictory images of and about them that somehow do make sense as part of their whole.

Are they truly ancient royalty, drifting between the eras and oscillating between myth and reality? Are they a formerly normal pair of siblings who simply got too close to the concept of eternity, an eternity ago? Are they immortal, unaging, constantly reinventing themselves in an environment where no one will notice? Are they symbols, archetypes taken physical form? Are they but the most recent mundane heirs to a very long line? Or are they just an ambiguously foreign and influential compulsive liar pedophile and his completely normal fourteen-year-old sister?

There’s a lot of angles to consider. I even quite like that “the Himemiyas are fair folk” theory I saw a post on recently – time and memory dilation, self-contained otherworlds, strange magic – though the whole “ten million tons of raw iron” thing does mess with that a bit, haha.

While this whole question is an interesting one, I don’t know how useful it is. It is the same question as “why are there so many cars coming out of the dueling arena floor,” or “how do people pull swords out of each other’s chests”. It seems almost presumptuous for me to try to rationalize the magic or metaphor of Ohtori, as if it’s something I shouldn’t try to explain fully. Something about it makes me want to just take it at face value, to accept that yes, these two are all of their possibilities at once. Though I tend to err on the side of realism, we can argue from each of these points.

I think the reading of the whole thing I like the most – and this is very subjective – is that they are completely normal people used as an example by a wider mythological force. They are at once the portrait that the world uses to illustrate the concept of love, as well as a more linear, literal long-term abusive couple.

The two were once ostracized and alone. Akio was once younger, and his ideals purer. Anthy cared about him very much, and wanted to protect him even at great cost to herself. The story of the children in the farmhouse is what built Ohtori – the dramatized Tale of the Rose; a story of escaping hatefulness and blame by positioning themselves into power, into definition. That’s all I’m comfortable counting as absolutely sure.

Something happens after this. The swords happen. The death of Dios and the transition to Akio happens. The swords happen. Ohtori happens. The swords continue happening. Years pass.

The duels begin – why?

This is obviously not the first cycle of the duels. Hints are everywhere, from Mikage to Ruka – this has happened before, both decades ago and very recently. Akio has been through this many times; ready and primed to start them up again at the end, even after what Utena had done. Perhaps he did not start the duels; only took over from some forgotten predecessor. And perhaps it really is better to read him as so thoroughly ingrained in this cycle that decades-old involvement is believable.

How old is Anthy? Anthy is fourteen. Anthy is four thousand. Anthy is already long dead. The dress falls to the floor. The dress does not exist. Who are you?

There is almost a sense of concrete timekeeping not really mattering here – that whether it has been six years or six hundred it has been a long time either way – that the damage is damage, and trying to classify it as worse or better one way does it a disservice. No matter the dates of it all, they have both been here a long time, and that is all that matters. Long enough that they have become worn down, used to it, transformed into “willing” parts of it. Anthy believes she is choosing this path because there are no other alternatives; and Akio does not want any of those alternatives. They would complicate his constructed path and its power. Akio grandstands, and feigns control of something he does not want to truly control, only comfortably inhabit.

Akio plays prince, but Anthy does not play princess. Anthy’s state is real. Deep.

If Akio is the End of the World, Anthy is the Heart of the World. The center, the focal point. The world moves from its limits to its heart; moving all things towards its center and preventing escape: Akio directs the hatred of the world, and Anthy collects it within herself. An engine.

Remember – Akio and Anthy are not only the archetype of an abusive relationship; they have become the archetype of the couple. The first prince and princess, the castle and the crown; the very concept of romance at Ohtori is patterned after these two. Theirs is the story that is told. Gender itself is patterned after these two. Everyone else is only grasping at echoes of their example, attempting to recreate it, with varying intents and varying levels of success.

This is, as you can imagine, bad news for nearly everyone. So what is this eternal relationship, this far down the line?

Akio is hard to interpret. Part of me cannot help but interpret him as this overwhelming force, this utter seat of worldly power, which is the exact interpretation he longs for. That power means little, however, in the world of children and easy choreographed adults he has so painstakingly constructed. Again, Akio plays. He plays at spunky heroism in a world he is already the final boss of. He plays at suave romance towards baffled children and drugged women. He plays at power, too.

Akio is the prince of the world. He lives in its tallest place. He occupies its highest seat of power. He drives its fastest car. He is the coolest man in the world. He also literally can’t beat a fourteen-year-old girl in a swordfight – but he can do worse to her.

There is nothing noble or unique in Akio. He has no revolutionary power, he has no magic behind him. None of it is his. He can move nothing forward, only keep things running. Akio is only able to hurt people, and even that is more a symptom of his experience and the naivety of his victims rather than any inherent property in him. He occupies his seat, and does not embody his as Anthy embodies hers.

Anthy knows this. She knows it well. She knows how ridiculous the whole charade is, and even still…

He is the only constant with her. He is, he insists, all she has, and takes away anything that would get in the way of that. He presents himself as something to cling to, and it works. She still remembers his younger self, from when the world seemed new and changing, and often she still loves him. Or at the least, some image of him. Part Stockholm, part lingering familial affection, and part strange nostalgia, despite knowing everything of the world.

Akio may play. The swords don’t play. Regardless of how stupid the situation is, it does not stop. Anthy can’t change things, either – he is still her world, and he is good enough at intimidation and punishment that there’s really no argument to be made. That is what he is good at. Maintenance. Coping. Adaptation. He is the bottleneck of all that holds her in place, but can only channel its greater driving force.

Every one of the thousand swords have been drawn from a human heart, and each has triangulated her image as worthy of contempt, of anger, of hatred. They see echoes of themselves in her. They twist themselves in an effort to identify her as worthy of their hatred. Anthy, the woman in absolute. Her strange and eternal adolescence. The omnipresent hatred the world bears her – for who she is, for what she does, for how her body is made to move.

She has been here so long. She has been through so many cycles. Whether one lifetime or a thousand, she has been ground and polished by the gears of the world as its most integral part. She has taken so much hatred that she has become hateful in turn, and bears her own kind of worldly cruelty bound up in her infinite body. She has given up, and does her part.

But the pain does not fade, no matter how familiar it becomes, no matter how accustomed she becomes to directing it. The pain does not and cannot fade. For she embodies pain, and understands it more thoroughly than anyone alive.

All women – no, no. All girls are like the Rose Bride. Akio sips his cocktail.

Touga and Nanami — Half the Summer’s Night

Nanami Kiryuu is in love with her brother.

I think we need work backwards from this point. Nanami is in love, and we need to understand what kind of love it is, and where it comes from.

Nanami’s love is a cultivated one. One that has been a very long time in formulation. One that has been nudged, encouraged, pruned and pushed for many years. One that has side effects. One that, whatever happened to create it, has given her this drive to abase herself, to fight for tiny tokens of affection and approval. More than anything, she has centered herself around winning some measure of recognition out of Touga’s usually distant exterior, and she has classed this as romantic.

That was probably a bit much to bite off at once. A dramatic assertion. Alright, let’s step back a bit, and work more with some actual examples. I want to start off with a quick thought experiment. Let’s take all of Nanami’s jokey “filler” episodes as seriously as we possibly can.

Nanami overhears a conversation about pesticide and misinterprets it as Touga trying to murder her and make it look like an accident. She unquestioningly accepts this as true for days until it is cleared up, during which time we learn that everyone’s idea of cool and proper brotherly behavior is engineering near-deadly accidents for their siblings as an excuse to rescue them and become closer.

Nanami packs up for India, again without question or hesitation the second Touga tell her to, and is nearly literally killed multiple times in increasingly absurd elephant attacks. (This one is probably the hardest to take seriously)

Nanami gets so excited about a cowbell sold as jewelry that it turns into a full obsession, and then she physically turns into a cow. This is accompanied by vignettes of her being raised like a calf, living in a barn, and eventually being sold, butchered, cooked and eaten by Touga.

Nanami lays an egg and spends an entire episode agonizing over whether this is normal, or if everyone in the school is going to laugh at and isolate her for it until she loses everything. This is again accompanied with imagery of her as a caged animal. She gets extremely attached to it, even after Touga tells her he would hate her and disown her if she were to lay an egg. She then runs to the woods where Saionji is cooking an entire basket of eggs in the middle of the night for some reason, is overjoyed when she finds hers again, and nearly abandons it but ends up coming back. She finds it suddenly giant and hatching, and then wakes up from the dream with her sheets wet with egg yolk.

Where does this anxiety come from? This paranoia, this desperation, this drama? The easiest thing to do is write her off as crazy, a naturally histrionic girl who has always been like this, that it’s just a part of who she is. If this was all she got, it would be so easy to write her off as exclusively comic relief; it would be almost impossible not to. But it isn’t – we see a lot more besides the jokes, and we have to use both of her episode archetypes to learn about the other. We have to ask ourselves, what happened to Nanami? Where does this jumping to conclusions, this fear of people come from? It’s very specific – situations where everyone is turned against her, faceless and laughing, and she isn’t even given the opportunity to speak and defend herself. What does that remind you of?

It looks like two things to me. Firstly it looks a lot like a dramatized version of how Touga treats her – dismissing her concerns, not really listening, chuckling smugly. It also looks a lot like the mob at Anthy’s childhood farmhouse.

But we’ll come to that later. Let’s go back to the cow nonsense. Did you know that Donna Donna is a real folk song?

On a wagon bound for market
there’s a calf with a mournful eye.
High above her there’s a swallow
winging swiftly through the sky.

“Stop complaining,” said the farmer,
“Who told you a calf to be?
Why don’t you have wings to fly with
like the swallow so proud and free?”

Calves are easily bound and slaughtered,
never knowing the reason why.
But whoever treasures freedom
like the swallow has learned to fly.

How the winds are laughing!
They laugh with all their might.
Laugh and laugh the whole day through,
and half the summer’s night.

When I first heard Joan Baez sing this song I was blown away. Like, it floored me, something clicked in my head. Being blamed for your own murder because you were born wrong. The world itself mocking you as you are brought to the end you know awaits you. It is so much more brutal than the truncated Japanese version, and Nanami’s goofy hallucinations of being a farm animal suddenly seem a lot more realistic. Hopelessly calling out to Touga as he sends her off to be sold. Seeing herself as steak on his plate. Never knowing the reason why this fear possesses her, this fear of abandonment, of slaughter.

Nanami Kiryuu is in love with her brother. She has said it so many times that it must be true by now. She doesn’t have to think about it anymore, because if she thinks about it she will have to admit something terrible to herself.

Nanami is lonely. Nanami grew up in a big empty house completely alone except for a few things – her faceless parents, their crowds of guests, and Touga. This was her world. We see in the flashback how the Kiryuu children were treated – Touga dressed up on a little throne, practically displayed to the guests, Nanami scolded bizarrely for doing anything on her own, for playing. The rest of the flashbacks are only Touga and Nanami – always alone together, and even Touga ignores her as a rule. She has to yell for his attention, feels as if it is always in danger of being taken away without warning. The only times we see Touga really be nice to Nanami is when she is already hurt in some way – crying after being scolded, crying after nearly being charged by a bull, worried sick over his injury.

Nanami is desperate to be loved, but I can’t see it as genuinely, originally romantic. I see a lonely girl who really wants a family who pays attention to her, who really wants a brother who likes her even outside of crises, who really wants to stop being ignored and left in the corner. It is not, properly, that Nanami is in love with her brother, only that she loves him despite everything. So where do the tones of romance come in?

Touga, we see, is very good at reading people – at least for his age. He’s not on 4D chess Akio levels, and does get flustered or caught off guard sometimes, but he’s very good at steering people towards certain things – lies of omission, subtle implications, grand insistences, among other strategies. His hinting at being Utena’s prince and his total failure to ever remind her about the night in the church, his little speeches about throbbing engines and cracking eggs that seem to be able to sweep absolutely anyone up in their currents. Touga is good at manipulation – he’s really good. I think this is a sign he’s been doing it for a long time, especially when compared to Saionji, who so desperately wants to be at that level but can still only muster brute force and yelling.

I’m certain he learned this through Nanami. I’m certain that all his little touches we see – the looking into her eyes a second too long, the half-jokes about kissing with a second too much of suggestive silence before the “just kidding”, the displaying his other relationships because he knows it will make her jealous – have been going on a long time, and are what made Nanami so utterly convinced that her feelings are romantic.

But they’re not. She has been taught, subtly and over a period of years, that feelings of affection and loneliness and emotional investment have no other outlet besides romantic, that there is no other way for her to be loved except in that context. So, being as young as she was, she accepts that as the way the world works – or at least the way she works. Of course it’s normal to be like this, her and Touga are special and always have been. And then begins the long building-up of her position, her feelings, into this thing that her life centers around. She is not allowed to have anything else.

I’m also sure that despite all this, she’s still not fully aware of what that romance would mean for her. It is a goal, but a not well understood one – almost similar to Akio’s “okay so we’ll both just go vaguely hang out in the castle forever, duh” offer to Utena at the end. I also don’t think it is at all a stretch to say that the girl who immediately assumes she will be jailed for being a space alien upon getting her first period might not exactly have the strongest grasp on what sex actually entails. Her anticipated eventual romance with Touga is strange – I don’t know what she expects to happen, or if she’s even thought about it that far; just that the journey there is the important part. The whole “like a dog chasing a car” joke.

This also puts into context her “I know where those feelings lead!” outburst in her last duel – she really didn’t know, until seeing how graphically and deeply it has hurt Anthy before her. And…

A lot of people read Touga as not really caring about Nanami in a lot of ways – he’s nowhere near as visibly and vocally interested in her as she is in him, and the whole culmination of her arc is him going on about he could never even like a girl like her, that they only speak to each other due to growing up together. That Nanami immediately believes this is also one of those worrying signs I mentioned.

The truth is, I think, extremely far from this. I think Touga is the one more invested in their relationship being romantic and sexual. It’s just that his attraction to her takes a different form.

Touga idealizes Nanami, just more quietly than she does him. All his sleeping around with the student body seems so shallow, so transient – he knows girls are into him for a large part because of his status. His reputation, his name, his position, his put-on charm. But Nanami knows him, really knows him, and hasn’t left yet. She sees something in him that the other girls don’t – something about him as a person, something about their shared history. This would normally be normal familial love, but with Touga it can’t be. He takes her for granted, and builds her up in his head as such.

But then there’s the other side of it, the worse side.

He likes playing with her, messing with her, watching her drive herself crazy. It not that he doesn’t like her, it’s that he likes her better crying and confused. There is this relishing of it – always bringing her back to that, never actually resolving anything, continuing to string her along with promises and tactically deployed affection – we don’t see this level of things even from Akio, who seems to treat the whole thing as a job instead of recreation. Touga’s smirks, his theatrics, his relishing of her suffering is apparent in most of their more important interactions. It’s chilling.

Again, the whole “more romantic” line after her last duel tells us very clearly in which direction Touga has been building this relationship. He knows what he is doing, and has for a long time.

But even before her last duel we saw this. The car.

Touga ultimately outright rapes Nanami. I want to make it really clear that I cannot in good conscience interpret what happened any other way. I want to, I want to read Touga more charitably than I do, but I can’t justify it.

We never explicitly see what happens in the car aside from the first forced kiss, but we do know it does not stop. Given everything else we know concretely about the context – who Akio is, what Touga wants, what the car represents – that is, for me, enough to be nearly certain. The car smashes through the glass walls of the house, and it does not stop, and the next day she appears in the arena sitting on its hood. She is different. Colder. Angrier. Brittle. Still both a bit lost and terribly aware of where she is at once. Touga pulls a sword from her chest and she spends the duel screaming.

It is a testament to Touga’s cruelty that he waits until Nanami is already exposed to the truth of Akio and Anthy’s relationship, to Anthy blank-eyed, out of her body, motionless and naked on the couch, until he makes his first explicit move on her. It is a testament to just how calculated his behavior is that he could have done this at any time before that, and she would have not objected so strongly. The entire couple episodes leading up to this is a long, orchestrated effort to confuse her, to play with her heart, to bring her to a certain place – the posing in the shower, the lying to her about the most important thing to her in her life, the theft of her transfer application. It is an effort to make her want in, and then want out, and then to finally tell her the central lie of Ohtori against a backdrop of its appropriate weight:

There is no way out. The car will not stop, even when you say no. You are not the one it listens to. This is the world, and I am its limits. You will still be here in the morning, in the afternoon, and in all the nights to come.

But she doesn’t accept so easily. Even the morning after, “I’m not like her!” in that she has not yet resigned herself as Anthy has.

“I want to surpass everything. To surpass my brother, to surpass the person I was.” The threat has not worked. It has only disillusioned her.

In all the scenes Nanami is present in after her final duel, she does not mention or speak directly to her brother once. She does not look at him. I know we’re talking about like, three or four scenes here, but it’s a genuinely striking difference compared to her former self.

Nanami never actually wanted what she thought she did. She wanted belonging, family, love, sure. Yes. Very, very much. But that’s not what she thought she wanted; it is not what she had been taught to want, and it’s not what Touga eventually forced upon her.

So at the end of the series she steps out of her long and fabricated love, and into a more uncertain space.

Akio and Touga — Masks Made of Words

The first thing a bunch of people are going to have to say about this section is “what about Touga’s dad,” so I’m going to address that before moving on to what I actually want to say. While, again, I’m not factoring anything from the movie into the bases of my real arguments, it’s important to mention in this case.

In Adolescence of Utena, Touga is raped by his father as a child. He also dates Utena Tenjou for some period before arbitrarily drowning in a river for her, sleeps with Shiori as a ghost, and this time around does not in fact assault his sister after several years of intensive grooming, due to being dead. He never comes in contact with the even more ridiculous movie version of Akio either, who is also a bumbling caricature that barely resembles his real self. Movie Touga bears more in common with manga Touga – wistful, unambiguously knightly, and mostly well-intentioned – than he ever does with series Touga. I don’t believe the two are reconcilable as the same character.

Let’s look back on the show’s treatment of parental figures. We have Touga and Nanami’s parents briefly say a line each during a single scene of a long flashback. We see Miki and Kozue’s dad once, from behind, on the phone, and their stepmother only mentioned. We see the silent coffins of Utena’s parents, and… correct me if I’m wrong, but that’s it. Aside from that one scene Mrs. Ohtori is in, we are given an outright Peanuts-style view of what should be the most significant adults in the series.

Parents are almost never in the picture. When they are, they are only tangentially mentioned as a piece of backstory meant to help us understand the corresponding sibling relationship better. Only an excuse to plot a source of drama between Miki and Kozue, or Touga and Nanami… in fact, aside from Utena and Kanae, who are rather uniquely positioned characters, the only time parents are ever even mentioned is in relation to siblings. Juri, Saionji, Ruka, Shiori, Wakaba, Tsuwabuki, Keiko, etc. – we never hear a word about theirs. Sure, that might be just because our more central characters get more exposition attention, but it’s still conspicuous.

Everything comes down to an echelon of peers. Akio is the single adult in Ohtori allowed to be active or relevant in the lives of its students.

For movie Touga’s father to be so central to his character, for him to actually have a face, is absolutely unprecedented, and just does not mesh with the worldview of the series. I can’t square it, and am going to ignore it completely. Besides, Touga in the series already has someone who fills the role of sexually intrusive paternal figure central to his character, in a more thematically coherent way – Akio Ohtori.

The second thing a bunch of people are going to have to say about this section is “Touga is also a victim of Akio’s,” which is like, partially true, and also partially irrelevant? Discussing their relationship demands discussing the morals of complicity more than anywhere else – it is hard to parse as an instance of un-nuanced abuse when it is also a relationship about teaching abusive behaviors, which Touga takes to naturally and beyond enthusiastically.

The most sympathetic Touga ever is is at the beginning, where we know nothing about him, and at the end, where he seems to come to care for Utena a bit and to start being a bit suspicious of Akio. The end is also where he starts screaming about desperately wanting Akio’s power, which makes the previous suspicion seem more like jealousy. Likewise, his feelings for Utena seem like something in the same way; a kind of burgeoning possessiveness. Sure, as he is right now he can make the girls fawn over him, but Akio can make them love him – he’s only even been able to do the same with Nanami, and in his pursuit of Akio’s power has begun to want more. Utena here is a shared target more than she is a real romantic interest.

If Touga has been conditioned into desiring Akio’s position in the same way that he has conditioned Nanami, what does that mean? Does it make his motivations better, his actions more excusable? How much does coercion matter here, how much does it count, when he throws himself into what it asks so eagerly? You can answer that any way you want, but I know which direction I’m seeing it from. Touga could be a dumb, impressionable kid subtly betrayed by an authority figure, but he still does what he does.

Touga is shown the true nature of the planetarium projector. Touga is shown the true nature of the rose bride. Touga is not only shown the true nature of End of the World; he leads other people to it, and is the primary agent in doing so – can you hear it? And still he participates in the months-long effort to get his own sister into the car, and is unbothered enough by this to call it romantic after the fact.

Could Touga be a decent person if removed from Akio’s influence? Maybe, if he is the type of person to want to step away from it in the first place. But even with full knowledge of what it is, and the power to leave, he shows no signs of considering it. At best, he does want to be rid of Akio – but only in order to take his place.

Why does Akio single Touga out? How did they get so close?

I previously called their relationship an apprenticeship, and I stand by that. Akio has seen something in Touga, something that he hasn’t seen in any of our other characters, and has chosen to induct him into his own ways.

There’s something special about Touga, since the beginning. He is always the last, capstone duel of a given arc. He is the only guy Utena even glances at outside of Akio. He and Akio have so much in common, too. Both suave and (relatively) older guys in positions of (relative) power, both egregious playboys with a taste for the finer points of female suffering, both putting on a tormented savior front to get away with things. Touga having an adoring little sister is really just the cherry on top, the final seal of why Akio sees an echo of his own power in Touga.

And he’s not wrong. From Nanami’s very first duel onwards, it became clearer and clearer to me the extent of Touga’s strange cruelty. It is both the same and different as Akio’s brand; moving through the same channels of power, but coming from a different personal history.

What is the difference between Touga and Akio? We might find out by looking a bit backwards.

Akio and Anthy built Ohtori out of desperation – two kids ran from a world trying to kill them, and while still too young to understand what they meant by it asked, “what is a world that could hold us?” Akio and Anthy were changed – Dios was indeed a different person; there is a muddying of the waters in terms of blame and responsibility. Akio certainly chose his own cruelty, but it was a decision he was pushed to.

Touga and Nanami, having been born into the world of Ohtori, are somewhat different. They have inherited the dynamic – the world has already been built to teach it, and welcome them into it. Touga’s cruelty manifested early – there was never a period of differently-named nobility with him. He has chosen to carry the same cruelty simply because he could. Touga did not have to fight or suffer for his position; it was already waiting for him.

Through this, we can come to a conclusion: the world gets worse. A feedback loop of empowerment and normalization of this type of predation; always allowing itself to become more entrenched and more outright violent, always tending towards the same symbolic markers of its power. One that supports itself, nurtures further manifestations of itself, and spreads ever outward like a stain.

The third thing a bunch of people are going to have to say about this section is, “Wait, okay – why would Akio be interested in passing on his power at all? He can’t possibly have any intention of abdicating his position, right? He’s far too arrogant and secure for that, what are you talking about?”

I can’t answer this yet, not here. However, I will return to it at the very end of this essay series, as it is the question the future of Ohtori hinges upon.

Anthy and Nanami — The Little Lower Road

I’ve heard the phrase “illusion of complicity” come up several times in various writings on sibling incest, and I think that concept deserves some discussion. There is a special sense of “being part of it,” that often gets thrown at victims – it shows up in all types of abuse, to some extent, but sibling incest gives it an environment to uniquely thrive in. It’s a combination of several things – sibling abuse being written off as kids messing around, a pre-existing social expectation of familial love and certain types of affection, and a more peer-like relationship that, when compared to parental incest or other instances of pedophilia with a larger age gap, makes the power dynamic seem, to some, less strong or not at all present.

More importantly than other people’s views on it, this illusion rubs off on the victims themselves. These same messages are thrown at them – “you made this happen”, “you let this happen”, “you wanted this, didn’t you?”, “this is a secret kept by both of us.” This is exacerbated by the natural closeness of the childhood sibling relationship; when both parties are still living together, often in an environment where the abuse happens because it goes unnoticed or ignored by parents anyway.

Utena presents this illusion – both sides of it – very strongly, and especially towards and between Nanami and Anthy.

It influences the audience first – an audience more likely to ridicule Nanami for the very symptoms of her abuse, an audience more likely to blame Anthy for staying. Nanami is practically begging for it, right? Like, come on, look at her. Anthy chooses to take the swords, from the very beginning, right? Doesn’t she maintain that even at the end?

This illusion is very much a tool. It manifests itself in the word “witch” – a contempt for the damaged-undamaged feminine, a willingness to hate the victim before critically examining the pristine mask of the perpetrator. A desire to cover it up, to not speak of it so candidly, to deny its existence and impact – even when, as we’ve seen, the world of Ohtori has literally constructed its image of love itself around it.

It is a silencing. It is a compartmentalizing. A quiet channel that victims are asked to move through; a kind of bearing-things we see in both Anthy and Nanami. The little lower road.

The sisters are made to carry everything – to be an anchor, a rock, a place to tether to while their brothers use the world as a playground. A dead cat, two distant parents, a stifled and blind longing shaking at its own admission. An angry village, the guilt that comes with years of helping to manipulate others, ten million swords. Duty. To settle down, and to not cry.

But this illusion also manifests between the two of them, and this, perhaps, is where it hurts most. Because for that resentment to exist means that both of them have bought into it already, and have turned it against both themselves and each other.

The first thing we notice about Anthy and Nanami is that they absolutely hate each other.

Nanami is suspicious and aggressive towards pretty much any girl that gets close to Touga. However, her dislike for Anthy is strangely intense. She truly goes above and beyond in her cruelty towards and anxiety around Anthy, even while Utena emerges as the more properly threatening figure. Why this singling-out?

Before we get into it, I think its worth it to mention that this is a bit hard to judge– our only real counterexamples of girls who get too close to Touga are Utena, Keiko, and all the girls on the phone, so it’s not the easiest thing to determine a standard of. I’m seeing this kind of public humiliation and light physical violence as exceptional, but she does something similar to Keiko later on. Maybe it’s Utena that’s the exception here – she gets angry at Utena, but never actually lashes out at her that much. Just something to think about.

If her targeting of Anthy is exceptional (and I do think it is; the lengths Nanami goes to here truly are unique) then we also need to figure out why. What does she see in Anthy?

I’m not sure there’s an easy answer here. I’m not sure Nanami herself knows. I think it’s based on not understanding her – Anthy doesn’t act like other girls she knows; she is threatening despite her innocent and peaceful exterior, and Nanami can’t tell why. Something bothers her – she knows she’s missing something, she sees how Anthy looks at her, and she doesn’t know what it means. Part of it must be an effort to understand, to finally grasp what her deal is. The weirdo who seems so much older than she is, who is always totally unfazed by even the worst she’s able to throw at her.

And then there’s the big reveal. As Nanami’s world is collapsing around her, the final blow is seeing why, in the end, Anthy is like that. It is unreasonable to expect that Nanami understands everything the moment she sees Anthy on the couch, but something does click in her, and continues clicking through the time leading up to her last duel.

Nanami finally understands the “growing up too fast” thing; she finds herself suddenly doing the same in real time. At the very end, Nanami clings to a sort of disgust towards Anthy, and what she has “done”. We have to remember this is less than a day after the car – I think this disgust is directed towards herself as well, even primarily, for letting the same thing happen to herself.

While Nanami tends to wear her feelings towards Anthy on her sleeve – fear, suspicion, jealousy, and eventually a kind of wounded, half-admitted understanding – how Anthy sees Nanami is more ambiguous.

I’ve seen something of a consensus that Anthy seriously hates and resents Nanami. That Anthy carries a shallow grudge for being bullied or whatever, and is the one behind all Nanami’s slapstick filler misfortune. Something about Anthy’s witchlike control over animals and Nanami’s kitten guilt, something about hidden channels of power. Anthy supposedly gets back at Nanami quietly, and relishes it.

Can we really say that? Saionji, Touga, maybe even Miki – none of them get turned into cows or thrown off cliffs, and arguably do much worse to her. Is Anthy really so petty to lash out at the weakest of her aggressors just because that’s the only place she can? Is that what we want to believe of her?

I can’t see this as just middle-school payback stuff. Nanami’s animal issues are years old at this point, and Anthy doubtlessly sees something else in Nanami besides generic bully: just as Akio sees himself in Touga, Anthy sees herself in Nanami. And while Akio sees a unique opportunity to extend his power, Anthy sees only insult.

Nanami must have felt like a cruel joke. Like watching a mockery of herself that happened to be a living, breathing, profoundly naïve girl. Anthy watches Nanami make all of her own old mistakes. Anthy watches her pose in caricatures of her own movements. It is a brutal reminder. “This is where I came from? This is what I was once like?” As if… as if…

Anthy already knew what was going to happen. She knew since the day Akio told her to plant the yellow roses. An idiot, blind little girl chasing her brother until the end, until the End of the World showed itself. She knew the whole story, just as she knew where all the other blind children were being led. It was only that she knew this story more intimately. She let her hands move. Maybe in Anthy’s lashing out in the more slapstick episodes there was even a sense of deterrence, of trying to drive Nanami away from her obsession. A kind of “Do you think this hurts? You better be ready for more.”

I think that there’s also a sadness there. Maybe even a jealousy. To see someone so obliviously love in the same way that she once loved – Nanami worrying in the corners of the mansion, Anthy worrying in the corners of the farmhouse. Anthy just doesn’t know how to deal with her. Looking at her hurts.

If there is one complaint I have about Utena as a show it is that Anthy and Nanami never get to truly reconcile. I think we come close – the glances they shoot each other in the chairman’s residence and in the arena mark a quiet understanding between them – but they still push each other away at the end. It couldn’t be any other way, really, but I still miss that final touch.

I think Anthy is almost glad to be hated by Nanami. Glad to see Nanami finally reject the image of incest, and see its horrible truth. At the same time it must hurt to see someone so like her be hurt in turn, and turn away. Anthy is here, and always will be. The best she can hope for is to see her state not be replicated so thoroughly.

Someone who we haven’t discussed yet has an issue with that.

Notes: In the next part we’ll be getting into Miss Tenjou herself, especially in relation to the often forgotten Kanae. Within these two we see a parallel gear in the mechanism of Ohtori – not so integral as what we’ve previously went over, but crucial nonetheless. Looking back from previous duel cycles to the very end of the series, we’ll find out just how Utena has managed to strike at and destabilize this seemingly unshakable edifice of institutional, cyclical child abuse – and why she was there in the first place.

Through sheer stubborn and reckless heroism, Utena does something that no one else has been able to imagine.

Chapter 4: part three, deer in the works

(See the end of the chapter for notes.)

We’re all familiar with the classical triad of Ohtori: the prince, the princess, and the witch.

It doesn’t take a genius to figure this out. The prince is the ideal male role, the princess the ideal female role, and the witch the vilified female role. While men who don’t quite fit the prince role can be somewhat looked down upon, there is always a way back for them, and half-assed princes usually get celebrated anyway. On the other hand, the witch role is explicitly a tool to punish women with: a bad end, a final boss, a figure that demands revulsion. There’s no vilified male role outside of “too distant from a prince”, and this doesn’t come with many consequences – but witches are absolute exiles. We know the story.

But it’s more complicated than that. This is more than just a cautionary tale, far more subtle than surface-level gender roles. The prince, princess, and witch make up a lens that Ohtori classifies all people through, and it begins to illustrate the process of how the place works.

Namely, Ohtori pits women against each other: princesshood is a competition. There are two opposed models of the princess that must win out against the other to prove that they are worthy of the position. Each are told that they are the princess and that the other is the witch, forming a feedback cycle of guilt, blame, and desperation which can only be escaped by recognizing it as a rigged system.

There are, after all, two ways to be a princess. Either be a prince’s sister, or marry him.

As the Himemiyas establish, the brother-sister image of royalty and romance came first. Incest remains the primal and exemplary story here; the world is built around it. However, it’s also untenable: abusive and sterile by nature, unavoidably violent, and unsustainable. While the harmful aspects of it have never much mattered to those who have built it, it cannot replicate itself on its own terms. So the creation of a second image of royalty and romance was necessary as a way to spread the archetype further: the husband and wife.

In the previous part, we’ve gone in-depth into the sister-princess archetype, as seen in Anthy and Nanami. Here, we’ll get into the wife-princess, as seen in Kanae and Utena – namely, examining its relationship with the general hierarchy of Ohtori, its purpose as a necessary but secondary counterpart structure to the sibling relationship, and most crucially, how it reveals the true purpose of the dueling cycle itself.

Ohtori is a machine of hierarchy.

Ohtori is built in concentric circles. Emanating out from the prince-princess image of the perfect couple, people arrange themselves based on proximity and accuracy to what the world asks of them. Power and position in Ohtori is directly tied to how faithfully you reproduce the image – more than just conformity, this ties into birth as well as behavior.

In our previous post, we went over the relationships between the five characters most central to Ohtori’s world. I think with that as a passable baseline, we can begin to piece together what the structure of Ohtori is, and how it works:

Ohtori is a power structure made to facilitate gendered abuse. Using sibling incest as its central ideal of gender and romance, as embodied in the prince and princess narrative, it ensures that relationships emulating this ideal will always tend towards coercion and inescapability. This is the center of Ohtori’s power, its summit – enshrined in its highest places and most cherished ruling mythology.

But Ohtori is, of course, more than its center. It is an entire world, and organized in a specific way. It’s classes of people are positioned closer to or further way from power depending on how faithfully they replicate the core narrative, and it turns out we can map this out rather easily.

Akio and Anthy are of course the closest circle, if not the central point itself, and Touga and Nanami occupy the second rung. Their prestigious family is the closest modern analogue we have to royalty; and their mansion the closest thing to a castle. Touga is the most visibly powerfully positioned person in the school sphere of Ohtori as president, a role that Nanami also fills later on, in much the same way that Anthy ends up doing so much of Akio’s dirty work. There’s a direct inheritance between the two pairs.

Further outward it gets more ambiguous. Saionji is a bit of an exception, riding off of Touga’s power but having nothing of it for himself outside of proximity. He is beginning to get a grasp on male violence and possessiveness, but does not have a definitive relationship of his own – he instead misguidedly pursues Anthy, mistaking himself as having a much higher position than he does. Touga mimics Akio, and Saionji mimics Touga, and while Saionji is still able to channel power effectively in this way, he does not come closer to the core. He is a ronin prince, as it were – a powerfully positioned man who reproduces and enforces the world, but is not at all integral to it.

The Kaorus are closer – the third tier after the Himemiyas and Kiryuus. Weirdly possessive unhealthy siblings who haven’t fully made the jump to romance, but who have certainly begun moving towards it. They’re probably the hardest to classify – them being twins makes the age-based power dynamic not as present even though Miki is technically older, and Miki’s cavalier obliviousness combined with Kozue’s clingy jadedness make for a relationship we don’t have a direct analogue for in the way that the other two families have in each other. Miki just barely begins to mimic Touga in the same way Saionji does; only enough for him to somewhat entitledly idolize Anthy, but without the direct violence of the others. Kozue, noticing this, tries to bring him back – a jealous girl trying to weaponize jealousy – by being as promiscuous as a thirteen-year-old can be in an attempt to regain his attention. Should she succeed, should they truly recognize each other as romantic interests, they will be even closer to the center than they now are.

The Juri triangle is even further out – a confused, insecure imitation of princeliness that just can’t fit due to the gender factor. You have a boy and two girls, pining and fidelity issues, scheming and hatred, the whole thematic suite… but none of them are related, Juri is vehemently disinterested in men, and Shiori ranks with Touga as one of the cruelest manipulators in the show. Ruka tries to salvage the whole mess with the same doomed savior shtick, but cannot, because he has no idea what he is doing, nor understands at all the people he is trying to do it with. They simply cannot reach the ruling myth of Ohtori, they don’t fit, and they fall apart.

Together with Saionji, these three form up a kind of fourth tier, made up of powerfully positioned people aspiring to that same ideal of unhealthy romance, but who cannot by nature fully embody it in the way that people with siblings can.

And then, out from the rich kids of the Council, we come to the fifth tier: the insects.

Tsuwabuki, whose weird vicarious sister complex has managed to get him very close to understanding how the world works, in the blind, amateurish, and nontrivially cruel manner that only children can achieve. Keiko, desperately shooting for the wife-princess role without having been chosen, out of time and out of luck. Wakaba and Tatsuya, heirs of the Onion Kingdom, whose lack of blood ties doom them to being forgotten both by each other and by the narrative – even worse, it dooms them to an inconsistent color scheme. All the less-specials are scrounging to find a place, to build their own mythology or worm their way into a preexisting one. Some of them begin to succeed, and get their own short stories, and some of them fail, and join the nameless crowds.

The ideal is passed down to everyone, preemptively ruining even what could have been healthy relationships. Every girl in the world is a princess, and all their kingdoms fell at birth.

The idea of “specialness” that Wakaba articulates turns out to be a direct cipher for “proximity to incest” – the crown jewel in the romance that incest attempts to build for itself is the idea of eternity, and eternity is nothing but stagnation, stasis, lack of change. Specialness is absolutely nothing but how faithfully one perpetuates dead-end image of the world Akio holds so dear, and love is not allowed to exist outside of its role as a tool towards this end.

So few people actually fit into either side of the narrative. Perhaps no one does. Having been based on a primal conception of abuse, romance by the rules can do nothing but ruin people. There is no castle, in the end. There are no princes in the world. The narrative uses raw sexual violence as we see in the car scenes, but it also uses guilt and feelings of being wrong to push people to that point. If you can’t fit into it, you deserve the suffering that causes – and no one can fit into it. This baseline of guilt and self-resentment makes deliberate manipulation a lot easier, and is precisely the objective of the entire arrangement.

Even though no one can fit perfectly, everyone tries. Everyone aims high, and hurts each other in similar ways. Stemming from the images of sister-princess and wife-princess, we see two parallel processes of subjugation that crop up everywhere, independent of their root myths. It is the existence of two kinds of female specialness – the inherent and the aspirational, groomed by the same source into an approximation of love, and pitted against each other instead of said source.

This is also why incest remains a taboo in Ohtori. The origins of its power must be obfuscated; a baseline of hatred must be established. The wife-princess is the image most presented to the world because it is more widely accessible, while the sister-princess is a secret, deeper counterpart, whose wider admission would undermine the systems of blame and persecution the system relies upon. If incest was admitted to be normal, it could not be sold as special, and neither would it be so easy to obscure how universally and incredibly damaging it is.

There is the specialness of being born special, and the specialness of being chosen by the special – and both types of specialness are lies. They are isolation tactics. “You aren’t like those swarms of insects,” Touga says to Nanami, and then turns around and says the exact same thing to Keiko. They are each convinced that if they do not have that specialness – a specialness that is always romantic – they are nothing. They are witches. They fall, and will never be allowed back. The world rejects them from romance, while still insisting that that romance is the only path to any kind of love; to any safety or belonging or happiness. So they fight, they scrounge, they rush towards the prince – the same source that has engineered the entire thing, who they are made to trust and strive to be worthy of, instead of recognizing him for what he is.

All love at Ohtori is meant to be founded on some type of coercion. If too many people were to see the truth of that, why, the world would have a revolution on its hands.

So it stages one, instead.

The people of Ohtori yearn for freedom. The suffering is palpable. Everyone knows something is wrong, but also knows to hide it – because it must just be their own issue, right? It must be unique to them, since everyone else seems so fine. Kozue is constantly frustrated and desperate, Miki constantly confused and panicking; Nanami is bursting with anxiety and resentment, and Touga probably is too. Juri has been miserable for years, and Shiori has managed to lie to herself so intensely about how she isn’t miserable that it transforms into a perverse joy. Utena feels totally out of place, having to force the prince act to center herself and keep herself from having to think so much. Saionji is still a pouty, hopelessly angry boy who only knows how to lash out.

Everyone is miserable, because of the place the world has brought them to. All this pain is focused on a sense of not fitting a narrative that no one can actually fit, but that everyone else seems to. And to all these sad, upset, and very much illusioned kids, a way out is presented. Served up on a platter.

Bring the world revolution. Duel, and claim the Bride.

What does that mean? When the duelists say that phrase, what do they mean by it? Presumably, the power that sleeps within Anthy is capable of reforming the entire world, of making anything happen. You had only need choose what shape it would take.

What would that shape be? If any of our characters were to suddenly gain that power, what would they do with it?

  • Miki reclaims his shining thing – maybe Anthy, but by the end of the series, more likely Kozue.
  • Juri finds the miracle of Shiori returning her love, and they not only know but accept the other’s feelings.
  • Saionji finds eternity in some weird idea of Anthy he had, and gets to hang out happily with Touga again.
  • Nanami erases all her insecurity and gets to be fully accepted, acknowledged, and loved by Touga again.
  • Touga cements his position, and completely assumes Akio’s role – with all that implies both for himself and for Nanami.

You’ll notice that each of these is, properly, a revolution. But they are revolutions that maintain the entire structure, the entire logic of power, and change nothing but the prince and princess seat – changing the aesthetic definition of love, but reinstituting all the harm it causes and evil it works in. It is a slight redirection of the swords – they never leave.

The duel cycle pushes all aware of it into becoming more entrenched into their own problems, instead of looking to heal, or grow, or understand. An easy solution is presented: just make this one romance work, and everything will be fine. Though Akio has no intention of changing the world, he has built a world that many are desperate to change, and the dueling cycle is a promise of such power. A promise of escape; a promise, towards children, of a kind of adulthood that requires no real growth. The dueling cycle being open-ended keeps people in line, as it were – keeps them dreaming of a way out of the world, with the terms set by the world itself.

But already we start to run into problems. Akio would never allow any of these to actually happen – they all challenge his position, and the mythology he has built. No matter how exactly they are convinced to aspire to his vision, since they do not fully fit, a revolution fomented by any of them would be disastrous to him. Each potential revolution unseats him.

So the duels are not a real option. He doesn’t let them be, and turns them into a false and insurmountable promise. He has a failsafe killswitch in the Dios apparition, which serves to keep people both fighting and losing. Nothing can stand up to it.

The truth is that the duels can’t be won. It’s not a competition; the duels aren’t made to be won. No one, in the end, has access to the fabled and foundational concept of eternity besides siblings, and even then it is an empty promise. Despite this, there always is a champion to act as an agent of this prevention, for Dios to work through: the girl who eventually arrives at the duel called Revolution.

And it is always a girl. It is always an aspiring princess, bearing a white rose and a cherished memory. A girl Akio selects by hand and heavily influences in childhood to be devoted to him. A girl who grows their entire life around the memory of him, a girl who defines herself by him. A girl like… a girl like… oh, who am I thinking of, who of our cast could possibly fit this description??? A girl like, like… ah! It’s obvious, isn’t it?

A girl like Kanae Ohtori.

The very first shot we see of Kanae, she is framed by pink roses. Not yellow or orange or black – pink. The only time we ever see pink roses outside of this is in association with Utena, and only at her worst moments of weakness: dueling in a girl’s uniform, wearing the Rose Bride dress. Akio holds a pink rose as he thinks of her, just before the black rose arc. Kanae’s theme of associated colors is bizarre, more varied than any other character – purple, pink, white, yellow, orange – and there’s probably even more to dig out of that.

Kanae is actually engaged to Akio – the “maybe” situation referenced over and over again in Utena’s fairytale flashbacks. Kanae is exactly where Utena has for so long wanted to be, and Utena doesn’t quite know how to react to that.

Kanae is apparently still in high school, yet no one really discusses or mentions her. Not even Touga, closest in age to her, who definitely should have been exposed to her at some point given his closeness to Akio. Utena worrying about Kanae is the context we most frequently hear about her in; everyone else basically ignores her… almost as if they have begun to slowly forget she exists in the first place.

Kanae has a surprisingly complex relationship with Anthy, that doesn’t make sense short term. How long have they known each other? Anthy isn’t usually this cold to people she has just met, and Kanae seems disproportionately upset at the rather mundane slights Anthy has committed against her. The other black roses get huge, life-defining issues dredged up in the elevator – Kozue’s brother complex, Wakaba’s long resentment, Tsuwabuki’s pubescent desperation, Shiori’s cruel obsession. Kanae gets “a girl I apparently barely know won’t think of me as family yet and isn’t that familiar with fashion and omigod she LOOKS at me weird”. There are two conclusions to be drawn here: either that Kanae is just naturally flighty and prone to overreaction, or that her relationship with Anthy is life-defining in the standard black rose way, but we can’t quite see why.

Kanae is the single black rose duelist who fights with her own sword. With that final piece of heavy implication, everything becomes clear. In Kanae, we see what the duels are meant to produce. What choice the duels are meant to force.

When Utena arrives at Ohtori, a dueling cycle has completed. A champion has been chosen. Kanae Ohtori reached the duel called Revolution, and when the dress appeared on her, she gave up the sword and accepted her absolute destiny. She accepted the proposal, became a princess, and claimed the fabled eternity that Akio had promised her.

It turns out “eternity” lasts, like, a year or two? Three, at best? Until he gets tired of you, or you get too old? Too old here being, of course, eighteen. A star only fades as it ages, after all.

We come back to the beginning. We are, at this point, familiar with the nature of the sister-princess, and now through Kanae we begin to understand that of the wife-princess. There have been many like Kanae, but only one Anthy.

The shape of Ohtori begins to take form. Two parallel processes of subjugation. The little sister and the child wife both groomed by the same person into an approximation of love, and then played off each other to keep them from finding a way out of it. As it turns out, the power structure is somehow a fraction worse and more intricate than just plain incest – it is not only a brother married to his sister, he also cheats on her.

As mentioned before, the “split-princess model” includes, by necessity, both sister and wife as complementary but inimical classes. Akio has his Kanaes and Touga has his Keikos, but both are transient compared to Anthy and Nanami’s permanence. The sister-princess is presented as a part of the mechanism, its definition, while the wife-princess is only its utilitarian sustaining fuel.

The point of the duels is not to determine a victor. It is all an elaborate stage play, an act, an excuse to ask and answer one question: “what is a princess?” Each of the two girls in the running are told different stories.

Anthy is told, “You are special. Nothing has changed, this one is too a failure. Knowing everything of this world, I still love only you, and as long as we have each other we can make it through our shared suffering. Don’t think about the other girl. She’ll be gone again soon, like all the others. Allow her this bit of comfort, before she becomes a witch in her turn. Give me the sword, princess.”

And the champion, be it Kanae or Utena, is told, “You are special. You have a story, and a destiny. You have overcome so much, and proved your worth, and now are welcomed to the center of worldly power. You can stop fighting now. Don’t think about the other girl. She’s just the background. She wants to be where she is, as a witch. Give me the sword, princess.”

And they both do, and the other swords come, and another revolution of Ohtori’s central gear completes and clicks into place.

Why does Kanae accept this offer, and Utena does not? What is the difference between them?

First off, I don’t think we can blame Kanae too much. We can’t condemn her too strongly for the choice she made. How much of a choice was it? When we look into what led up to it, this narrative and experience so utterly controlled by Akio, the incredible currents of power winding completely through it – how could you not make that choice? How could you not retreat to comfortable ignorance after seeing that? High-schoolers are easy to lie to. Girls’ hearts are easy to play with. Kanae is just the latest in a likely incredibly long list of girls claimed by the same process, and we cannot name them as fully complicit in a system that deliberately warps their will to its ends.

Of course it is wrong to submit to someone like Akio. Of course it is wrong to abandon Anthy to her fate. We can also say it is wrong to assist in a bank robbery, but with the gun held to your head and the other tellers bleeding out, are you really going to be a hero? There is a right answer here, but it is hundreds of times harder to make in a situation hundreds of times worse than an isolated heist. The duels bring you to the very heart of the world, and to not look away is unbearable.

Very, very few people walk away from Omelas. This only magnifies the city’s cruelty, and does not serve to excuse it.

Kanae has settled, like so many countless women in history have been made to settle before her. She has accepted the arranged marriage, chosen to make the best of the situation, chosen to selectively turn a blind eye to what happens under her own roof. This by its nature continues to enable and support Akio’s predation – but he’s still hurting her, too. It may be miles better than the swords, but it is still an empty life. She never really talks to Akio, she is just another nameless piece – far from the ideal of mutually inhabiting the castle, the most Kanae is allowed is occasional and heavily drugged staged makeout sessions while otherwise being ignored by Akio in favor of his sister and other, younger girls.

This is the role of the wife-princess. To sustain the line, not to bear the full brunt of its violence. Again: two parallel processes of female subjugation; one of reproduction for reproduction’s sake, and one of cruelty for cruelty’s sake. Mandatory family-building on one hand, and power-trip sadism on the other. Wife and sister, each oscillating between the basement and throne room of the same castle; forever placeless, confused, and denied personhood.

In this overlapping state, one sees the world from the palace perspective. And the world is screaming.

Why did Utena make her choice, then? Who is she, what about her allowed it? Why couldn’t Akio see it coming? Despite the world, despite the piles of bodies?

Utena was on the track to where Kanae is. She was hand-selected, and Akio does not often make mistakes. But something happened, something he could not account for or prevent – something that he let slip through the cracks. Why was he so sure of himself at the end? Why was the mastermind so genuinely surprised by one little girl who he has basically controlled the life of for who knows how long?

Akio is so absorbed in his own self-built mythology, he has forgotten that the human spirit exists outside of it. He has long since convinced himself that Ohtori is the only world. He cannot see beyond its walls, and nor can he see certain things within them.

Akio does not believe in love. This is not to say Akio thinks love is dumb, or not worth anything, but that he genuinely cannot imagine it actually existing. His idea of love is so hollow, so evil, so utterly divorced from actual love – and he has created a world where most love tends in this direction. A naïve romantic dream that soon gives way to the harshness of the world, a love that can never remain whole and healthy, that always sinks deeper into stagnancy, codependency, and suffering. Just as he has. Akio does not know what love is, and can no longer recognize it.

He does not even recognize what genuine mutual support looks like, what basic friendship looks like. He is so used to looking at people as tools with their own shallow relationships to exploit, he is not prepared for them actually caring about each other. He has done his absolute damnedest to kill the possibility.

So when Utena and Anthy begin to get close, he writes it off as shallow and meaningless. Utena’s talk about liberating Anthy is just a boisterous, naïve savior complex, and Anthy is the same as she always is. She tells him as much every week. His plan is still fine. When Utena and Anthy begin to become friends, he ignores it again. Nanami and Keiko are “friends”. Touga and Saionji are “friends”. They must be only using each other, in the same way that everyone else is using each other, and if they find a bit of mutual solace in that, so be it.

And they are. They are, for the majority of the series, using each other. It is something Anthy is very familiar with, and something that Utena has to do to make herself feel better. They admit as much to each other, on the roof.

And suddenly everything is different. They have looked at each other.

Utena has seen the unimaginable violence the world works in. From the hotel to the couch; from her, to Anthy, to everyone. She has seen all Akio’s promises fall empty, all the final destinations of the fairy tale made clear. She has seen the truth of what she had forced herself to dream of for so long, and she finds herself unwilling to allow it to continue happening to someone else.

And Anthy has seen the brilliant humanity the world strives so strongly to destroy. She has seen that this one person has been willing to understand her, to not run away, to pull her back from the brink. She has seen her own heart, and broken as it may be, God, she still has one. She never was a corpse, living or dead.

When Utena and Anthy fall in love – when they do more than fall in love, when they at last begin to truly care for each other – Akio does not know. He does not know, even at the utter end, sitting at his old desk again. He does not understand.

Theirs is a love not based on Ohtori’s conception of it. They have grown beyond that. Theirs is not a cute love - it is founded on anger, on outrage, on mourning and on terrible empathy more than it is on what we have been told this entire time is romance. There are no stories here but their own. There is, we see, far more than the lie of royalty, and the world in all its force has not been able to take that from them.

“I came all this way to meet you.”

Utena has rejected the surface-level ideas of princesshood a long time ago, and now rejects them again at their absolute core. Her real revolutionary nature lies in the fact that she rejects princehood, as well. She throws out the entire conception of gender as forced adherence to romantic roles, as posturing imposition and coercion. She utterly discards the entire system, refuses to be defined by it, and in doing so pierces a single point in the heavy veil of Ohtori.

Utena Tenjou punches the stone ground so hard that her fist breaks, and drags her broken body to the heart of the world. She bleeds pure love, and the mechanism cannot bear it.

She did not save Anthy. She only made it possible for her to leave. She showed her a glimmer of a better world – a world where people on the brink of death keep fighting for the sake of those dear to them. A world where people keep moving against the current of pain, and deny it its power. A world where the whole prince and princess charade tears away, and leaves behind only humans. Where coffins are thrown open. Where lives the utter audacity of love. Where there are no roads.

A world where Anthy can look her brother in the eye and say no.

So she does.

And Ohtori dies.

But we’re forgetting someone again.

“No. They should have been taken care of at the beginning of the year.”

“’Himemiya’? Is that familiar to you?”

Nanami’s chest tightened, but she didn’t show it. “Oh, her? Yes, yes, of course she’s familiar. She transferred. Perfectly explicable.”

“Okay… but… oh, look at this! There’s another one. ‘Arisugawa’! Listed as fencing club captain, and council treasurer! How could we have missed that?”

“I’m council treasurer. And Miki’s been captain since Ruka graduated. Don’t you remember that?”

“Of course I do! Which is why this whole thing is so weird. Why do we have her photo if she never went here? This is, like,” she flipped through her notes, “the tenth or so person I’ve found that I just can’t explain. And that’s just in her year.”

“Probably just an error with the dates.”

“I… I guess, but… ugh! I’ve looked through so many of the past graduating classes, and none corroborate it! All their own fencing captains, everything in order…”

Nanami stood up, looking a bit cross. “Why are you spending so much time on this?”

Keiko was at a loss for words for a moment. “I mean, this is an important project, right? The record reorganization.”

“Yes. But no one asked you to go this far.”

“Still, I… oh, I don’t know. I just feel as if I’m missing something, as if… oh, never mind.”

“Are you feeling well?”

“Yes.”

“Sleeping enough?”

“Y-yes! But…”

“You’re worrying me.” She softened her tone a bit, but kept it authoritative. “Go home, Keiko. It’s late, we’ll call… we’ll call someone down in the morning to help figure this out.”

Keiko sighed, both reluctant and relieved. “Alright, Nanami.”

“…’Nanami’?”

“Oh! Sorry, sorry, I meant –”

“Don’t worry about it. I was just surprised.”

The two were quiet for a moment. The rain let up.

“We should probably get going.”

“You’re right… Nanami.”

They each allowed themselves a very small smile, and did not let the other see it.

Nanami walked home, half-wondering about it all. So much was still fuzzy – the borders between her dreams and reality; all her memories were in disarray. But they were all there. She remembered Juri, of course, it was only that her apparently real memories of her were all tied up in memories of the river, somehow, with the council, with some nonsense about bowling balls… how were you supposed to tell which way was up?

The leaves had all turned brown on the pavement by now, and were slick and dark with the recently passed rain. A lingering, humid warmth cut the cold dusk air, when the purple shadows of sunset just barely blushed at the border of the yawning blue and black. She let her legs walk her home, and allowed her mind to wander just a bit more than usual.

The house was quiet. Dark. She saw the light on in Touga’s room as she approached, but as she came inside saw that none of the others were on. That was strange. The thought “he probably has some girl over” pushed at her and she pushed it away again, violently.

She hung up her umbrella. She loosened her uniform’s tie – the winter uniform, with the awful teal blazer, that hadn’t existed until last month. She took off her shoes. She made her way through the silent and massive house, and opened her door.

The dress stood on its own in the center of her bedroom.

It was white. It was not supposed to be white.

Her brother stepped quietly from the shadows, wearing a new uniform bright against the dark. His hand moved to turn on the light, and her heart fell through the floor.

Detached from the moment, Nanami realized immediately what this was. She also realized that a few months ago it would have worked. She would have melted into his arms even as the terror began to rise in her; she would have succumbed, and let herself harden around the steel. But it was too late. It was far too late. She still remembered it. She remembered Anthy, she even remembered Utena. And she remembered, every waking second, the sound that rushed through the End of the World.

He spoke impossibly gently. “Good. I see you understand, Nanami. Now, knowing everything of this world –”

“Are you trying to sound cool!?” She surprised herself with how loud she shouted. The sense that there should be music playing promptly disappeared and left a close, echoless silence.

He sighed and shifted his stance, unbothered.

“Did you think I would still say yes? After everything?”

“Put the dress on, Nanami.”

“No!”

“Haven’t you seen it?” He stepped forward, straightening his posture. “I know what you’re looking for. I truly do. I want it too, but this is… this is the only way we can be together. You don’t know how special, how precious you truly are… how necessary. We have already come too far. This is what you are.”

“Liar! God, you’re not even good at this!”

“Nanami.”

“I wanted to believe that I’d imagined it, that I was only… Did you change? Were you always like this? Did… did he do something to you? I… I…”

“He only showed me that which was in front of me. Eternity. Our eternity.”

She said nothing.

“Nanami.” At this point he could not help a slightly impatient tone, “Put. The dress on.”

“You don’t get it?” Her voice strained and became faint. “Who… who do you think you are?”

And, in a voice that threatened to swallow the sky, he answered: “The End of the World.”

She turned and sprinted out of the house.

She did not cry. She would not allow herself to cry. She only ran, slipping on the leaves, her breath bursting in little white clouds. She did not cry even when she heard the door slam again behind her, even as she heard Touga following her – begging, cajoling, threatening, cursing. She did not look back, and she did not cry, and his voice immediately fell silent as she clattered over the open thresh-

old of the Academy. She ran, the air cold as a knife at her face, and she did not think about it or cry a drop until she was already at the station ten minutes later, the blind train pulling up in front of a breathless and freezing girl who had nothing in the world but her uniform and schoolbag.

Only then did she deign to cry. She cried hard as the night city flew behind her, and then the moonlit sea. She cried for the weight of his hand on her shoulder and the years of dead selves that accompanied it. She cried for the memory of the name of a cat and the yawning hatred of empty rooms. She cried for Anthy, and she cried worse for Kozue, and she cried terribly for the evil they had all been taught was love. She cried and the world slowly disappeared.

It fell to the distance, and as the melted average of the passing buildings slowly came to a single story among the bare trees, she collected herself. She stopped shaking. She wiped her eyes, and unfolded her limbs which had been drawn like a vise around her bag and shoulders, and finally looked up.

Anthy Himemiya sat directly across from her, wearing a warm pink dress with her hair down. She smiled, and with an unbearable, nonchalant gentleness, she spoke. “It’s good to see you here.”

And in that moment there was nothing else to say, and the world had no roads.

Chapter 6: part four, hatching nanami

And now we know everything of this world. What happens next?

I wrote a short proof-of-concept fanfiction to answer the question “what happens after Anthy leaves Ohtori,” and I guess this essay will be an effort to justify it. I’d recommend reading it before the rest of this post.

Though, in the simplest form I can get it, what happens after is that the doors are open. Utena has made it possible to see outside of the Academy, out of the entire system. That knowledge may be ignored by some people, but even then it will nudge at them subconsciously – Utena has proved, even with no one to witness it, that there is something besides Ohtori’s world. There is a way out.

The thing about Ohtori – the thing about structures in general, really, especially gender – is that for it to continue standing people must buy into it. They must believe the story. Ohtori as a system depends on people upholding the ideals of its own conception of romance, because it works exclusively through people and relationships. There is nothing physical in the entire construct; even the swords are human hearts behind their metaphor.

The only reason our cast tortured itself so much in the series was a shared and deeply held belief in the system. Wakaba aspiring for its specialness, Saionji attempting to make a place for himself, Kozue giving up her purity to make it easier. Everyone sees the whole structure as unshakeable, immutable, default. How could anyone change the world, beyond sanctioned and inaccessible revolution? You either believe in love and try to make it work within the system, or stop believing and try to exploit the system as best you can – but that system in and of itself is never called into question.

Until Utena.

Her giving up, her walking away, is the only way to get out of this trap. She decided that she didn’t actually want to try to make it work anymore. The lie of the prince was exposed and rejected. Two girls who were both called princess and witch walked away without having to be either label. The whole thing was thrown out – maybe only in one relationship, maybe only symbolically – but whatever happened, it happened at the utter core of it.

The duel called revolution remains without a victor. No counter-image has been put forward: the palace is empty. Different people will react to this in different ways. Some will leave the academy, and some will cling even tighter to it. Ohtori will react, too – it is a machine that is more than capable of stabilizing itself when challenged even this deeply. With both Anthy and Utena absent, and Akio panicking at his sudden impotent irrelevance, the entire archetypal trio of figures at the helm of the ship has all but vanished – however, instead of simply collapsing, the edifice will first attempt to reestablish itself upon a different foundational relationship.

Looks like we’ve spent the entire show quietly building up a pair of candidates.

We’re in heavy speculation territory here. I freely acknowledge this is all guesswork, but not the baseless kind. I’m projecting what I think happens next off of my previously laid-out conception of what Ohtori is, what it values, and how it works – namely; child abuse, male power, and an overwhelming system of romantic mythology to keep everyone in line. I’ve seen a truly incredible range of opinions on the next course of events – but based on my own reading, I can only see one way forward: Touga begins to push Nanami into being his princess, at the heart of the world.

The palace is empty. The Kiryuus inherit it. We’ve long since established Touga’s hungry eagerness to take up the position – so the choice in the end is Nanami’s.

And, honestly? We’ve already established what that choice will be.

But we have a few loose ends to tie up before we get into the utter end. We still need to establish a bit more baseline before we reach epilogue territory. There’s still two sibling pairs we haven’t discussed as thoroughly in this series of essays: the Kaorus, and the Arisugawas.

Miki and Kozue — Lightning Strikes the Forest

“Ok, you’ve got me,” a more skeptic hypothetical audience member might be asking me right about now, “power dynamics and knowing coercion is what makes this whole thing so terrible. But what about incest without that? What about the best case scenario? Let me go devil’s advocate here – let’s get rid of the age difference factor, let’s assume the sister is the one initiating in this case – how do you parse that? Can you still condemn it so easily?”

Not as easily, no; there’s the rub. But we can still very clearly condemn it.

I haven’t touched on the Kaorus much, which might come off as weird considering I’ve spent however many words before this yelling about siblings. They comprise our third main pair, one degree more removed from the central archetype when compared to the Kiryuus. This is where the ambiguity kicks in, and makes them harder to parse – they seem more common, more believable almost, than the Kiryuus’ and Himemiyas’ curated conformance and intensity.

Miki and Kozue, for the moment, have the freedom to still be people before the prince and princess story fully claims them. As the youngest and most mutually confused, they deal in more moral greyness, more subtlety, and less explicitness. The Kiryuus can be clearly identified as an emulation of the Himemiyas, but the Kaorus aren’t fully following in the steps of either.

The question here is: we know Touga could be an Akio, but could Miki be a Touga? Could Kozue?

The morals of responsibility get shaky when dealing with this stuff. We can clearly name Akio and Touga as deliberately abusive, but the why there is never just simple hatred or cruelty. That is a part of it, yes, a massive part of it – but they are looking for something in doing what they do to their sisters. Akio is looking for an eternity that has been rotting for a long time; Touga is looking for power in a world he feels powerless in. Both, in their abusiveness, dig themselves deeper into what they flee, and perpetuate the structure.

What are Miki and Kozue looking for?

Kozue is looking for purity. For childhood. For a return to her previous innocence, which was taken from her too early. Somewhere between neglectful parents and a stream of hungry boys there was a darkening of her world, a dirtiness that she does not see in Miki. Maybe if she capitalized on that dirtiness, she can push herself hard enough to get back. Where else has she ever been happy besides with Miki? Why doesn’t it feel right anymore? Those questions don’t matter. She just needs to make it work one more time.

Kozue, in Miki’s last duel, accepts her own version of the rose bride’s dress, standing next to Miki perfect in his princely council uniform. She has brought him here, she has determined that this relationship is the only route to finding what she’s looking for. “Hey,” she says to Anthy, sidling up next to her, “Tell me how to be just like you.”

I don’t think Kozue knows what being like Anthy would mean for her. She thinks she knows - but she has desensitized herself only to the more shallow modes of power-weighted sexuality, and does not understand Anthy’s state. This speaks volumes on what she thinks she and Miki are – she does not truly want to move towards Akio and Anthy’s relationship, only backwards into her and Miki’s old one.

Miki is looking for belonging, and to make sense of himself. The two were raised in an environment of fancy neglect, and for a long time only had each other. Miki has opened up a bit more, become more involved in student life, but has distances himself from his idea of home. Kozue does the same thing, but more loudly while still clinging to it, but Miki does it in truth. The fact that he even has a sister blindsides us the first time we learn about it.

What is he running from? Maybe she has grown past him for the first time, and he doesn’t want to admit it. Maybe he is scared of her, in some way. Maybe he wants to break from his past, convinced it has no place for him, seeing it as a ruined place instead of Kozue’s vision of innocence and peace. Maybe he remembers more, and does not want to.

So he looks elsewhere, and stumbles into Anthy and Touga, of all possible people. I’m sure those two have healthy advice for him.

The Kaorus’ songs talk about time. About returning to the past; about embodying the past, present, and future. Will we find eternity if we go far back enough? If we regress enough? If we return to an idea of childhood only to find that it has already been tainted and changed by a false romance?

Miki and Kozue have lost something. A once-familiar dynamic that did not require words has somewhere along the way fallen out of sync, and left both of them never having learned how to talk about it. They are in an era of reformation – trying to figure out how they feel about each other.

We see here how Ohtori perverts formerly normal relationships. Each of them looks to the world for an answer on what to do next, and the world answers “obsession and possessiveness”. Of course everything will go back to comfortable normalcy if Kozue can manage to get closer to Miki again, and while spending time with Akio and all the other boys she learns what getting closer again would require. Of course Miki can find a place he belongs in again, as long as he can manage to find a special love again – one that, Touga teaches him, must have been romantic.

They have lost each other, and look for each other in the wrong places, with the wrong motivations.

Are the Kaorus outright abusive towards each other, currently? Maybe not. I don’t think so. This comes off as just general miserable unhealthiness to me, not entirely instigated by either of them, where each of them seem to be hurt by it in the same way. Do they have the potential to be abusive, however? Absolutely.

The world is pushing them in the direction of romance. There is a path prepared for them. They each take on a kind of cruelty. Miki borders on chauvinism in his attitude towards Anthy, and Kozue has gotten very attached to her jealous “impurity”, but it’s hard for me to name either of them as fully responsible for this weird codependent mess. At Ohtori, when one comes to know the way the world works, they do one of three things: reject it, pretend it does not exist, or accept and embrace it: Miki is trying very hard to ignore it, and Kozue is attempting to embrace it.

The key here is that neither of them are actively seeking to hurt the other. Neither of them are, properly, looking for power. Kozue might want to shock Miki into action, and Miki may want to just stop being confused, but their possessiveness comes in the form of ideas towards a goal that is something other than possessiveness. It has been the idea of romance being the only kind of love they can have that makes them so unwilling to look at each other. They are each worried that if they do, they will see something they don’t actually want.

But Kozue has convinced herself she wants it. It is easier that way, after all. And Miki flinches first, looks back, and sees it. They have one duel together, one moment of tense, stilted prince-princess playing-house – and then they break apart again.

The last we see of them, Kozue has crawled back to the piano, but keeps her distance. I’d like to believe they are slowly finding a way through.

Juri and ? — Crossing the River

I really didn’t like writing this one, but I can’t ignore it.

Juri… where do we begin with Juri? I guess we start at where she starts at, so often: Shiori.

For a long time I didn’t understand why Juri loved Shiori. I couldn’t see what she saw in her. It seemed irrational from the outside, as if Juri was just being naïve and willfully blind about what Shiori is like. Even with nearly every character having similarly miserable romantic obsessions, something about how she relates to Shiori just didn’t click for me. It is more subtle than our usual fare.

Juri has never been able to move through the world in the way Shiori has. Shiori in her eyes is almost ethereal, walking without touching the ground; a sort of mysterious ambiguity which looks almost like detachment from the grand social game. Shiori is dreamy, impulsive, vague in all her mannerisms. I’d call it childish, in both the innocent and cruel sense: Shiori tears the wings off butterflies. Is this just weird sadism, or a curious and scientific mind?

Maybe this is an engineered mystery. Shiori moves as smoothly as Touga does through the rote of social positioning, and if she understands that she has this charm to her, she certainly has also learned to weaponize it. We see her utterly joyous when she gets to hurt Juri – does she do this just for the pleasure of it, or is she just a confused, resentful, limited girl only knowing half of the story?

Regardless – Juri insists on believing the best of her. I think she is almost fascinated with Shiori’s freedom, her ease and lack of immobilizing worry in her life. I get very strong images of someone shielding a kitten from a fire even as it claws at their breast. Juri is only holding out for the day when the rebellious phase ends; the day that it works, the day that she knows her feelings – and hopes to learn something in the process. Until that day, it doesn’t really matter what Shiori does.

I’ve found that the key to understanding these two is the fact that Juri’s love for Shiori is a truly unconditional one. It is almost as if she is trying to prove something to herself.

Ikuhara in one of the commentary tracks spoke about why the romanticization of incest works. In his words, it is a relationship that is permanent by nature. It is the idea of two people sharing the same body, or one person living in two bodies. A shared self, a home. Intimacy. Unconditionality. This is a screaming and blatant lie, though sometimes a comforting one, and everything that takes this as a base will be founded on its lie.

The concept of the eternal castle finds its origin here.

Please bear with me:

I think it’s interesting that in a world so defined by attempts at heterosexual sibling relationships, our most explicitly lesbian character also breaks the pattern by having an older sister instead of brother. I think it’s interesting that this is revealed at the utter emotional climax of the series, in a cutaway at the same time Utena has just been backstabbed to the ground.

I think it’s interesting that the single reference to Juri’s sister is involved with the river leitmotif. We see the river with Nanami’s cat, with Adolescence’s Touga, and with Juri’s sister. Something about sacrifice for love. Something about irreparable early loss. Something about the death of children seeping into one’s identity.

Juri has so clearly seen through so much of the world, but still finds herself bound to it. Why is this? What was it that made her see? Is it only her history with Shiori; is she somehow this disillusioned while still so fervently believing?

It’s very easy for me to read Juri as a survivor of sibling abuse, of some kind. Her situation is unique in that it has already been a long time since, unlike Nanami, and in that she has actually gotten away, unlike Anthy. It’s very easy for me to see all her stilted, angry coldness as an attempt to cope with that. Her distance, her resentment of everyone else’s frivolity a reaction to being exposed earlier to more blatant modes of how the world works.

It’s very easy for me to parse her fixation on Shiori an attempt to rediscover or rebuild an eternity she may have been promised and denied – to build a new story to define herself by as a way to outgrow an older and more terrible one. Juri’s, Shiori’s, and even Ruka’s songs uniformly focus on the concept of duality, of being two opposite things at once, speaking to a deep confusion and sense of wrongness that I believe Juri is trying to find a way out of.

This is an extremely bold and extremely serious assertion, with less basis and less sense even than anything I’ve said about Kanae. I realize that fully, and honestly apologize for it. I definitely don’t want to posit this as canon, only as one option of how to read things – there’s an argument to be made here. I don’t bring this up just for the hell of it.

If we have established anything in these essays, it is that being a little sister in Utena means something, and in Juri’s case, it worries me. Over the course of the show’s 39 episodes, it rains exactly twice: once for Nanami, and once for Juri.

Let’s leave it at that.

Eternity

Why does Utena focus on sibling abuse so intensely?

This isn’t a story that’s told very often. It is very rarely talked about so frankly, much less sympathetically. A big part of what makes Utena so important to me is that it dares to tackle the issue with all the gravity and integrity it requires. This show is genuinely the only fictional work I’ve seen that is both mostly about discussing the subject, and dedicated to condemning it.

Utena does take incest as a kind of archetype and metaphor for abuse as a whole, but in doing so doesn’t shy away from discussing it in specific, in the context of trends and mechanisms specific to it. The savior prince stems directly from the protective older brother, and the waiting princess directly from the petulant little sister – but this is a story anyone can tell themselves. Romance as an idea has been perverted. Blood poured into the water at the source of the fountain. Pain becomes universal, and cheap.

Fate; an enduring and validating narrative to justify your life’s course. Specialness, the shining thing; the idea of, even if just once, hearing “it has to be you, it can’t be anyone but you”. Eternity; security, stability, an end to fear and anxiety. True love. Belonging. Home.

This is what we look for in intimacy. This is what all human hearts yearn for. At Ohtori, and in our own world as well, we have been lied to about where to find this. The narrative of incestuous romance capitalizes viciously on the desire for all these things. It promises an easy route to all of them; a place that inherently has them. It lies through its teeth, for one purpose only.

We asked this question at the beginning of Part Two and were only able to answer it through different characters’ limited views. But now we can answer it for real:

What is incest?

It is, in its ideal form, eternity. It is something that never ends. It is something you can never get out of you, or escape from. Eternity is a lack of change, a lack of growth; a preserved, static, stagnant portrait. It is the idea that one never has to grow or mature again. The promise that everything is just fine as it is, that there is a place that will accept you as you are, that you do not have to worry about. This promise is empty.

Those who build systems like this fear change and maturity because it undermines their own power. It makes it harder to lie and manipulate. They are happy with eternity because they do not want to relinquish their power to some idea of “human respect” or whatever. They want to remain as they are, forever: positioned to take advantage of anyone in their spell for the sake of shallow powertrip pleasure of convenience, at the cost of ruined lives.

The promise of “you are perfect as you are” turns very quickly into “you are perfect when you are exactly as I want you, and worthless otherwise”. It can’t be lived up to naturally, and requires a violently enforced warping of the very self. If Anthy has grown accustomed to where she is, it is out of last-resort coping and adaptation, not out of an aboriginal love of being stabbed.

Eternity and revolution are opposite concepts.

It is, by its nature, abusive. It is a relationship that cannot work and does not even believe itself to be able to; only pretending to for the benefit of its victims. In some ways it is the first instance of abuse – the closest and earliest it can happen to a person; a midpoint between parental abuse and non-familial peer abuse that encapsulates the worst of both.

The pressures of family make romance impossible. There is always an element of power in any familial relationship, and this dynamic runs even deeper than non-familial ones. Someone is older, someone is more easily believed, someone is physically stronger. There is the blanket cultural expectation of loyalty to family that makes even saying no so much more difficult, not to mention speaking out about it. Incest always happens in a kind of violent secrecy, that must obscure the harm it does in order to continue. It is a capitalization on a pre-existing, natural kind of love; ruining what should be one of the most lasting and supportive relationships a person has in their lives out of an incomprehensibly strong selfishness.

This doesn’t just show up naturally. This ever happening depends on a betrayal of a very fundamental human trust. It is the erosion of the ability to believe in anything; to believe in a family that won’t hurt you out of shallow self-obsessed entitlement, to believe in an idea of love that is based on anything other than violence, sex, and power.

It is the legitimization of abuse. I mentioned Utena having an attitude of universal condemnation, and that is very much an exception. Works that focus on incest tend to either treat it as an unnuanced shock-value plot device, or else build it up into an idealized romance between equals whose darkest bits only come from outside taboo attitudes. From Game of Thrones to Oreimo, we either see it consistently brushed to the side or viciously sold as innocuous forbidden love. Even Saitou’s After the Revolution chapters are guilty of this – continuing to write Nanami out entirely, and presenting Miki and Kozue as healing from the events of the show through embracing the very relationship that caused the injury in the first place. If this is all we ever see of it in fiction, it either seems outlandish or unimportant in real life, and feeds into the constant refrain of those who do not want to understand: “What’s so bad about it, after all?” “Weren’t you just kids experimenting?” “Haven’t you moved on yet?” and finally, “You let it happen.”

It is the lie of consent; that all “yes”s are equal. It is an obfuscation of social and personal pressures that can change one’s will; this insistence that being groomed since birth can somehow produce feelings that are still naturally your own. No victim comes into this willingly. None of us actually fall in love. Some were never under any illusions about this, and some most certainly are.

Anthy “loves” Akio because she has been robbed of everything else in her life that she could hold on to through the swords’ pain. Nanami “loves” Touga as the results of a years-long campaign to produce that in her, to make her dependent and obsessive via a constant environment of manufactured unreality. Kozue “loves” Miki because she has been convinced that retreating into unhealthy, childish specialness is the only way to stay afloat in a world that does a lot worse to girls than longing. These are false loves, based on fear, on desperation, on intense guilt and blame, on a Stockholm loss of options.

All they are looking for is a place to anchor, and the only option given is one that hates them.

This image replicates itself. If it can be done here, it can be done anywhere.

Eternity never lasts, in any of the names it sells itself by. It cannot. Eternity isn’t real. We start to realize. Juri has probably known for a long time, but is still in denial. Anthy and Utena abandoned the concept entirely. And now, Nanami…

Nanami comes to turn away from it also. She turns away because she has understood two things: one, that she is not alone, and two, that there is a way out. The lessons that she has learned at the end are the same ones we must take with us if we hope to make any dent in our own culture – one that is disturbingly similar to Ohtori.

Conclusion

Nanami starts the show off as a joke. A caricature, a classical trope. We’ve seen Nanamis a hundred times before, especially in anime and manga. The entitled brat. The spiteful rival. The popular bully. She has a polished ojou laugh, sneers with every motion, even has a gaggle of lackeys. She is as much archetype as she is shoujo stock character. In another series she could be a true antagonist.

For a fourth of the show she is sold as a side character, and much of her screentime consists of gags even beyond that. Nanami is thrown around by elephants, has cutesy misunderstanding escapades, all absurd and over the top. Surely this is a girl who exaggerates. Surely she is just prone to the drama and weirdness. Surely her cruelty is as senseless as it is pathetic. Surely we should look at her as an enemy to our real heroines. A witch to be defeated.

And then we see more of her.

When we see Touga place the rose crest onto Nanami’s finger, we realize that we’ve already been told this story. It is the same story as the one Utena tells to herself at the beginning of each episode, trying to hammer it even further into her skull.

When we finally see Anthy, young and in the farmhouse, worrying herself sick over her unresponsive and distant brother, we realize that we’ve already been told this story. It is the same story as Nanami with her hair down, helplessly peeking into Touga’s depression room with the phonograph blaring.

Nanami is on the exact same path as Utena and Anthy. Princesshood.

Nanami’s character for much of the series hinges on confusion. She is constantly pushing herself to keep up with this set of shifting goalposts, to sort out all her competing feelings. She is a knot of a girl. She has this desperate need to simultaneously grow up and retreat into her past, which translates naturally into chasing after Touga to achieve both. Touga understands everything, and if she can just make him like her again, she’ll understand too. We know this.

But how does that illusion break?

It happens in one place. It begins collapsing when Touga plays with her heart just a bit too far, deteriorates past salvageability when she learns about Anthy, and is entirely out of her system at the end of her last duel. She will need time to put things together, but her suspension of disbelief has already been pushed too far. She has seen something so clear and so terrible that she can no longer believe in it.

Ohtori depends on belief. Nanami has fallen out of love, and no longer is as blind as is required for the grooming protocols to work on her. Touga jumped the gun on her, went too fast, was a bit too honest – and now, she knows. Maybe not everything about how the world works. Maybe not all the theory and dynamics around it. But she certainly knows the truth of what incest is, and the allegiances towards it of those most in power.

Through the lessons of Anthy and Utena, Nanami most of all has learned that there is more than this. There is no metaphysical fate around her neck, there is no loyalty she owes to the world. What those two dared to accomplish echoes down to her; exposing the structure that so desperately depends on silence and invisibility. It is hard to see, especially so suddenly. Nanami reacts in the short term with an emphatic retreat into normalcy, into chaban, but it can’t hold. She can only go up, or out: up to the false promise of specialness, or out into a better world.

Even before the end of the show Nanami has made her choice. It is a tentative one – a nervous one, which still clings a bit to denial – but her little racquetball speech tells us enough. “There was nothing, nothing at all,” and maybe that “nothing” is kinder than the “everything” of the palace. She has seen it all for what it is: the rigged dueling game, the castle that existed in image alone, the song and dance of cajoling predation. All pathetic immaturity in the end. A culture of idiot adolescence.

Utena is about breaking from the narratives of abuse. It is about breaking from the miserable modes of existing that the world insists are the only ones available to you, and building something else. It is about coming into the fullness of your self – on your own terms, and independently from all those who would control and hurt you. Utena is about building a space where this is possible.

This can only be done when we know everything of the world, and turn away from it nonetheless.

Nanami makes it out because she sees the violence that she has faced is not only her own. She does not carry this pain alone. In Anthy, in Utena, in Kozue, even in Juri and Miki she has seen kindred spirits, and come to understand that she is not all that special, after all. The world grinds down on everyone. When she begins to understand the true and full extent of the damage it has done both to her friends and enemies, she understands that it is bigger than her, and can no longer accept it.

This is not a story that is often told. We do not often tell stories about sisters in love with their brothers. We do not often tell stories about brothers destroying their sisters. We do not often tell stories about girls groomed since childhood by tall dark strangers, nor do we often tell stories about women trapped in empty lives where even the spark of the lie has faded. We do not often tell stories about the boys and men who have experienced the same thing.

We do not often tell stories about girls finding strength in each other. We do not often tell stories about women striking back at not only the people that have hurt them, but the very concepts that have enabled those people’s evil in the first place. We do not often tell stories, in this world, that are strong enough to end it.

But Utena does. And Utena does.

We can’t be fooled. There is more to the world than this empty, self-serving dynamic. This is not some natural, unavoidable rule of existence. There is more! More to life than this cycle of ownership and victimhood, more to love than violence and desperation. We do not have to be what the worst parts of this world ask us to.

We have to expose the structure for what it is, in all the places it has claimed. And we can provide that light, no matter our supposed and enforced powerlessness.

We have to talk about it. We have to tell our stories. We have to take stories like this seriously. We have to speak it, in all the ten thousand years of poetry it requires, because for people to break themselves out of it they must know what Nanami now knows. You are not alone. None of us have to be alone in this. That is as comforting as it is terrible, and there is work to be done to be worthy of it.

We have roads to build. We have princes to kill. If the chick does not break its shell, it will exist as a living corpse until it quietly disappears.

And Nanami is hatching.
