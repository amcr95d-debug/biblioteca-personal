Harry Levin

Interrogation, Doubt, Irony: Thesis, Antithesis, Synthesis

I

In reconsidering Hamlet, we cannot pretend that we are unaware of what happens next or how it all comes out. Knowing what will finally be decided, critics have grown impatient over its agonies of decision, and have blamed Hamlet for undue procrastination. But what may be a foregone conclusion to them must be an open question to him, as we have reminded ourselves by watching the process unfold, and observing how the tone is set through the interaction of questions, answers, and unanswered speculations. Having rehearsed the play once with an emphasis on the interrogative mood, let us push the interrogation further by returning to certain indicative passages, tracing now an inner train of thought, and later placing it in a broader perspective. Interrogatio is classified—by the rhetorician, Henry Peacham—as a form of pathopoeia, which in turn is neither more nor less than a device for arousing emotions: “Examples hereof are common in Tragedies.” Dubitatio, our next figure of speech and thought, is less emotional and more deliberative. As it is defined by Abraham Fraunce, in The Arcadian Rhetorike, “Addubitation or doubting is a kinde of deliberation with our selves.” The orator deliberates between rival options: either to revenge or not to revenge, whether a visitant comes from heaven or hell. For doubt is a state of mind where the questioner faces no single answer nor the lack of one, but rather a choice between a pair of alternatives. Etymologically, the word stems from dubitare, which means precisely to hesitate in the face of two possibilities. The structure of Hamlet seems, at every level, to have been determined by this duality. “A double blessing is a double grace” (I.iii.53).

Similarly, the texture is characterized by a tendency to double and redouble words and phrases. From the very first scene, the speeches abound in hendiadys: “gross and scope,” “law and heraldry.” Sometimes the paired nouns are redundant synonyms: “food and diet,” “pith and moment”—Saxon balancing Latin as in the doublets of Sir Thomas Browne. Adjectives or verbs are coupled at other times: “impotent and bedrid,” “countenance and excuse.” This reduplication seems to be a habit of courtly diction into which Hamlet himself falls now and then: “the purpose of playing . . . is . . . to show . . . the very age and body of the time his form and pressure” (III.ii.21–25). By the count of R. A. Foakes, no less than 247 such pairings are scattered through the play. They are doubtless more ornamental than functional; yet they charge the air with overtones of wavering and indecision. The Clown goes farther with his equivocations, putting his finger on serious ambiguities. And Hamlet goes too far with his double entendres, besmirching the maidenly innocence of Ophelia. Claudius, in his opening address to the Council, establishes himself as a practiced exponent of stately double-talk. With unctuous skill, he manages a transition from the old King’s death to himself and his inherited queen. Antithesis is condensed into oxymoron: “delight and dole,” “defeated joy.” Some of these mannerisms will have their echo in the stilted language of the Play-King: “Grief joys, joy grieves, on slender accident” (III.ii.209). The formal style is a mask, which accords with the dress and etiquette of the court; Claudius is virtually winking, when he speaks of “an auspicious and a dropping eye” (I.ii.11). Hamlet, speaking informally and ironically to Horatio, sums up the paradoxical situation:

The funeral bak’d meats

Did coldly furnish forth the marriage tables.

(180–81)

The incrimination of Claudius by the Ghost, duly recorded in the book of Hamlet’s brain, is an object lesson in duplicity. Claudius himself is unremittingly conscious of the distinction between the “exterior” and “the inward man” (II.ii.6). Both in communing with himself and in dealing with others, he seldom fails to distinguish between words and deeds, or face and heart. He introduces Gertrude by publicly casting her in a dual role, “our sometime sister, now our queen,” as he does his nephew shortly afterward, “my cousin Hamlet, and my son” (I.ii.8, 64). Hamlet resentfully picks up the implications, and caustically refers to his

II

“kindless” uncle as “a little more than kin, and less than kind” (I.ii.65). The two meanings of “kind” are among the earliest of Shakespeare’s puns, and they point to a basic theme. Throughout the tragedy there are references to “kind” in the sense of nature, and in the sense of relationship. But the two are not merely homonyms; they are related through the common idea of family or race. The pathos of Ophelia’s madness turns upon the loss of “kind” in both senses. It is a “deed of kind” that she cannot understand, and she goes mad singing snatches of old songs, which are themselves “unshaped” and “unvalued” (“unvalued” meaning both “invaluable” and “worthless”). The ambiguity of “kind” is a continual reminder of the confusion between the natural and the familial, which is the tragic subject of the play.

This theme is developed by means of a series of antitheses, which are at once psychological and rhetorical. Hamlet’s problem is to reconcile the claims of nature and of grace, of blood and of spirit, of revenge and of conscience. He is called upon to be at once the son of his father and the son of God, to obey the Ghost and to obey the moral law. The conflict is presented in terms of a series of pairs: the dead King and Claudius, Gertrude and Ophelia, Fortinbras and Laertes, Horatio and Osric, the Player and the Clown. Each pair sets up a contrast and a comparison, and each character is defined by his relation to the others. The play is a network of such relationships, and its meaning is to be found in the pattern they make.

The first of these antitheses is that between the dead King and Claudius. The Ghost describes his brother as “a wretch whose natural gifts were poor / To those of mine” (I.v.51–52), and Claudius admits that Hamlet is “the most immediate to our throne” (I.ii.109). The contrast is between the ideal and the actual, between the heroic and the political, between the man of honor and the man of expediency. Hamlet’s father is described as a Hyperion, a Mars, a Mercury; Claudius is a “satyr,” a “mildewed ear,” a “vice of kings.” The son is called upon to be like his father, but he finds that he is more like his uncle. The Ghost’s command to revenge is a command to be true to his father’s nature, but the very act of revenge is a denial of that nature. The Ghost is a figure of the old heroic code, and Hamlet is a figure of the new consciousness, which cannot accept that code without question.

III

The second antithesis is that between Gertrude and Ophelia. Gertrude is the Queen, the mother, the wife; Ophelia is the daughter, the maiden, the lover. Gertrude is guilty of incest and adultery; Ophelia is innocent and pure. Gertrude is a woman of the world; Ophelia is a child of nature. Gertrude is associated with the court; Ophelia is associated with the countryside. Gertrude is the object of Hamlet’s disgust; Ophelia is the object of his love. The two women represent the two poles of Hamlet’s attitude toward sex. His mother’s sin has contaminated his whole view of womanhood, and he cannot separate his love for Ophelia from his loathing for Gertrude. When he cries, “Frailty, thy name is woman!” (I.ii.146), he is speaking of his mother, but he is also speaking of Ophelia. The tragedy of Ophelia is that she is destroyed by a corruption she does not understand. She is the innocent victim of the sins of others, and her madness is a symbol of the breakdown of the moral order.

The third antithesis is that between Fortinbras and Laertes. Both are young men of action, both are sons of fathers who have been killed, both are called upon to revenge. Fortinbras is the son of the King of Norway, who was killed by Hamlet’s father; Laertes is the son of Polonius, who was killed by Hamlet. Fortinbras is a soldier, Laertes is a courtier. Fortinbras is calm, deliberate, and successful; Laertes is passionate, impulsive, and doomed. Fortinbras is the man of honor who acts according to the code; Laertes is the man of passion who acts according to his emotions. Hamlet compares himself to both, and finds himself wanting. He admires Fortinbras, who “makes mouths at the invisible event” (IV.iv.50), and he envies Laertes, who “in a riotous head” (IV.v.103) dares to defy the King. But he cannot be either, because he is too conscious of the moral issues involved. The contrast between Fortinbras and Laertes is a contrast between two kinds of action, and Hamlet’s dilemma is that he can accept neither.

IV

The fourth antithesis is that between Horatio and Osric. Horatio is the scholar, the skeptic, the friend; Osric is the courtier, the fop, the servant. Horatio is the man of reason, who questions everything and accepts nothing without proof; Osric is the man of fashion, who accepts everything and questions nothing. Horatio is the ideal spectator, who understands Hamlet and sympathizes with him; Osric is the caricature of the courtier, who understands nothing and sympathizes with no one. Horatio is the representative of the audience, the one who will tell Hamlet’s story; Osric is the representative of the court, the one who will carry out Claudius’s orders. The contrast between them is a contrast between the world of thought and the world of appearance, between the reality and the show. Hamlet’s praise of Horatio is one of the most moving passages in the play, because it expresses his longing for a world where such integrity is possible.

The fifth antithesis is that between the Player and the Clown. The Player is the actor who can weep for Hecuba, who can “force his soul so to his own conceit” (II.ii.562); the Clown is the gravedigger who jokes about death, who can “sing at a grave-making” (V.i.67). The Player represents the power of art to move the emotions; the Clown represents the power of humor to distance the emotions. The Player is the man of feeling, who identifies with his role; the Clown is the man of detachment, who observes the absurdity of the human condition. The contrast between them is a contrast between two ways of dealing with death: the tragic and the comic. Hamlet is caught between them, and his final acceptance of death is a synthesis of both. He learns from the Player that emotion can be expressed, and from the Clown that death is not to be feared.

V

The final synthesis is achieved in the last act, where all the antitheses are resolved. Hamlet returns from England a changed man. He has ceased to struggle against his fate, and has learned to accept it. He says, “There’s a divinity that shapes our ends, / Rough-hew them how we will” (V.ii.10–11). He has come to see that his own plans are not the only ones, and that there is a power beyond his control that works for good. He says, “The readiness is all” (V.ii.234). He has learned to be ready for whatever comes, without fear or hope. He has achieved a state of disinterestedness, which is the highest wisdom. He no longer hates Claudius, or loves Ophelia, or mourns his father. He is beyond all passion, and therefore beyond all suffering. He is ready to die, and he dies with the words “the rest is silence” (V.ii.369).

The synthesis is not a simple return to the beginning, but a resolution of the contradictions. Hamlet has learned that the truth is not to be found in any single answer, but in the tension between answers. He has learned that the world is not to be understood by reason alone, but by the acceptance of mystery. He has learned that the meaning of life is not to be found in action, but in the readiness to act. He has learned that the only way to overcome death is to accept it. His dying words are not a denial of life, but an affirmation of its limits. The silence that follows is not empty, but full of meaning. It is the silence of a man who has seen the truth, and has found that it cannot be spoken.

This is the final irony of Hamlet: that the hero who begins as a man of words ends as a man of silence, and that the play which begins with a question ends with an answer that cannot be articulated. The tragedy is not that Hamlet dies, but that he dies without being able to tell us what he has learned. He says, “Had I but time—as this fell sergeant, Death, / Is strict in his arrest—O, I could tell you— / But let it be” (V.ii.347–349). The rest is silence. And that silence is the most eloquent thing in the play. It is the silence of the infinite, which cannot be expressed in finite words. It is the silence of the truth, which cannot be captured by language. It is the silence of the self, which cannot be known by the mind. It is the silence that Shakespeare leaves us with, and that we must fill with our own understanding.
