I

Freud and Love:
Treatment and Its Discontents

In his journey through the land of love Freud reaches Narcissus only after having traveled over the dissociated space of hysteria. The latter leads him to establish the "psychic space" that he will explode, first through Narcissus and finally through the death drive, into the impossible spaces of "lovehate,"[1] that is, infinite transference.

Narcissism—A Screen for Emptiness

The hypothesis of Narcissus is crucial to this Freudian course. Before calling itself "death," the libido undergoes a first threat to its omnipotence—one that makes the existence of an other for the self appear problematic. Freud seems to suggest that it is not Eros but narcissistic primacy that sparks and perhaps dominates psychic life; he thus sets up self-deception at the basis of one's relationship to reality. Such a perpetuation of illusion, however, finds itself rehabilitated, neutralized, normalized, at the bosom of my loving reality. For Freud, as we know, binds the state of loving to narcissism; the choice of the love object, be it "narcissistic" or "anaclitic," proves satisfying in any case if and only if that object relates to the subject's narcissism in one of two ways: either through personal narcissistic reward (where Narcissus is the subject), or narcissistic delegation (Narcissus is the other; for Freud, the woman). A narcissistic destiny would in some way underlie all our object choices, but this is a destiny that society on the one hand, and the moral rigor of Freud on the other, tend to thrust aside in favor of a "true" object choice.[2] And yet on closer examination even the Ego Ideal, which insures the transference of our claims and desires toward a true object laden with all the pomp of good and beauty as defined by parental and social codes, is a revival of narcissism, its abeyance, its conciliation, its consolation. Freud's text, one might say, imposes an omnipresence of narcissism which permeates the other realms to the point that one finds it again in the object (where it is reflected)—if we assume that an object can be designated, in other words symbolized and loved as such, outside of chaos, rejection, and destruction.

Moreover, the ubiquity of the notion of "narcissism" goes hand in hand with its being far from originary. It is a supplement, and Freud points out that it is the product of a "new action," which we should understand as that of a third realm supplementing the autoeroticism of the mother-child dyad: "Die autoerotischen Triebe sind aber uhrfänglich; es muss also irgend etwas zum Autoerotismus hinzukommen, eine neue psychische Aktion, um den Narzissmus zu gestalten." "The autoerotic drives, however, are there from the very first; so there must be something added to autoeroticism—a new psychical action—in order to bring about narcissism."[3]

That observation endows narcissism with an intrasymbolic status, dependent upon a third party but within a disposition that chronologically and logically precedes that of the Oedipal Ego. It prompts one to conceive of an archaic disposition of the paternal function, preceding the Name, the Symbolic, but also preceding the "mirror stage" whose logical potentiality it would harbor—a disposition that one might call that of the Imaginary Father (a point I shall return to). Lacan takes up Freud's observation only briefly to emphasize the need to stipulate the "mirror stage." He specifies that "The human ego establishes itself on the basis of the imaginary relation."[4]

The question prompted by the Freudian notion of narcissism would then be the following: what is this narcissistic "identity"? How stable are its borders, its relation to the other? Does the "mirror stage" emerge out of nowhere? What are the conditions of its emergence? A whole complex structuration can seemingly be conceived through what is after all a psychiatric term, "narcissism"; it is an already ternary structuration with a different articulation from the Ego-object-Other triangle that is put together in the shadow of the Oedipus complex.

Furthermore, the ubiquity of Freudian narcissism has caused some to suggest that narcissism is no more than a Freudian fantasy—and that nothing else exists but originary mimetism. Such a thesis is probably a paranoid version of what would lie at the basis of social and symbolic relations: it finds its mechanism in the "scapegoat" theory, where Melanie Klein's "projective relationship" unwittingly serves as a cornerstone for society and the sacred. Nevertheless, it is still a fact that narcissism, caught in a play of rebounds within the Freudian text, in a first stage seems to be a mimetic play that would establish psychic identities (Ego/object), until that play finally, and in the dizziness of rebounds, reveals itself as a screen over emptiness. That notion has been developed in psychoanalysis by André Green, whose reflections I draw upon for this particular point.[5]

Consequently I shall emphasize this notion of emptiness, which is at the root of the human psyche. It does not reveal itself merely because "psychotic states" have broken forth on psychoanalytic couches or have shown through the low points of many neuroses. One is compelled to note that the aims of psychoanalysis have changed. After psychiatric semeiology, Freud had discovered the symptom as metaphor, that is, condensation, of fantasy. Now, and thanks to Lacan, one analyzes the symptom as a screen through which one detects the workings of signifiance (the process of formation and de-formation of meaning and the subject); these coextend with the speaking being as such and, consequently, they cut through not only "normal" and "pathological" states but also psychoanalytic symptomatology. In this respect, the arbitrariness of the Saussurian sign has placed us in front of a bar, or even an emptiness, that constitutes the referent/signified/signifier relationship, of which Lacan has merely taken up the "visible" aspect in the gaping hole of the mirror stage. Saussure's arbitrariness of the sign and Lacan's gaping hole both readily point to what might be understood from the standpoint of representation—given the uneasy uncertainty, ubiquity, and inconsistency of "narcissism" in Freud . . .

Thus, against the background of linguistic theory and language learning, the emptiness that is intrinsic to the beginnings of the symbolic function appears as the first separation between what is not yet an Ego and what is not yet an object. Might narcissism be a means for protecting that emptiness? But against what?—a protection of emptiness (of "arbitrariness," of the "gaping hole") through the display of a decidedly narcissistic parry, so that emptiness can be maintained, lest chaos prevail and borders dissolve. Narcissism protects emptiness, causes it to exist, and thus, as lining of that emptiness, insures an elementary separation. Without that solidarity between emptiness and narcissism, chaos would sweep away any possibility of distinction, trace, and symbolization, which would in turn confuse the limits of the body, words, the real, and the symbolic. The child, with all due respect to Lacan, not only needs the real and the symbolic—it signifies itself as child, in other words as the subject that it is, and neither as a psychotic nor as an adult, precisely in that zone where emptiness and narcissism, the one upholding the other, constitute the zero degree of imagination.

We have, however, reached the threshold of another question: what is it that preserves this emptiness—a cause for complaint but also an absolute necessity of the so-called narcissistic structures, a fleeting effect of enigmatic as well as creative non-sense—at the heart of childhood narcissism? This is where we need to return to the notion of "identification."

Einfühlung—An Identification with a Metaphorical "Object"

Amatory identification, Einfühlung (the assimilation of other people's feelings), appears to be madness when seen in the light of Freud's caustic lucidity: the ferment of collective hysteria in which crowds abdicate their own judgment, a hypnosis that causes us to lose perception of reality since we hand it over to the Ego ideal.[6] The object in hypnosis devours or absorbs the ego, the voice of consciousness becomes blurred, "in loving blindness one becomes a criminal without remorse"—the object has taken the place of what was the ego ideal.[7]

The identification that provides the support for the hypnotic state known as loving madness rests upon a strange object. This archaic identification, which is characteristic of the oral phase of the libido's organization where what I incorporate is what I become, where having amounts to being, is not, truly speaking, objectal. I identify, not with an object, but with what offers itself to me as a model. That enigmatic apprehending of a pattern to be imitated, one that is not yet an object to be libidinally cathected, leads us to wonder whether the loving state is a state without object and reminds us of an archaic reduplication (rather than imitation), "possible before any choice of object."[8] This enigmatic, nonobjectal identification might be related to the internal, recursive, redundant logic of discourse, which is accessible within the "afterspeech"; it is an identification that sets up love, the sign, and repetition at the heart of the psyche. For the sake of an object to come, later or never? . . . It does not matter, since I am already in the throes of Einfühlung . . . Later I shall examine the conditions that allow the advent of that uni-fication, that identification, on the basis of autoeroticism and within the preoedipal triad . . .

For the moment let me simply note that becoming as the One is imagined by Freud as an oral assimilation; indeed he links the possibility of archaic identification to the "oral phase of the libido's organization,"[9] and he then cites Robertson Smith who, in his Kinship and Marriage (1885), describes the communal bonds set up through participation in a common meal as resting upon "the acknowledgment of the possession of a common substance."[10] Ferenczi and his followers would later develop the notions of introjection and incorporation.

Nevertheless, one might well wonder about the notional slippage that takes place between the "incorporation" of an object, or even its "introjection," and an Identifizierung that is not on the level of "having" but locates itself at once on that of "being-like." On what ground, within what material does having switch over to being? While seeking an answer to that question it appeared to me that incorporating and introjecting orality's function is the essential substratum of what constitutes man's being, namely, language. When the object that I incorporate is the speech of the other—precisely a nonobject, a pattern, a model—I bind myself to him in a primary fusion, communion, unification. An identification. For me to have been capable of such a process, my libido had to be restrained; my thirst to devour had to be deferred and displaced to a level one may well call "psychic," provided one adds that if there is repression it is quite primal, and that it lets one hold on to the joys of chewing, swallowing, nourishing oneself . . . with words. In being able to receive the other's words, to assimilate, repeat, and reproduce them, I become like him: One. A subject of enunciation. Through psychic osmosis/identification. Through love.

Freud has described the One with whom I fulfill the identification (this "most primitive aspect of affective binding to an object")[11] as a Father. Although he did not elaborate what he meant by "primary identification," he made it clear that this father is a "father in individual prehistory."

An "Immediate" and Objectless Identification

A strange father if there ever was one, since for Freud, because there is no awareness of sexual difference during that period (more accurately: within that disposition), such a "father" is the same as "both parents." Identification with that "father in prehistory," that Imaginary Father, is called "immediate," "direct," and Freud emphasizes again, "previous to any concentration on any object whatsoever": "Diese scheint zunächst nicht Erfolg oder Ausgang einer Objektbesetzung zu sein, sie ist eine direkte und unmittelbare und frühzeitiger als jede Objektbesetzung." Only with secondary identification does the "libidinal covetousness that is part of the first sexual period and is directed toward the father and the mother appear, in normal instances, to be resolved in a secondary, mediate identification that would come and reinforce the primary, direct identification."[12]

The whole symbolic matrix sheltering emptiness is thus set in place in an elaboration that precedes the Oedipus complex. Indeed, if the primary identification constitutive of the Ego Ideal does not involve libidinal cathexis, drives are dissociated from the psychic realm. Simultaneously, what one can only call the absolute existence of transference is established, a transference laden with libido. It is a transference rather than an "identification," a transference in the sense of Verschiebung, a displacement, as in the Interpretation of Dreams, but also and at the same time in the sense of Übertragung, as it will show up during treatment and be directed toward the person of the analyst. Finally, such a transference is called immediate (unmittelbare) and works in the direction of a complex, composite, and, in short, imaginary realm ("the father in individual prehistory").

We know that, empirically, the first affections, the first imitations, and the first vocalizations as well are directed toward the mother; it is thus hardly necessary to stress that one's pointing to the Father as the magnet for primary love, primary identification, is tenable only if one conceives of identification as being always already within the symbolic orbit, under the sway of language. Such appears to be, implicitly, the Freudian position, which owes its acuity as much to Freud's sensitivity concerning the dominant place of language in the constitution of being as it does to the resurgence of monotheism in his thought. But is there really a difference?

On the contrary, there is Melanie Klein's well-known position, which must be called inexpressible and closer to ordinary common sense. The bold theoretician of the death drive is also a theoretician of gratitude seen as "an important offshoot of the capacity for love," "necessary for the acknowledgment of what is 'good' in others and in oneself."[13] Where does this capacity come from? It is innate and leads to the experience of a "good breast" that sates the child's hunger; it is also apt to convey the feeling of a plenitude that would be the prototype of all subsequent experience of jouissance and happiness. Melanie Klein's gratitude is nevertheless and at the same time directed toward the maternal object in its entirety: "I am not saying that for the child the breast simply represents a physical object."[14]

Yet, along with such innateness, Melanie Klein maintains that the capacity for love is not an activity of the organism (as it would seem to be for Freud, according to Klein) but rather that it is a "primordial activity of the ego." Gratitude would stem from a necessity to confront the forces of death and consist in a "progressive integration born out of the life drive."[15] Without being identical with the "good object," the idealized object reinforces it. "Idealization stems from persecution anguish and constitutes a defense against it," "the ideal breast is a complement of the devouring breast."[16] It is as if those who are unable to set up a "good breast" for themselves naturally manage it by idealizing; now idealization often collapses and reveals its cause, which is the persecution against which it had established itself. But how does one succeed in idealizing? By what miracle is that possible in a Kleinian life where two live without a third party other than a persecuting or fascinating penis?

The problem is not to find an answer to the enigma: who might be the object of primary identification, daddy or mummy? Such an attempt would only open up the impossible quest for the absolute origin of the capacity for love as a psychic and symbolic capacity. The question is rather: of what value would the question be when it actually bears on states existing on the border between the psychic and the somatic, idealization and eroticism, within analytic treatment itself? To emphasize transference, the love that founds the analytic process, implies that one hears the discourse that is performed there starting with that limit of advent-and-loss of the subject—which is Einfühlung.

Provided one does not forget that in analysis any discourse complies with the dynamics of identification, with and beyond resistances, this entails at least two consequences for interpretation. First, the analyst situates himself on a ridge where, on the one hand, the "maternal" position—gratifying needs, "holding" (Winnicott)—and on the other the "paternal" position—the differentiation, distance, and prohibition that produces both meaning and absurdity—are intermingled and severed, infinitely and without end. Analytic tactfulness—ultimate refuge of an interpretation's relevance—is perhaps no more than the capacity to make use of identification and along with it the imaginal resources of the analyst, in order to accompany the patient as far as the limits and accidents of his object relations. This ability is even more important precisely when the patient has difficulty in establishing, or fails to establish, an object relation.

Metonymic Object and Metaphorical Object

Secondly, the Einfühlung gives the language signifier exchanged during treatment a heterogeneous, drive-affected dimension. It loads it with something preverbal, or even nonrepresentable that needs to be deciphered while taking into account the more precise articulations of discourse (style, grammar, phonetics), and at the same time while cutting through language, in the direction of the unspeakable, indicated by fantasies and "insight" narratives as well as by symptomatic misspeech (slips of the tongue, illogical statements, etc.).

Such analytic attentiveness to Einfühlung through transference speech imposes another status of the psychic object on the analyst's attention, one that is different from the metonymic object of desire called "object 'a'" by Lacan.[17]

We are dealing less with a partial object than with a nonobject. As magnet of identification constitutive of identity and condition for that unification, which insures the advent of a subject for an object, the "object" of Einfühlung is a metaphorical object. Carrying autoerotic motility to the unifying image of One Agency that already sets me up as an opposite One is the zero degree of subjectivity. Metaphor should be understood as movement toward the discernible, a journey toward the visible. Anaphora, gesture, indication, would probably be more adequate terms for this sundered unity, in the process of being set up, which I am presently conjuring. Aristotle refers to an epiphora: a generic term for the metaphorical motility previous to any objectivation of a figurative meaning . . . The object of love is a metaphor for the subject—its constitutive metaphor, its "unary feature," which, by having it choose an adored part of the loved one, already locates it within the symbolic code of which this feature is a part.[18] Nevertheless, situating this unifying guideline within an objectality in the process of being established rather than in the absolute of the reference to the Phallus as such has several advantages. It makes the transference relation dynamic, involves to the utmost the interpretative intervention of the analyst, and calls attention to countertransference as identification, this time of the analyst with the patient, along with the entire aura of imaginary formations germane to the analyst that all this entails. Without those conditions doesn't analysis run the risk of becoming set within the tyranny of idealization, precisely? Of the Phallus or of the superego? A word to wise Lacanians should be enough!

Metonymic object of desire. Metaphorical object of love. The former controls the phantasmatic narrative. The latter outlines the crystallization of fantasy and rules the poeticalness of the discourse of love . . .

During treatment, the analyst interprets his desire and his love, and that sets him apart from the perverse position of the seducer and from that of a virtuous Werther as well. But he must display himself some times as desiring, other times as loving. By ensuring a loving Other to the patient, the analyst (temporarily) allows the Ego in the throes of drive to take shelter in the following fantasy: the analyst is not a dead Father but a living Father; this nondesiring but loving father reconciles the ideal Ego with the Ego Ideal and elaborates the psychic space where, possibly and subsequently, an analysis can take place.

Henceforth, the analyst must in addition let it be known—since he is an analyst and neither a good shepherd nor a fatherconfessor—that he is a fleeting, failing, or even abject subject of desire. He will then trigger within the psychic space his love has allowed to exist the tragicomedy of life drives and death drives, knowing in his nescience that if Eros opposes Thanatos they are not evenly matched in their struggle. For Thanatos is pure while Eros has, since the beginning, been permeated with Thanatos, the most deep-seated drive being the death drive (Freud).

To say that the analyst handles love as a discourse allowing idealizing distance as a condition for the very existence of psychic space is not to assimilate the analytic attitude to that of a primary love object, the archaic prototype of the genital love, as Balint's work suggests with seductive munificence.[19] Concentrating, for a while, one's thoughts on love within analysis actually leads one to scrutinize, in the treatment, not a narcissistic merger with the maternal container but the emergence of a metaphorical object—in other words the very splitting that establishes the psyche and, let us call this splitting "primal repression," bends the drive toward the symbolic of an other. Only the metaphorical dynamics (in the sense of a heterogeneous displacement shattering the isotopy of organic needs) justifies that this other be a Great Other. The analyst thus temporarily stands in the place of the Great Other inasmuch as he is a metaphorical object of idealizing identification. It is in knowing this and doing it that he creates the space of transference. If he represses it, on the other hand, the analyst becomes the Führer that Freud already loathed in Group Psychology—a loathing that showed to what extent analytic practice was not exempt from such hysterical phenomena.

Hate Identification, Love Identification

"It is easy," Freud believed,

to translate into a phrase the difference between identification with the father and affection for the father as sexual object (der Unterschied einer solchen Vateridentifizierung von einer Vaterobjektwahl): in the first instance the father is what one would want to be (das, was man sein möchte), in the second he is what one would want to have (das, was man haben möchte). In the first instance, it is the subject of the ego that is concerned, in the second it is its object. That is why identification is possible before any choice of object is made (Es ist also der Unterschied, ob die Bindung am Subjekt oder am Objekt des Ichs angreift. Die erstere ist darum bereits vor jeder sexuellen Objektwahl möglich).[20]

It will be noted that the first identification Freud points to in this study is a morbid identification with the mother (for instance, the little girl takes up her mother's cough on account of "a hostile desire to take the mother's place—ein feindseliges Ersetzenwollen der Mutter—in which case the symptom expresses the erotic fondness for the father"). Though conceived within the system of the Oedipus complex (Entweder ist die Identifizierung dieselbe aus dem Ödipuskomplex), such an identification nevertheless reminds one of Melanie Klein's projective identification, which is sustained by the "hostile" as well as guilt-ridden desire to take the place of a persecuting mother out of envy. Object identification because of hatred for one part of the object and fear of persecution. The second type of identification is revealed by a symptom that apes that of the loved one (the daughter, Dora, catches the father's cough). Here, "identification has taken the place of erotic propensity, and the latter has been changed, through regression, into identification" (die Identifizierung sei an Stelle der Objektwahl getreten, die Objektwahl sei zur Identifizierung regrediert). Without hostility in this case, identification coincides with the object of desire through "a kind of insertion of the object into the ego" (gleichsam durch Introjektion des Objekts ins Ich). Love, contrary to the morbid identification mentioned above, would be the merging of the identifying ideal with the object of desire. In the third place, libidinal desires can be completely lacking when identification with another person is made on the basis of some common traits.

One is thus led to conceive of at least two identifications; a primal one, resulting from a sentimental (Gefühlsbindung an ein Objekt), archaic, and ambivalent affection for the maternal object, more frequently produced by the impetus of guilt-producing hostility; and the other, which underlies the introjection into the ego of an object itself already libidinal (libidinöse Objektbindung), providing the dynamics of the pure loving relationship. The first is closer to depersonalization, phobia, and psychosis; the second is closer to hysterical lovehate, taking to itself the phallic ideal that it pursues.

Between Hysteria and Inability to Love

The lover is a narcissist with an object. Love involves a sizable aufhebung of narcissism; consequently, the relationship established by Freud between love and narcissism must not cause us to forget their essential difference. Is it not true that the narcissist, as such, is precisely someone incapable of love?

The lover, in fact, reconciles narcissism and hysteria. As far as he is concerned, there is an idealizable other who returns his own ideal image (that is the narcissistic moment), but he is nevertheless an other. It is essential for the lover to maintain the existence of that ideal other and to be able to imagine himself similar, merging with him, and even indistinguishable from him. In amorous hysteria the ideal Other is a reality, not a metaphor. The archeology of such an identifying possibility with an other is provided by the huge place taken up within narcissistic structure by the vortex of primary identification with what Freud called a "father of individual prehistory." Endowed with the sexual attributes of both parents, and by that very token a totalizing, phallic figure, it provides satisfactions that are already psychic and not simply immediate, existential requests; that archaic vortex of idealization is immediately an other who gives rise to a powerful, already psychic transference of the previous semiotic body in the process of becoming a narcissistic Ego. Its very existence and my being able to take myself for it—that is what already moves us away from the primal maternal satisfaction and situates us within the hysterical universe of loving idealization.

It is obvious from the behavior of young children that the first love object of boys and girls is the mother. Then where does one fit in this "father of individual prehistory"? Freud's bent perhaps causes him to speak as a Jew, but he speaks primarily as a psychoanalyst. He in fact dissociates idealization (and with it the amatory relationship) from the bodily exchange between mother and child, and he introduces the Third Party as a condition of psychic life, to the extent that it is a loving life. If love stems from narcissistic idealization, it has nothing to do with the protective wrapping over skin and sphincters that maternal care provides for the baby. Worse yet, if that protection continues, if the mother "clings" to her offspring, laying on it the request that originates in her own request as confused neoteinic and hysteric in want of love, the chances are that neither love nor psychic life will ever hatch from such an egg. The loving mother, different from the caring and clinging mother, is someone who has an object of desire; beyond that, she has an Other with relation to whom the child will serve as go-between. She will love her child with respect to that Other, and it is through a discourse aimed at that Third Party that the child will be set up as "loved" for the mother. "Isn't he beautiful," or "I am proud of you," and so forth, are statements of maternal love because they involve a Third Party; it is in the eyes of a Third Party that the baby the mother speaks to becomes a he, it is with respect to others that "I am proud of you," and so forth. Against this verbal backdrop or in the silence that presupposes it the bodily exchange of maternal fondness may take on the imaginary burden of representing love in its most characteristic form. Nevertheless, without the maternal "diversion" toward a Third Party, the bodily exchange is abjection or devouring; the eventual schizophrene, whether phobic or borderline, will keep its hot-iron brand against which his only recourse will be hatred. Any borderline person ends up finding a mother who is "loving" for her own sake, but he cannot accept her as loving himself, for she did not love any other one. The Oedipal negation of the father is here linked with a complaint against an adhesive maternal wrapping, and it leads the subject toward psychic pain dominated by the inability to love.

If one grants the ternary structure of narcissism and its already harboring the hysterical beginning of an idealizable object (the object of love germane to primary identification), how can one, to the contrary, understand the inability to love? The cold, set, and somewhat false complaint of the borderline person that he is unable to love needs perhaps to be related not to narcissism but to autoeroticism. Previous to the "new psychic action" that includes a third party within narcissism, the autoerotic set-up has neither an other nor an image. All of its figures, all figures disappoint it as much as they fascinate it. The autoerotic person cannot allow himself to be "loved" (no more than he can let himself be lovable), except by a maternal substitute who would cling to his body like a poultice—a reassuring balm, asthmogenic perhaps, but nevertheless a permanent wrapping. Such a false mother is the only "farthering" [père-manence] tolerated by one who, henceforth, will indolently be able to enjoy his own organs in polymorphous perversity. He is undifferentiated, set within the shattered territories of his parceled body, coiled up about his erogenous zones. He is indifferent to love, withdrawn in the pleasure that a provisionally reassuring diving-suit gives him. The autoerotic person is not, however, autistic: he discovers objects, but they are objects of hatred. Nevertheless, during those moments that have no saving grace and when the subject is deprived of durability, the hatred that an opposite object projects before him works indeed more strongly upon himself, threatening him with decomposition or petrifaction. The autoerotic person who complains or boasts of being unable to love is afraid of going mad—schizophrenia or catatonia . . .

Dynamics of the Ideal

The subject exists only inasmuch as it identifies with an ideal other who is the speaking other, the other insofar as he speaks. A ghost,[21] a symbolic formation beyond the mirror, this Other who is indeed the size of a Master, is a magnet for identification because he is neither an object of need nor one of desire. The Ego ideal includes the Ego on account of the love that this Ego has for it and thus unifies it, restrains its drives, turns it into a Subject. An Ego is a body to be put to death, or at least to be deferred, for the love of the Other and so that Myself can be. Love is a death sentence that causes me to be. When death, which is intrinsic to amorous passion, takes place in reality and carries away the body of one of the lovers, it is at its most unbearable; the surviving lover then realizes the abyss that separates the imaginary death that he experienced in his passion from the relentless reality from which love had forever set him apart: saved . . .

The subject's identification with the symbolic Other, with its Ego Ideal, goes through a narcissistic absorption of the mother as object of need, an absorption that sets up the Ideal Ego. The lover is cognizant of the regression that leads him from adoring an ideal ghost to the ecstatic or painful inflating of his own image, his own body.

Such a logic of idealizing identification leads one to posit, as lining of the visual, specular structure of the fantasy ($ ◊ a) in search of the ever inadequate image of a desired other, the existence of a preliminary condition. If the object of fantasy is receding, metonymical, it is because it does not correspond to the preliminary ideal that the identification process, $ ε A, has constructed. The subject exists because it belongs to the Other, and it is in proceeding from that symbolic belonging that causes him to be subject to love and death that he will be able to set up for himself imaginary objects of desire. Transferred to the Other ($ ε A) as to the very place from which he is seen and heard, the loving subject does not have access to that Other as to an object, but as to the very possibility of the perception, distinction, and differentiation that allows one to see. That Ideal is nevertheless a blinding, nonrepresentable power—sun or ghost. Romeo says, ". . . Juliet is the sun," and that loving metaphor transfers onto Juliet the glare Romeo experiences in the state of love, dedicating his body to death in order to become immortal within the symbolic community of others restored by his love, precisely.

The ideal identification with the Symbolic upheld by the Other thus activates speech more than image. Doesn't the signifying voice, in the final analysis, shape the visible, hence fantasy? Whenever we observe how young children learn forms, we are led to understand to what extent "sensorimotor spontaneity" is of little avail without the help of language. Poets have known from time immemorial that music is the language of love, and it has led them to suggest that the yearning captured by the loved beauty is nevertheless transcended—preceded and guided—by the ideal signifier: a sound on the fringe of my being, which transfers me to the place of the Other, astray, beyond meaning, out of sight.[22]

In short, identification causes the subject to exist within the signifier of the Other. Archaically, primitively, it is not object-oriented but carried out as transference to the place of a captivating and unifying feature, a "unary feature." The analyst is an object (necessarily a partial one) but he also exerts the drawing power of a "unary feature," of a nonobject: the actual drifting of a possible metaphoricity.

Here the term metaphor should not bring to mind the classic rhetorical trope (figurative vs. plain), but instead, on the one hand, the modern theories of metaphor that decipher within it an indefinite jamming of semantic features one into the other, a meaning being acted out; and, on the other, the drifting of heterogeneity within a heterogeneous psychic apparatus, going from drives and sensations to signifier and conversely.[23]

Since it is not object-oriented, identification reveals how the subject that ventures there can finally find himself a hypnotized slave of his master; how he can turn out to be a nonsubject, the shadow of a nonobject. Nevertheless, it is because identification is not object-oriented that the signifier's nonobject-oriented underlying layer of drives becomes activated during the treatment that is carried out without the Einfühlung being repressed. In such a case, therefore, it is possible for transference to gain a hold on nonobject-oriented psychic states such as "false selves," borderline cases, and even psychosomatic symptoms. It is indeed true that one is ill when not loved; this means that a psychic structure that lacks an identifying metaphor or idealization tends to realize it in that embodied nonobject called somatic symptom—illness. Somatic persons are not those who do not verbalize, they are subjects who lack or miscarry the dynamics of metaphoricity, which constitute idealization as a complex process.

Finally, being the magnet for loving identification causes the Other to be understood not as a "pure signifier" but as the very space of metaphorical shifting: a condensation of semantic features as well as nonrepresentable drive heterogeneity that subtends them, goes beyond them, and slips away. Actually, by stressing the partiality of the "unary feature" during idealizing identification, Lacan located idealization solely within the field of the signifier and of desire; he clearly if not drastically separated it from narcissism as well as from drive heterogeneity and its archaic hold on the maternal vessel. To the contrary, by emphasizing the metaphoricity of the identifying idealization movement, we can attempt to restore to the analytic bond located there (transference and countertransference) its complex dynamic, which includes the narcissistic, drive-animated pre-object-orientation and allows it to be tied down to signifying ideals. From this standpoint, there would be no analytical idealization that did not rest upon sublimation. In other words, psychoanalysis skirts religious faith in order to expend it in the form of literary discourse.

Immediate and Absolute

Freud's definition of "primary identification" as "direct and immediate" (direkte und unmittelbare)[24] has not, as far as I know, aroused the attention of analysts. In light of that phrase, let us reflect for a moment on the value that speculative philosophy, particularly that of Hegel, assigns to such immediacy.

The immanent presence of the Absolute in Knowing is immediately revealed to the Subject as the recognition of that which never left him. More specifically, the Hegelian immediate (Unmittelbare) is the ultimate disengagement of consistency for the sake of form, the internal overthrow of reflection-in-itself, matter being removed from the self, without yet being for itself and hence for the other. Hegel notes in his Science of Logic, "Immediacy, which, as reflection-in-itself, is consistency (Bestehen) as well as form, reflection on something else, reflection doing away with itself."[25] Heidegger, in his text on Hegel's Introduction to the Phenomenology of the Spirit entitled "Hegel and His Concept of Experience" wished to investigate that immediate presence of the Absolute in order to show the a priori or arbitrariness of the "immediate" and reveal, on both its far and near sides, the "blossoming of the Logos," dear to Heideggerian discourse. Within the scope of these reflections, one might maintain that the immediate, being the autoseverance of certainty in the self, is at the same time that which severs it from object-relation and bestows on it its power of acquittance (Absolvenz) without mediation, without object, but keeping and containing both; hence the immediate is the very logic of parousia, that is, the presence of the subject for the object. "It behooves him to keep any relation that merely pertains to the object . . ." is Heidegger's comment. As the most basic indication of parousia, the immediate also presents itself as the logic of Absolvenz, as severance outside of relationship, and constitutes the absoluteness of the absolute. "It is there, in auto-representation, that the parousia of the absolute is displayed" (ibid.).[26]

In other words, the presence of the Absolute in Knowing is immediately revealed to the subject; consequently, any other "means" of knowledge is no more than a recognition. "The absolute is from the outset in and for itself beside us and wants to be beside us," Hegel states in his Introduction to the Phenomenology. Such a being-beside-us would be "the manner in which the light of the truth of the absolute itself enlightens us," as Heidegger says in his commentary. We are immediately within parousia, "always-already," before producing a "relationship" to it.

Let us put aside the visual aspect, be it imagined or imaginary, of that immediacy of the Absolute, which Heidegger enabled us to hear when he unfolded the word for knowledge [Wissenschaft] in its sonorousness [novisse, to have a knowledge of, viso, to look at], and which Lacan emphasized when he placed the mirror at the core of the Ego's formation. Let me first stress that specular fascination is a belated phenomenon in the genesis of the Ego. And let us try to think through the philosophical investigation against the backdrop of what the analyst might see in the appearance of the term "immediate" at the heart of primary identification.

With Freud, the arbitrariness of paternal emergence seems undeniable, at any rate absolutely necessary to the interpretative analytic construction. Nevertheless, clinical experience has led us to ascertain that the advent of the Vater der persönlichen Vorzeit takes place thanks to the assistance of the so-called pre-Oedipal mother, to the extent that she can indicate to her child that her desire is not limited to responding to her offspring's request (or simply turning it down). This assistance is none other than maternal desire for the Father's Phallus.

Which one? The child's father or her own? For "primary identification" the question is not relevant. If there is an immediacy of the child's identification with that desire (of the Father's Phallus), it probably comes from the child's not having to elaborate it; rather, he receives it, mimics it, or even sustains it through the mother who offers it to him (or refuses it) as a gift. In a way, such an identification with the father-mother conglomerate, as Freud would have it, or with what we have just called the maternal desire for the Phallus, comes as a godsend. And for a very good reason, since without that disposition of the psyche, the child and the mother do not yet constitute "two" . . .

As for the image making up this "imagination," it should not be conceived as simply visual but as a representation activating various facilitations corresponding to the entire gamut of perceptions, especially the sonorous ones; this because of their precocious appearance in the domain of neuropsychological maturation, but also because of their dominant function in speech.

Nevertheless, let us not be mistaken about the ease of such an immediacy. It entails an important consequence: within that logic, the word "object," just like the word "identification," becomes improper. A not-yet-identity (of the child) is transferred or rather displaced to the site of an Other who is not libidinally cathected as an object but remains an Ego Ideal.

Not I

Let me now point out that the most archaic unity that we thus retrieve—an identity so autonomous that it calls forth displacements—is that of the Phallus desired by the mother. It is the unity of the imaginary father, a coagulation of the mother and her desire. The imaginary father would thus be the indication that the mother is not complete but that she wants . . . Who? What? The question has no answer other than the one that uncovers narcissistic emptiness; "At any rate, not I." Freud's famous "What does a woman want" is perhaps only the echo of the more fundamental "What does a mother want?" It runs up against the same impossibility, bordered on one side by the imaginary father, on the other by a "not I." And it is out of this "not I" (see Beckett's play with that title) that an Ego painfully attempts to come into being . . .

In order to maintain himself in that place, to assume the leap that will definitely anchor him in the imaginary father and in language or even in art, the speaking being must engage in a struggle with the imaginary mother, for whom it will eventually constitute an object separated from the Ego. But we are not at that stage yet. The immediate transference toward the imaginary father, who is such a godsend that you have the impression that it is he who is transferred into you, withstands a process of rejection involving what may have been chaos and is about to become an abject. The maternal space can come into being as such, before becoming an object correlative to the Ego's desire, only as an abject.

In short, primary identification appears to be a transference to (from) the imaginary father, correlative to the establishment of the mother as "ab-jetted." Narcissism would be that correlation (with the imaginary father and the "ab-jetted" mother) enacted around the central emptiness of that transference. This emptiness, which is apparently the primer of the symbolic function, is precisely encompassed in linguistics by the bar separating signifier from signified and by the "arbitrariness" of the sign, or in psychoanalysis by the "gaping" of the mirror.

If narcissism is a defense against the emptiness of separation, then the whole contrivance of imagery, representations, identifications, and projections that accompany it on the way toward strengthening the Ego and the Subject is a means of exorcising that emptiness. Separation is our opportunity to become narcists or narcissistic, at any rate subjects of representation. The emptiness it opens up is nevertheless also the barely covered abyss where our identities, images, and words run the risk of being engulfed.

The mythical Narcissus would heroically lean over that emptiness to seek in the maternal watery element the possibility of representing the self or the other—someone to love. Beginning with Plotinus at least (see Five), theoretical thought has forgotten that it rumbled along over emptiness before lovingly springing toward the solar source of representation, the light that enables us to see and with which we aspire to become equal, idealization following upon idealization, perfecting upon perfecting: In lumine tuo videbimus lumen. Psychotic persons, however, remind us, in case we had forgotten, that the representational contrivances that cause us to speak, elaborate, or believe rest upon emptiness. Possibly the most radical atheists are those who, not knowing what the ability to represent owes to a Third Party, remain prisoners of the archaic mother, for whom they mourn in the suffering of emptiness.

Within sight of that Third Party I elaborate the narcissistic parry that allows me to block up that emptiness, to calm it and turn it into a producer of signs, representations, and meanings, I elaborate it within sight of the Third Party. I seduce this "father of individual prehistory" because he has already caught me, for he is simple virtuality, a potential presence, a form to be cathected. Always already there, the forming presence that nonetheless satisfies none of my autoerotic needs draws me into the imaginary exchange, the specular seduction. He or I—who is the agent? Or even, is it he or is it she? The immanence of its transcendence, as well as the instability of our borders before the setting of my image as "my own," turn the murky source (eine neue psychische Aktion) from which narcissism will flow into a dynamics of confusion and delight. Secrets of our loves.

The Ideal Ego sated with the Ego Ideal will take over from that alchemy and strengthen the defenses of the narcissistic Ego. Consciousness, along with moral conscience (that stern and precious paternal inheritance), will not truly lead us, under the tyrannical protection of the Superego, to forget the narcissistic emptiness and its surface composed of imaginary recognitions and cathexes. At least it will help us block them up; they always remain as more or less painful wounds at the heart of our functions, successes, or failures. Beneath homosexual libido, which our social objectives catch and maintain captive, the chasms of narcissistic emptiness spread out; although the latter can be a powerful motive for ideal or superegotic cathexis, it is also the primary source of inhibition.

In being narcissistic one has already throttled the suffering of emptiness. The fragility, however, of the narcissistic elaboration, underpinning the ego image as well as ideal cathexes, is such that its cracks immediately reveal the negative of our image films to those that others consider to be "narcissistic." More than insane, empty, that lining of our projection and representation devices is yet another defense of the living being. When he succeeds in eroticizing it, when he allows the nonobject-oriented, prenarcissistic violence of the drive directed toward an abject to run wild, then death triumphs in that strange path. The death drive and its psychological equivalent, hatred, is what Freud discovers after stopping off at Narcissus. Narcissism and its lining, emptiness, are in short our most intimate, brittle, and archaic elaborations of the death drive. The most advanced, courageous, and threatened sentries of primal repression.

In contrast with Melanie Klein's "projective identification," the proposition I am offering here has the advantage of pointing to, even before the Oedipal triangle and within a specific disposition, the place of the Third Party; without the latter, the phase Melanie Klein calls "schizo-paranoid" could not become a "depressive" phase and thus could not carry the "symbolic equivalences" to the level of linguistic "signs." The archaic inscription of the father seems to me a way of modifying the fantasy of a phallic mother playing at the phallus game all by herself, alone and complete, in the back room of Kleinism and post-Kleinism.

As for language, the notion outlined here differs, furthermore, from innatist theories concerning linguistic competence (Chomsky) as well as from Lacanian notions of an always-already-there of language that would be revealed as such in the subject of the unconscious. I of course assume, with respect to the infans, that the symbolic function preexists, but also maintain an evolutionary postulate that leads me to seek to elaborate various dispositions giving access to that function, and this corresponds as well to various psychic structures.

In the light of the above, what I have called a "narcissistic structuration" appears to be the earliest juncture (chronologically and logically) whose spoors we might detect in the unconscious. Conversely, understanding narcissism as origin or as undecomposable, unanalyzable screen leads the analyst (no matter what theoretical warnings might be given in other respects) to present his interpretative discourse as a haven, either comforting or confrontingly aggressive, for a narcissism that thus finds itself recognized and renewed. Whether comforting or authenticating (by rational criticism, for instance, in interpretations of the "mental process" sort), such a welcome falls into the trap of narcissism and seldom succeeds in leading it through the Oedipal procession on to the topology of a complex subject.

In fact, clinical practitioners like Winnicot protected themselves against such a danger, if only by always advocating a mixture of "narcissistic" and "Oedipal" interpretations in so-called psychotic states. Nevertheless, if the dead end that has just been noted can be encountered by others, the reason for it must probably be located in a basic omission—that of the agency of the imaginary father from the start of primary identification, an agency of which "projective identification" is a more belated consequence (logically and chronologically). One may still reach that dead end, by the same token, if one ignores the very concrete and specific structuration required by psychicism within that very elementary disposition, which the term "narcissism" threatens to reduce to a fascination for what is nothing but the mother's phallus.

Persian or Christian

The dynamics of primary identification, which structures emptiness and object as what may have appeared as a "narcissistic screen," will allow us to examine another enigmatic juncture on the Freudian path.

Freud's uneasiness concerning Christianity is well known, and his rationality would not let him put it into words with respect to revealed religion, but, dazzled and prudent, he did express it when faced with Persian religion. "The sun-drenched face of the young Persian god has remained incomprehensible to us."[27] It is indeed possible to interpret that refulgent jouissance as "direct and immediate" primary identification with the Phallus desired by the mother; this amounts neither to being the mother's Phallus nor entering the Oedipal drama. A certain phantasmatic incestuous potentiality is thus set aside; it works from the place of the imaginary father and constitutes the basis of imagination itself. Moreover, the subsequent naming of that relationship perhaps represents the conditions of sublimation.

In Freud's text, the "refulgent and incomprehensible" face lacking an Oedipal feeling of fault or guilt would be that of the leader of the horde of brothers who kills the father and boasts of his feat (as Ernest Jones suggests).[28] One might, on the other hand, consider a pre- or non-Oedipal disposition of that jouissance; a position of symbolicity that stems from primary identification, coupled with what the latter infers in regard to sexual nondifferentiation (father-and-mother, man-and-woman) and immediate transference to the site of maternal desire. That would constitute a fragile inscription of subjecthood, one which, under the subsequent Oedipal sway, would retain no more than a phantasmatic status. In addition, such a warm but dazzling, domesticated paternity includes imaginary exultation as well as a risk of dissolving identities that the Freudian Oedipal process alone ends up strengthening, in the ideal hypothesis of course.

Maintaining against the winds and high tides of our modern civilization the requirement of a stern father who, through his Name, brings about separation, judgment, and identity, constitutes a necessity, a more or less pious wish. But we can only note that jarring such sternness, far from leaving us orphaned or inexorably psychotic, reveals multiple and varied destinies for paternity—notably archaic, imaginary paternity. Those destinies could or can be manifested by the clan as a whole, by the priest, or by the therapist. In all cases, however, we are dealing with a function that guarantees the subject's entry into a disposition, a fragile one to be sure, of an ulterior, unavoidable oedipal destiny, but one that can also be playful and sublimational.

Seducer or Ideal Father

The tragic dynamic of the Father's idealization is taken up again in Moses and Monotheism through the theme of the election of the Jewish people by its God and through the story of Moses. There is nothing to make one conceive this election as a revival of the old idea, subsequently abandoned by Freud, of the father as the hysterical person's first seducer. The father who brings a people into being through his love is perhaps indeed closer to the "father of individual prehistory," and, at any rate, to the idealizing agency that drains early identifications, not as object but as "unary feature." One might nevertheless interpret Freudian thought with respect to this loving father in the following fashion. The hysterical structure of the horde of brothers construes him as a seducer, an agent of the libido, of Eros, and puts him to death; this is Moses' murdered body. Yet there is also a structural necessity for his unique love as symbolic choice; it appears later on as a pressing need to lay down moral rules or a right to the tribe. The father will then be recognized not as seducer but as Law, as an abstract agency of the One that selects our identifying and idealizing power. The Christian trinity, for its part, reconciles the seducer and the legislator by inventing another form of love—agape, symbolic (nominal, spiritual) from the very start and corporeal, absorbing the acknowledged murder of the erotic body into the universalist profusion of the symbolist distinction for everyone (brother or stranger, faithful or sinner). (See Four, "God Is Love.")

What is opposed to the recognition of the imaginary father? What is it that produces its repression, or even its burial? Freud drops the word "character," with its well-known anal connotations. "Whatever resistance character might later be able to bring to bear against the influence of abandoned sexual objects, the effects of earlier identifications, carried out in the most precocious stages of life, will always keep their general, lasting features."[29]

Character is one of the limits to what is analyzable, and that is confirmed by the difficulties encountered in the region we are now investigating. Furthermore, because of the anal character's resistance against primary identification, the advent of the abject during treatment can clearly be seen as the first breach in resistance . . . Nevertheless and above all it is Oedipal rivalry, which creates mediations, that tragically darkens the dazzlement of primary identification. Within the Oedipus complex, the question is no longer "Who is it?" but "Who has it?"; the narcissistic question "Am I?" becomes a possessive or attributive question, "Have I?" It is nonetheless true that by starting from Oedipal dramas and their failures—backwards, in other words—one will be able to detect the particulars of primary identification. It is to be noted, however, that "boundary states" lead us there directly, locating the Oedipal conflict as ulterior or secondary.

A boy will have difficulty tearing himself away from the petrifying situation of being his mother's phallus; or if he succeeds, through the maternal grandfather (among others) who has come in between, he will never cease waging war against his brothers in the shadow of an inaccessible father. Only in poetic enunciation will it be possible for him to be son-and-father within the immediate and direct disposition of primary identification, and bypassing sexual difference—witness the troubadours and Joyce. As for the girl, she will retain the traces of that primary transference only if assisted by a father having a maternal character, who nevertheless will not be of much help in her breaking away from the mother and finding a heterosexual object. She will thus tend to bury that primary identification under the disappointed feverishness of the homosexual, or else in abstraction, which, as it flies away from the body, fully constitutes itself as "soul" or fuses with an Idea, a Love, a Self-sacrifice . . . If ever a jouissance remains, it still seems to partake of that archaic differentiation that Freud so delicately and elliptically touched on under the heading of "primary identification."

"Narcissistic structure" thus remains a permanent fixture in the love grievances that beckon to us . . .

John, the Ferryman, and Emptiness

John comes into analysis with the complaints of borderline cases, which have been fully catalogued by Winnicot, Fairbairn, and Rosenfeld—false self, sexual impotence, professional dissatisfaction. His discourse seems to pay tribute to fashion, of which he is yet largely unaware, when he plays with signifiers, deals with words as if they were objects, or proceeds by fragmentary, illogical, chaotic sentence concatenations; thus, after having lived and talked so much, he gives the impression of being empty. The theme of emptiness, explicit during the treatment of this man, generates multiple metaphors and configurations, all centered in the mother, for which he never uses the possessive adjective. As if repression were problematic, all incestuous as well as murderous contents are present in his discourse. Nevertheless, if they have a meaning, they have no signification for this patient. Within the empty enclosure of his narcissism, contents (drives and representations) could not find an other (an addressee) who, alone, might have given a signification to their weighty meaning that is still felt as empty because it is deprived of love. Transference caused two elements to surface out of the void, and they allowed the long walk around the Oedipal problematics.

First there was the outbreak of abjection.[30] Either desired or to be killed, the mother was embodied only as abject, repulsive, decked out with all the details of a previously frozen anality. In the same way, and still protected by an explicitly idealizing transference, the patient transforms the uncertain boundaries between what is not yet an Ego and what is not yet an Other by filling this not-yet-an-Ego with "abjects," thus bringing it out of emptiness, and then giving it only a narcissistic consistency. "I am repulsive, therefore I can be." Neither subject nor object, both ab-jects each in his or her turn, mother and son painfully separate all through the initial stage of the treatment, necessarily activating the body's boundaries (skin or sphincters), fluids, and ejections, so that passing symptoms might find a place in them. I saw that elementary structuration of narcissism as preceding any possibility of "projective identification," which, although diffuse during the first phase of the treatment, did not appear essential (it had a meaning but no signification); only later could it be elaborated and interpreted.

Meanwhile, and this is the second noteworthy element corresponding to the advent of the abject, the patient has a dream. In order to shield himself from his mother's lover who attracts his Oedipal identifications, John races away frantically, but he is losing ground when an old man, who resembles a saint, miraculously shows up. "It is Christopher, I think, the one who carries the child Jesus, who lifts me upon his shoulders and takes me across the bridge. He carries me, but my own feet are doing the walking . . ." The following sessions evoke John's father, who died when he was very young, but also the maternal uncle and grandfather, with whom he had spent his early years. The father, who had been disparaged up to now, averred absent or of no account, is shyly silhouetted in the patient's talk as an "unassuming intellectual," "movie buff," "reader of James" ("strange for an unpretentious clerk, reading works like that"); this to uphold him in his struggle against the abject, thus giving him stabler boundaries, selves that last a little longer before appearing to be false, conflictual landmarks that blaze out the whiteness of a narcissism whose emptiness he initially deplored.

Unlike Freud's patients, the borderline speaks of Eros but dreams of Agape. What was interpreted as a "problematic repression," or even as a "lack of repression" in such patients appears to be rather another position of repression. With the borderline patient, a negation weighs above all and heavily on primary identification. To say that this indicates a "repudiation of the Name of the Father" is too sweeping and inaccurate, if only because of the existence of transference and, following upon the treatment, the emergence of the Oedipus complex, which can be more or less analyzed. But that repression reminds one if anything of a negation of Agape (I shall use this term as synonymous to primary identification), with everything this implies concerning repression of homosexuality when a man is involved; it modifies the status of those representations linked to repressed erotic drives and mainly to erotic relationships within the dual relationship (including "projective identification"). Consequently, affect representatives pass through the censorship of repression and appear within discourse as empty, without signification. Discourse itself undergoes an analogous process; laden with drives, it is nevertheless experienced as "castrated," John says, without consistency, empty, too, for want of that elementary, archaic Third Party who could have been its addressee and, by receiving it, could have authenticated it. If all that remains is an Oedipal Father, a Symbolic Father, no struggle against the "abject," no becoming autonomous with respect to the phallic mother, could be inscribed in the body of language.

The analyst, along this route, is summoned in place of the imaginary father, especially (and this is what the borderline patient dreams of) in order for him or her, apprehensively, to serve as a support for abjection.

Marie and the Absence of the Mother

Marie exhibits all the delightful throes of hysteria: demand following upon demand, affirmation upon affirmation, until she encounters "total failure," which yet leaves her "cold," although dramatically restless, apprehensive, distressed . . . "It's amazing, I'm constantly struck by the futility of it all." This does not, however, spare her the symptom that prompts her to come into analysis—a suffocation that grips her as soon as she sits at the wheel of her car. Marie's story is not an ordinary one. Abandoned by her mother, who disappeared during the war, she was first taken care of by her father's family and then put out to nurse. The father remarries and, "completely terrorized by his wife," Marie says, sees his daughter only rarely; to him she is the burden of a youthful error whom he is ready to support but not to love. The mother's real absence raises to the highest pitch idealization and hatred toward her, with nothing left but the latter when, at age 25, Marie meets with and is disappointed by the family of the one who never ceases not to have room for her. Marie's relationships with women are frequent, conflictive, and "insignificant": "That doesn't interest me," she says, after having hundreds of times duplicated her "symptom," as she puts it, when going to visit those women. But she holds back, expresses nothing, raises no objection—"totally masochistic, you can say that again." Concern for an essential narcissistic protection makes her "obliging, friendly, kind," whereas her two ("never fewer than two," Marie specifies) sexual partners with whom she maintains alternating separate and conflictive relationships allow her to lose nothing of either the structure or the bounties of her childhood, and they restore to her a completion that is sometimes "suffocating" but very satisfying, above all during the quarrels of the threesome.

Out of the central emptiness of narcissism that the story of Marie outlines perhaps too straightforwardly (but how many actual, adored or hated, mothers of hysterical persons undergo the same occultation behind the screen of a winded narcissistic quest in the infinite mirror of hysteria?) Marie extracts no object against which to pit herself, measure herself, or project herself. What was abjection for Jean is for Marie pure and simple inanity, restless, feverish, and hollow; she is on an impossible search for a "real job," a "true love," which would bring an end to "nobody loves me." Such a logic dooms her to be a victim, but she realizes it only when a friend tells her, lost as she is within a space without boundaries, punctuated only by her symptoms (the "suffocations"—a boundary, barrier, or buttress?) and the jouissance of her fits of anger.

On the occasion of her father's serious illness, when she thought she might lose him, Marie had a dream. There is a death notice, a man has died, but the name on the notice is that of a woman. It is soon clear that it is the name of Marie's half sister, her father's favorite. Marie subsequently discovers that the two men in her life also had other women, hence she is not unique. She becomes jealous, flares up against those women, against the analyst . . .

The hysteric speaks of Agape and dreams of Eros Thanatos. But whether in this or that disposition of her love, she sustains her narcissistic infinite by jumbling the boundaries with her mother, and they both founder there in the delights of absence. Absence in relation to what? In relation to the elementary shift effected by primary identification, which allows for the existence of a potentially symbolic Other. For if it is transferred to the place of the imaginary father, inasmuch as he guarantees entrance into language and thwarts the phobic and psychotic potentiality of fusion hysteria, it is transferred along with the kit of representation but without the caboodle of drive.

The caboodle remained in the emptiness of maternal fusion and/or maternal absence. I do mean and/or, for, at this juncture, provided needs are satisfied (by a wet-nurse, nurse, or mother who is only a care-giver without an other desire), having a mother or not makes no difference: they are the same, she is the same. The being that satisfies needs (that is, the mother without desire for the Other) can leave no other spoor than that of not being, of nonbeing. What endows the mother with existence is primary identification, on the basis of which the hysterical person's mother will not assume the outlines of an abject but those of a stranger, an absentee, an indifferent one, before becoming, thanks to the incipient Oedipus complex, a conflictive object of projective identification.

Such a hysteria will then experience its Eros with women, while waiting for a symbolic, idealizing Agape on the part of a man who will never, just the same, correspond to its design. That is what mortgages the Oedipus complex of hysteria and explains why it will have the greatest difficulty in choosing a loving object of the paternal kind. For, in that structure, the imaginary father does not exist—he gave out before allowing it to have an object finally capable of love and hate emerge out of maternal emptiness, an erotic object necessarily in the mother's likeness (for the man and for the woman).

Caught between derealization (blurring of boundaries, somatic symptoms), where a narcissism without boundaries unfolds in self-satisfied fashion, and settling scores with women—scores that are necessarily anal but repressed and for that reason not at all abject—hysteria seeks its identity under the stern attention of the symbolic father, a ruthless father. The way toward Oedipal identification with the father is either blocked or impeded by repression of the imaginary father who is fully transferred to the mother's account. The hysterical person, man or woman, is not the mother's phallus but does not want to know this. Negation of primary identification endows him or her with that perverse plasticity, coyness, feigned susceptibility . . . pretending that she does not exist since she is . . . the mother, in other words, nothing . . . or an inaccessible totality.

It is possible to discern in the narcissistic hollowing out of the mother and in the anal economizing (in the sense of thrift) of abjection concerning her (the hysterical person spares itself maternal abjection, allowing, with respect to this proto-object, only emptiness or hatred, a "lovehatred"), one of the conditions permitting the violence characteristic of projective identifications within this structure. Still more pronounced with women, these features, as I see it, shed light on the feminine paranoia that lies dormant in so many cases of hysteria.

Matthew or the Walkman Against Saturn

Matthew is one of those youngsters equipped with walkmen who have recently invaded the streets of Paris and, I suppose, are rarely seen on couches. He comes in wearing his headphones steeped in music that is "classical, of course," as he specifies; he removes them only when he sees me, putting them back on upon leaving my office. A university graduate, an "expert and bored" mathematician, as he puts it, he devotes himself to singing, which, however, he is no longer able to do, hence his entry into analysis. Gifted in artificial languages, Matthew was no longer able to speak with his friends, nor could he utter a single word during a previous attempt at analysis that supposedly lasted three months. During several months of face-to-face therapy Matthew did not so much analyze as learn to put together a discourse for an other. Afterwards, reclining, he retraced a family history that was interrupted by brief sequences during which he said he was the victim of aggression—on the street, subway, or bus. Music isolates him from such violence, but now he believes that it also brings it about. An elliptic, allusive language, as if fastened to abstraction, serves him more to delineate space than to signify something for me. For him, speaking is painful and tiring, either too diffusive or too intrusive. Music alone harmonizes that bipolarity (abstraction—intrusion), which, without the headphones, becomes petrified and ties Matthew to his bed, without a phone, cut off from others, as if "surrounded with a chalk circle, invisible and impassable."

This phobo-obsessional equipment began to thaw when treatment caused the image of a devouring father to appear—eating, voracious, insatiable. A father-Saturn who took the place of the "poor guy" and induced a whole series of masculine and feminine figures, educators-persecutors-seducers; starting from there, Matthew began to examine the role of his walkman and his retreat into music.

Basing myself on this stage of his analysis, punctuated for me by Matthew's arrival with the headphones, I still have the feeling that he fears paternal seduction; is this phantasmatic or real? Appended to his mother, described as the key figure in the family, Matthew has not ceased being her phallus. Within their dual economy, which the father did not broach, it seemed apparently inconceivable that she might have a desire other than her child. The voracity of the dual symbiosis, accompanied by denial of the imaginary father and, consequently, an outpouring of withheld anal sadism, came back to Matthew from outside, projected—and this as soon as an object appeared, a masculine one preferably (for the mother blended with the patient).

Music was the father-again, the landmark, the intermediary between confusion on the one hand and the invisible chalk circle, besieged by onslaughts, on the other. It allowed Matthew to set up a mobile identity for himself and to reject out of it, as abject, whatever did not belong (especially the Oedipal array to which were added the more archaic oral loathing and sadism). Matthew was gleeful, ecstatic, and amorous but only as walkman. The headphones were a spot that included all other spots, an organized, differentiated infinity that filled him with consistency and allowed him to face Saturn's devouring but also to have his own destructiveness toward him recognized. Matthew's maternal uncle was a well-known pianist. Analysis made use of the walkman; identifying the shell that the headphones were destined to become, it turned it into a premise of autonomy, of demarcation.

Obsessional neurosis, in the vault of its rituals, shelters a drifting, an instability that reveals the failure or fragility of primary identification, and that is what Matthew's walkman helped me to hear. . .

"O God, I could be bounded in a nut-shell and count myself as king of infinite space, were it not that I have bad dreams," Hamlet said (II, 2), and Borges quotes those lines at the outset of his Aleph. "The place where all the places of the universe can be located, without intermingling," the Aleph is a "privilege" granted the child in order that "the man, some day, might engrave a poem."

Could what is thus being considered be the condition, and for some the sublimational possibility, for remaking an imaginary father, taking his place, creating his place within language? Such an economy takes nomination closest to that spot without object, both point and infinity, blocked identity and immediate identification. It is the place where narcissism is said to hold sway only in the painful manner of Hamlet, surrounded by abjectness, emptiness, ghostliness, and quest for paternal love. For before killing him in Oedipal fashion, the speaking being, in order to speak, loves the "father of personal prehistory." Suffering, he beguiles himself with the sound of his cross, an acrobat walking a tightrope: should he let himself be walled in alive or make a poem out of it?

VI

Throes of Love:
The Field of the Metaphor

Through the Looking Glass

IT MAY SEEM paradoxical to be seeking the discourse of amatory relationships in borderline esthetics. It may seem strange that, instead of bringing to the fore the straightforward language of simple idealization of the love object, one analyzes the painful or ecstatic states where the object slips away. Such a choice is not imposed simply because the staging of amatory capture in a narrative has been more fully investigated. It has been determined essentially by two observations.

The first, psychoanalytic in nature, amounts to asserting that the amatory experience rests on narcissism and its aura of emptiness, seeming, and impossibility, which underlays any idealization equally and essentially inherent in love. (See One and Three, "Narcissus: The New Insanity".) Even more so, when the social consensus gives little or no support to such an idealizing possibility, as may be observed at the present time (a phenomenon of which the religious and moral crisis is only one aspect), the derealization that underlies amatory idealism shows up with its full power.

In the second place, when one transposes into language the idealization on the edge of primal repression that amatory experience amounts to, this assumes that scription and writer invest in language in the first place precisely because it is a favorite object—a place for excess and absurdity, ecstasy and death. Putting love into words, and this stresses the utterance more than the propositional act ("I must utter as close as possible to what I am experiencing with the other") necessarily summons up not the narcissistic parry but what appears to me as narcissistic economy. Such and such loving speech that we view as being in a state of uncertainty and metaphorical condensation reveals the continuity of the narcissistic economy, and this includes the "insignificant" love experience that does not dare express itself differently from what is on the surface, does not venture to seek its logic beyond the looking glass where lovers bewitch each other. Because it thus speaks love's painful but also constituent truth, that scription attracts us. We become its readers during the intervals of our own loves when we are able to withstand looking at them beyond the surface, in more fundamental fashion. It is a view that we cannot necessarily share with our partners but to which our dreams, our anguish, and our jouissance bear witness.

On the Referent and Subject of the Metaphor

Let us call metaphor, in the general sense of a conveyance of meaning, the economy that modifies language when subject and object of the utterance act muddle their borders. One already senses that one of the purposes of this study is to base a theory of metaphor on a given number of specific states of the subject of the utterance act. My reading of metaphoricalness constitutes neither a philosophical challenge against the metaphor, nor an expansion (conversely symmetrical) of its impact on any language act; it assumes a theoretical distance which is not that of speculative philosophy either. It finds its justification in the ambiguous, loving distance, germane to psychoanalytic transference, and is based on contemporary analyses (semantic, syntactic, discursive) of the metaphorical act.[1] I shall, however, try to understand such broadened metaphoricalness within the amatory economy of the subject of the utterance act, who demonstrates by means of metaphors the complex process of identification (narcissism and idealization).

"The metaphorical exists only within the bounds of metaphysics," Heidegger propounds.[2] The assertion is incontrovertible as far as the field of metaphysics itself is concerned, and it suits the metaphoricalness particular to philosophical discourse, which was able to survive because of the very banishment of the metaphor.[3] I shall, nevertheless, in this discussion, go over some points that are essential to my purpose.

We know that, ever since Plato (see Gorgias, but also Phaedrus), philosophical discourse has been obsessed with setting up the boundary between philosophy and rhetoric, which is the real requirement for its existence.

Eidos, Omoiosis, Analogia

The problematics of ressemblance, of the "striking image," worries, as we know, the whole of Platonic thought. It has often been shown, since Heidegger, how light, the "visible," and the "image" conceal themselves in the "idea" and establish thought itself as a lexicalized metaphor, eidos. Let us, however, remove from that constituent imagery of the idea the portion of resemblance that, for its part, sustains the metaphor. "It is the east, and Juliet is the sun," Romeo tells Juliet, for Juliet and the sun are equally dazzling to him, they are alike in that both are a dazzling light. Omoioma, atos: Plato uses those words when discussing love in Phaedrus, and he posits that the amorous soul notices an omoioma, an imitation of celestial things in the things of this world that resemble them and for that very reason causes it to fall in love, puts it beside itself. One is in love with what resembles an ideal that is out of sight but present in the memory. The entire motion of metaphorical conveyance is already present in the omoiôsis relation, which has the advantage, at the very dawn of Greek thought, of placing love in concert with image-making, resemblance, homologation. Greek ontology buried that movement, but we find it again, with the same Platonistic terminology, in the Gospels where omoiôma is used to think out the relations between God and his creatures (Romans 6:5). Nevertheless, medieval theology, moving away from the amatory metaphoricalness of the Gospels,[4] became interested in the Aristotelian notion of analogia, intent as it was to make the state of shock inherent in amatory omoiôsis and metaphor thinkable. Aristotle's place is thus fundamental in the "ontologizing of the metaphor" constituted by creating the philosophical category of analogy. Torn between ontology and theology, Aristotle's thought keeps aiming for their reconciliation, which, as it is impossible, finally wrecks his ontological design.[5] One of the tokens of this conflict is the effort to elaborate a univocal philosophical language untainted by any poeticalness and yet attentive to some of the signifying effects of the latter.

Having thus expelled metaphorical ambiguity from the field of philosophy, Aristotle kept trying to gain, within that very field, "a scrap of equivocalness."[6] He succeeded in doing so, on the one hand, by gradually blunting the predicative function's preciseness (the predicative copula may be understood as a being said of—"Socrates is [said to be] a man"—and as a being in—"Socrates is a musician," where "musician" is an accident of the substance). But on the other hand and above all, Aristotle is led to speak of analogy (in a different sense from the one he ascribes to this word when he considers it as an instance of metaphor) when discussing aporia in "the multiple meanings of being." There is a being, but this may be said in various ways. How can we reconcile these two assertions if not by analogy? Indeed, having inherited from outside his own philosophy a Platonic theology of the being-as-One, Aristotle is forced to reconcile his discourse on the physical world in the diversity of its categorial meanings with the pros hen, ad unum. The divine ousia constantly underlies the categorial oneness of being. That is precisely what is written in the Metaphysics when the word analogy is introduced: ". . . the causes of all are the same. They are the same or analogous," and further on, "the causes of substances may be treated as the causes of all things."[7] The thinkable, the ontological, is based on the theological unthinkable, which, although unthinkable, but perhaps for that very reason, sets the structure of the investigation. The analogy thus introduced was first proportional, hence mathematical, before it was set forth as attributive analogy.[8] By breaking with the Poetics Aristotle salvages some metaphoricalness for the sake of another no longer metaphorical but transcendental discourse: analogy is witness to this pros hen, ad unum in his investigation. And one will have to wait for the whole post-Kantian expansion of positivist logic before Carnap and Russell would define predication as the assignment of something to a class and thus extricate the problem of attribution from the sphere of analogy. What is nevertheless lost along the way are the other meanings of the predicative copula, notably the function it has to designate the acting being, thereby giving rise in particular to a question concerning the very act of uttering. The philosophical salvaging of analogy by Aristotle had the advantage of opening up the question of being as act, and at the same time of raising the problem, if only implicitly, of the act of naming. Certain Aristotelian expressions, moreover, state this rather clearly: "The metaphor signifies things in action" (Rhetoric III, II, 1411b, 24–25); "the imitators may represent the whole story dramatically, as though they were actually doing the things described" (Poetics 1448a, 24); "Now the action is represented in the play by the Fable or Plot" (Poetics 1450a, 3).

Let us not forget that advantage, let us not bury it in the simple logical motion of predication or predication as motion. For such a dynamic will enlighten us concerning the significance of the other metaphor, the real one, the poetic one. Let me simply stress here that analogy, even when philosophical, is necessary for thought in order to organize an investigation (not a science) that would be ontotheological (Heidegger's phrase) and dependent on being as One and on the act.

Furthermore, medieval theology, splitting off in its turn from the poetical metaphoricalness of Biblical discourse, postulated with Thomas Aquinas an analogia entis. It distinguished first between an analogy of proportio (comprising a definite distance and a strict relationship, determinata habitudo, between the terms) and an analogy of proportionalitas, which is a simple similarity of ratios (for instance, six is to three as four is to two). But in order to imagine the relationship between infinite God and finite creatures Thomas Aquinas posited causality itself as analogy. What had been a relationship of significance (of creatures by analogy with God) becomes an efficiency: one may name God according to creatures only on account of the relationship between creatures and God, their principle and their cause, in whom all the perfections of existing beings preexist excellently.[9] Such an analogy, which has been raised to the level of cause, indeed excludes the strictly poetical metaphor; but, as Paul Ricoeur notes,[10] it also integrates it surreptitiously; this happens when, examining two analogous meanings (for instance, using "wise" in speaking of man and of God), Thomas sees that the nominis significatio (germane to the "wise man" and attributed to God by analogy) is exceeded in the res significata (God) by a surplus of meaning impossible to circumscribe . . . The surplus of meaning (the res significata is richer than the nominis significatio) that results from the new predicative motion is, properly speaking, the metaphorical effect specific to poetry, which Thomas' logicality wished to contain by means of analogia entis.

A final observation concerning the philosophical history of the metaphor as outlined by Paul Ricoeur will bring us closer to my purpose. It is true that a certain conception of the metaphor, itself stemming from a reductive metaphysical interpretation, which moreover is no longer in favor, lends itself to restricting that image to the field of metaphysics. I refer to the interpretation that sees in it a simple shift from literal to figurative, primary to secondary, animate to inanimate, etc.: all such distinctions are indeed metaphysical, they can be upheld only within a naive theory of metaphor dominated by the word and insensitive to its syntactical and narrative dissemination. The work done I. A. Richards, Max Black, and, on a different level, by Emile Benveniste, Roman Jakobson, and contemporary semanticists who, in their wake, locate the metaphor in sentence and discourse bring out a certain potentiality that is specific to the Aristotelian interpretation of the metaphor. The kurion ("common" name) or the idion (translated as "proper") simply refer to the starting term of metaphorical motion and seem not to have had for the Greek philosopher the meaning of a primal, primitive (etumon) own. Present day theory of metaphor brings to the fore the interference between two nonhierarchized semantic fields and two reference areas also nonhierarchized; it does so by giving greater importance in its analysis to the sentence or discourse context of metaphorical motion as well as to the utterance act as referential and intersubjective act.

"Being Like" or the "Unbeing"

Metaphoricalness consequently appears to me as the utterance not only of a being as One and acting, but rather, or even on the contrary, as the indication of uncertainty concerning the reference. Being like is not only being and nonbeing, it is also a longing for unbeing in order to assert as only possible "being," not an ontology, that is, something outside of discourse, but the constraint of discourse itself. The "like" of metaphorical conveyance both assumes and upsets that constraint, and to the extent that it probabilizes the identity of signs, it questions the very probability of the reference. Being?—Unbeing.

Paul Ricoeur, in his fine study, speaks of an "ontological vehemence" specific to the semantic aim of the metaphor as act, which harbors in itself the premonition of the unknown that the concept is about to take hold of. This is where I part company with him. His interpretation permanently ontologizes the metaphor, which he had yet so strongly reinstated in its possible but not absolute ontotheological scope. By saying that the metaphor signals a new reference to be named, Ricoeur encloses metaphorical dynamics within a speculative philosophy whose plan he explicitly acknowledges, a philosophy subservient to Being as One, comparable to the process that, besides, we both detected in Aristotle and Thomas Aquinas.

Is there, however, another possibility for thinking out metaphoricalness, without simplifying it in order to reduce it to a metaphysics itself simplified, and without limiting its impacts as "premonition of a concept" within the framework of speculative philosophy? It seems so to me. I shall interpret metaphorical dynamics as established not by the designation of a reference inevitably reducible to being, but by the relationship the speaking subject has with the Other during the utterance act. The utterance is precisely what seems to me, from the position of an analyst, the only basis for meaning and significance in discourse.

What I am calling for is not a simple reversal in perspective: setting up an inner foundation, a "mood," a basis for discourse—in place of an outer one, the referent. The subject is not simply an inside facing the referential outside. The subjective structure, understood as a specific articulation of the relationship between speaking subject and Other, determines the very situation of reality, its existence or nonexistence, its overturning or hypostasis. In such a perspective, ontology becomes subordinate to the signifying structure that sustains a given subject in its transference upon the Other. (See in One the second section entitled "Einfühlung: Identification with a Metaphorical 'Object.'")

I have observed that the ultimate support of Aristotelian metaphor is a being who acts. The poetic as well as the categorial metaphor merely convey "motion and life"; yet Aristotle stresses that "act is motion." Nevertheless, in the face of the difficulties that ontology encounters in its attempt to define "strength" and "act," I shall venture the following proposition. The "being who acts" could exist only for a subject in symbolic contact, that is to say in motion, in transference with another. The being who acts gives its all in subjective experience, and this is even truer in the love between two subjects—that climax of destabilizing-stabilizing identification. There is no act, just as there is no sexual act, outside of love, for it is in the constituent violence of its field that the subject's signifying structure is shaken, drives and ideals included. There, on account of the subject's modification—the questioning of the subject in amatory experience—a modification of its being and of being is carried out; they are opened out, if you will, unfurled. No longer physics but speaking subjectivity will henceforth ask the key epistemological question; what is mobility, what is innovation? "What is" means, in this context, how does one express them? The amatory experience as dynamics of the crisis and of subjective and discursive renewal, along with its linguistic correlative, metaphoricalness, appears to be, from that standpoint, at the core of an essential debate.

The univocity of signs undergoes equivocality and is resolved in a more or less undecidable connotation when the subject of the utterance, in a state of transference (of love) toward the other transposes the same process of identification, of transference, to the units of language—the signs. The undeniable referential effect of this process, which stems from the referent having been made ambiguous, should not conceal its subjective basis. The signifying unit (the "sign") opens up and reveals its components: drives and sensory elements (as in a synesthetic metaphor)—while the subject itself, in a state of loving transference, flares up from sensation to idealization.

If one agrees with that interpretation, one understands why philosophical thought on metaphor is rooted, with Plato, in a reflection on love and on the direction philosophical discourse must take in relation to that reflection: Plato aims at a mastery of love and at the same time of metaphor, through ontotheology. One also understands why theological discourse alone, concerned with the One and the relationship of the speaking being to that One—hence with faith—was forced to proceed edge-to-edge along the metaphor, which produced a determination of the theory of analogy starting from the poetic field but outside of it. Finally, when theology is emptied of its essence and, with Descartes, holds the other in a position of a causa sive rationem and seeks the true basis of reason only in the articulation of judgment and no longer in analogy, which, even when preserved, loses its function—we then witness a double banishment. Nascent rationalism brushes aside in the same stroke analogy, the scar of metaphoricalness in the specific domain of ontotheology, and its correlative, the Ego affectus est: the loving subject. To make possible the advent of judgment and the Ego cogito.

Psychoanalysis: Poetry and History

The psychoanalytic stance allows one to record a true transformation of Western discourse, in which the metaphor is at stake, accomplished at the expense of the loving subject; it thus determines a position that can only be termed outrageous within the history and typology of interpretative discourse. Functioning under the same amatory conditions that rule the production of metaphoricalness in poetic discourse, psychoanalysis nevertheless keeps a certain distance from it since it produces, with respect to that discourse, a knowledge effect. Does this mean that it produces its concept? If that were true, it would not be distinguishable from speculative philosophy and ontotheology. It might be better to say that it analyzes in precise fashion such a proximity to speculative philosophy. Not in disseminating each concept by way of metaphor or asserting that every sign is necessarily a forgotten metaphor that must be brought to the fore in order to dissolve its idealizing conceptual seizing. Rather, it does so by preserving a typology of discourses (for instance, the "poetical" is not the "philosophical" discourse, which is not the "analytical"), and setting for itself the regular task of being, on the one hand, a scene of metaphor production (as in the amatory state or in poetry) and, on the other, a scene of provisional interpretation. It is the provisional (this means such-and-such, for the moment) that inserts duration instead of the absolute in psychoanalytic interpretation. Psychoanalysis is the most internalized moment of Western historicality. The provisional also inserts the chance of subjective encounter in the dynamics of transference. If it is the amatory impact that makes me say, and if such saying can have a history, this means there is no absolute that is external to our love, our discourse. Neither your actual history nor anyone else holds the reference to our discourse's signification. Being in love conveys a throbbing, passionate, unique meaning, but only here and now, and which might, in another juncture, be absurd. For the first time love, and with it metaphoricalness, are removed from the authoritarian domination of a Res externa, necessarily divine or deifiable. Love and metaphoricalness, thus deontologized or, in the extreme, dehumanized, henceforth constitute a determination of language with all its resources spread out. The subject himself/herself is merely a subject: a provisional accident, differently renewed within the only infinite space where we might unfurl our loves, that is, the infinity of the signifier. Love is something spoken, and it is only that: poets have always known it.

From now on amatory styles will be spread out before us like different historical embodiments of the metaphoricalness that is essential to loving states: like stylistic variants of the cure, another name for life, which the Western subject has experienced for two thousand years through his amorous attitudes, which have been inserted in amatory codes now made official.

Toward a Typology of Meaning Transfer

Courtly rhetoric will allow us to observe how metaphor becomes at the same time invocation, song, and things unspoken, a secret that lies heavily on the name of the Lady as well as on the very meaning of the message in the most extreme, the most hermetic texts. The joi, apotheosis of amorous jouissance, is no more than the exaltation of the troubadour and the autoconsumption of signs.

A belated mystical crisis in the seventeenth century will allow us to see, with the work of Jeanne Guyon, how, in an amatory metaphoricalness subordinate to the dominant position of an extradiscursive absolute, as idealized as it is stern (as God was for her), the "pure-love" ideal interrupts metaphorical effervescence and leads either to the making of trite metaphors or to silence.

The incandescence of Romantic metaphoricalness, and even more so that of Baudelaire, will show us how, in a love lined with a forfeited object ("The Carrion") and a divine ideal ("God is a scandal, a scandal that brings a profit"—Fusées), metaphor becomes antithetical, as if to blur all reference, and ends up as synesthesia, as if to open up the Word to the passion of the body itself, as it is.

Mallarmé, closer to us but still so strange, put the melancholy of love's helplessness into words in a manner more underhanded than Nerval. (See the final pages of "Our Faith: The Seeming" in Three.) Attracted to the other sex, he turned a problematic enamorment into lace (because his signs remain so elliptical, so pierced), and yet renewed it and finally left it in abeyance on account of the possible identifying possession of the other sex. The language of such a love, whitewashed and sung as "resonant inanity," is rather the ellipsis than the metaphor—ultimate form of condensation on the brink of aphasia.

The Stendhalian egotist's amatory fantasy demonstrated, with the help of irony and fiasco, how modern love became political matter or diplomatic cunning. "Crystallization," however, was a test of strength supported by an ideal figure—a mother who is as dead as she is masterful. Metaphor then slipped into the metonymy of the impossible quest, but that amatory pursuit was itself sustained by the belief that the ideal—a metaphor—exists. By interpreting it, we entered the era of psychology.

Finally, a brief text by Georges Bataille, My Mother, will show how, when eroticism no longer is the open secret of amatory experience and reveals itself in its obscene and cruel nakedness, the lover's discourse is no longer set forth in metaphors but in deficient narrative on the one hand, meditation on the other. As if it were imperative that everything about desire not be said in order for love, thus a certain idealization of the other, to persist, and that this be the condition of the semantic swelling constituted by metaphor in the narrow sense of the term (be it Romantic or Surrealistic). Does this mean that pornography deprives us of metaphoricalness? Perhaps it spares us the metaphor properly speaking, but not the transfer of meaning. The acknowledgment of desire in language opens up the narrative field: one can witness the admirable process of that transformation when the poetic Romance of the Rose by Guillaume de Lorris becomes the narrative Romance of the Rose by Jean de Meung. The times did not yet allow much sexual daring, but in the second part we are not spared the violence of the amatory situation and this fact, aside from shocking all the feminists of the period, changed the first part's metaphoricalness into didactic allegorism in the second. With Bataille, as under a magnifying glass, the obscene avowal of the nucleus of love that constitutes incest produces a narrative made up of deferrings and free associations—one that is as classical as it is shattered.

Thus perceived, literature appears to me as the privileged place where meaning is elaborated and destroyed, where it slips away when one might think that it is being renewed. Such is the metaphor's effect. Likewise, the literary experience stands revealed as an essentially amorous experience, unstabilizing the same through its identification with the other. In this it emulates theology, which, in the same field, has strengthened love into faith, thus subjugating by means of the absolute the critical moment that love comprises; today literature is both the source of "mystical" renewal (to the extent that it provides new amatory spaces) and intrinsic negation of theology to the extent that the only faith literature conveys is the assurance, painful just the same, of its own performance as supreme authority. In love with our own productions, under empty skies, we have not departed from esthetic religion. Religion of the imagination, the Ego, Narcissus, esthetic religion dies harder than Hegel thought. He had perhaps forgotten, while wanting to settle a score with theology, that in this respect one had to consider our loves—with which theology, for its part, had had the cunning to concern itself. Since then, forsaken by faith but ever loving, hence imaginative, egoized, narcissistic, we are the faithful of the last religion, the esthetic one. We are all subjects of the metaphor.

VII

The Troubadours:
From "Great Courtly Romance"
to Allegorical Narrative

When Doing Is Saying

FIRST THERE WAS the troubadours' courtliness followed by the trouvères'; a part of the southern French language to begin with, finally of northern French, it was a miraculous creation of the twelfth century. It imposed on the world for the first time in such massive fashion the fin amor, that refined perfection, as joyful as it was ideal, for which we still hold a postromantic nostalgia. Whether or not it was influenced by Arabic or Catharistic mysticism, the fact remains that it carried over into secular domain the amatory fervor of an Ego affectus est that was fired by Bernard of Clairvaux's Cistercian mysticism. How mysterious the twelfth century was, a premature renaissance that could light up within men's souls the splendors of the beyond. A celebration of immanence within transcendence, the fin amor is yet essentially an art of Meaning. Irreducible to a simple feudal court ethic no more than it is a very subjective valorization of "the master's wife" (literally domina, the lady), such love involves a code. Included therein is the coloration, both sentimental and erotic, of the relationship that aims at completion (fin) and remains an adultery. It will be noted that roles, originally metaphorical, were assigned to the woman and the man—suzerain and vassal respectively, the lady levying a "distress," the man offering a "service." Made up of onor (both fief and claim to fame) and partage (equality, informality among courtly people that sets them apart from the outside world), that love includes a merce, a reward from the lady, and does not at all remain platonic. All the more reason for its remaining secret: the senhal, the taboo affecting the lady's name, is one of the conditions for its exaltation and perfection. Nevertheless, the rules of amatory behavior that one infers from courtly songs often cause one to forget that the essential achievement of courtliness, at least of the sort that comes to us across the centuries, is incantation.

Before becoming an art of love, or rather because it was one and at the same time as it was, courtliness was an utterance act. Which one?

A Speech That Is Sung

First, like Greek lyrics, this amorous speech is a song. Linguists are not accustomed to pay much attention to that aspect of courtly rhetoric, an essential one just the same. It is true that we only have 264 troubadour melodies, that is to say about one tenth of the extant poetry,[1] and it is only in the case of Bernart of Ventadour's work that the considerable number of eighteen melodies remains. Furthermore, the square notation system used, as in Gregorian music, which is precise for pitch but has no concern for duration, hence for rhythm, makes the deciphering of those witnesses to courtly songs very problematic. It seems, however, that the absence of any indication of rhythm is not simply a poverty in transcription but conveys a particular feature: the dominance of melody and personal variation in the flexible tempo of the song, which is known as tempo rubato. The complex, opulent, sensual mingling, made up of meanderings, pleasureful incantations, expressive vocalizations, spasmodic windings, or, on the contrary, bearing on supple groups of words, constitute in fact the first encoding of the singer's amorous transports, the tokens of his joy or joi. The term has been attested to in both its masculine and feminine forms and denotes a jouissance, a vital force, an embellishing and refining spirit, a "celebration of being"; thus, Arnaut de Mareuil writes, "As the life of fishes in water, mine is in joy and will be there forever." The song is his essential signifier and signified. It carries no referential, objective signification, it is the meaning of joy.

The song is not a metaphor but, as the most direct inscription of jouissance, it is already a transference, a longing of affect for the absolute meaning that shies away.

One might maintain that the discursive equivalent of that musical uncertainty, that wavy, jubilatory hesitancy of the voice, would be the rule of the trobar clus, the exclusive style that contrasts with the common or clear style, the trobar leu.

The trobar clus gave rise to the trobar ric, the rich, accomplished style, which, while comprising obscurities, integrates them—not in the fashion of a formalistic, esoteric pursuit but as intrinsic requirement for the alchemy of secret love itself, kindling a fire in subjective units as well as in signifying ones. Rambaud III, count of Orange, spoke of entrebescar los motz, intertwining, entangling words ("Cars bruns et teinz mots entrebec / Pensius pensans"—Thoughtfully thoughtful, I intertwine rare, somber, colorful words).

Intertwining Words

We now come to the rhetorical technique that aspires to be worthy of the song's jubilatory incantation. How does one translate into univocal signs such a semantic irradiation, such a contamination of signs and individuals in the experience of amatory involvement?[2]

The use of homophones or of words with similar voicing adds to musicality, but above all it throws doubt on meaning at the very core of the sign. "Arnaut sends his song at once and about the uncle [sur l'ongle et l'oncle] to please the cruel lady who holds his verge's soul, he sends it to the friend he desires, whose fame enters all chambers" (probably Bertran de Born).

The play of oppositions ("his verge's soul") introduces a logical paradox that, too, furthers the primacy of affect over meaning. One observes the same tendency with Arnaut Daniel, knight of Riberac: "I am Arnaut who hoards the wind, / and hunts the hare with an ox, / and swims against the current."[3]

This art of confusing words is not, however, experienced by the troubadour as a pursuit of nonmeaning but of what I shall call, for want of a better word, an affect, to the extent that, in the joi, it goes beyond meaning and bears witness to the unrepresented, the unrepresentable. "As for me I consider that as much sense is needed to keep one's right mind as to mingle words," Giraut de Bornelh asserts, but he specifies, "For exquisite meaning brings and bestows meaning in proportion to the blame incurred for its unrestrained nonmeaning; but I believe that no song, ever, is as valuable in the beginning as when one understands it later."

The syntactic outcome of the entrebescar los motz is often a juxtaposition of phrases that avoids the concludent setting of phrasal utterance and follows the song's meandering. "For neither prayer, nor play, nor viol, could, from her, by the thickness of a reed, separate me. . . What am I saying? May God engulf me or plunge me into the abyss. . ." (Arnaut Daniel). Finally, it is in the ambiguity of metaphor that one also reaps the overflow of signification through joi.

To illustrate the point let us examine a song by Arnaut Daniel.[4] This text has the advantage of being a song about song and of displaying clearly some of the ambiguities of courtly metaphor.

Master of the trobar ric, praised by Dante and translated by Ezra Pound, Arnaut Daniel composed this song on song and at the same time on love, which engrosses one because of its concision and ambivalence. Ambiguity, already noted in the works of other troubadours, resides in the courtly vocabulary itself, always erotic and sentimental at the same time; here, however, it weaves the deep meaning of the amorous state as such. Not only do the abstract and the concrete overlap (fencing/defense, Lucerne/light, withstand/draw, and so forth), and erotic connotations filter into the frustrated reverence for the Lady (seek/desire, her body floods down over mine, and so forth), but the pairs of opposites frame the text from the very beginning. To plane / to complain, to maintain / to belittle, to lose / to take, shop/tavern, work/intoxication, flood/dry up . . . as well as semantic shifts starting with phonics such as rime/lime, marves/malvatz and up to the final statement, ("I hunt the hare with an ox and swim against the current")—thrust the reader into semantic as well as phonic uncertainty, reversibility, and contamination in signs, which carry the ambiguity of metaphorical meaning to its peak. Is love a metaphor of song, or is song an image of love? Song-love, happy or painful? The one and the other undoubtedly, unity of opposites, paradox, rain, flooding, joi . . .

Guilhem, count of Poitiers, the first and already the most accomplished of troubadours, emphasized that courtly incantation has no fixed, objectal, limited reference, but necessarily goes through the conjuring up of the void and the undecidable. "Farai un vers de dreyt nien (I shall make a line of pure void)," and also, "ag ai lo vers, no' say de cui (I make a line, I know not of what . . .)."

The Ambiguity of Two Messages: Lady or Joy?

In point of fact, at the limit, courtly songs neither describe nor relate. They are essentially messages of themselves, the signs of love's intensity. They have no object—the lady is seldom defined and, slipping away between restrained presence and absence, she simply is an imaginary addressee, the pretext for the incantation. Like a dreamlike staging but without the narrative act, without even the ability to hold the vocabulary to a concrete meaning, let alone the vocabulary of love, the courtly song refers to its own performance. One should read it while hearing it and interpret it as a vast activity transposing univocal meaning outside of its limits, toward the two borders of nonmeaning and the mystical, metaphysical totality of Meaning. This latter focus of incantation soon led to the inscription of courtly rhetoric into religion and changed the Lady into a Virgin Mary. Not only was the ideology of the times a contributing factor, but the very dynamics of the courtly utterance act, stretched between loss of meaning and hypostasis of meaning, lent itself to it.

One should, in short, always split the courtly message into an M1, made up of a literal signification and having the Lady as referential object; and an M2, referring to joy alone and whose sign is the song but also the excess of meaning, the "more-than-meaning" brought in by indefinite syntax, paradox, or metaphoricalness in the very vocabulary. The referent of M2 is paradoxical at the very least, for, as the joy of song, it finishes off the incantation in the very process of the subject of the utterance act in his performative jouissance. Is this an apotheosis of narcissism? Or the acknowledgment, beyond the confusion of references ("she" is often, for the troubadour, the song [which is feminine in French] as well as the lady), of incantation's conveying a meaning in motion that the linguistic uttering could not possibly assume: precisely the meaning of involvement, of loving identification. "I dare not speak, unless in song," the Châtelain de Coucy said.[5]

The culmination—in the sense of fulfillment and end—of courtliness saw the incantation disappear to the benefit of narrative. Through the same motion in which the song lost its Dantean dignity (Dante extolled it as the perfect realization of poetry, bringing together music and language in unum et idem) the courtly utterance became more literal. The metaphors that moulded courtly code became lexicalized, hence commonplace. It was allegory's turn to express the representability of courtly utterance. Allegory, however, the personification of the semantic and subjective tension specific to metaphor, became its grave. Allegory sets and moralizes, it parcels out, soothes, and pontificates. One might think of the ballades and rondeaux by Charles, duke of Orleans, in order to pinpoint the shift from song to narrative. Charles's style, averse to the rhetoric of incantation and stripping the text of any generalizing or ambiguous suggestiveness to the benefit of the concrete, of objective experience, on the verge of mediocrity, is doubtless a personal feature.

The fact yet remains that this is a broad evolution. As figure of speech, allegory preserves an allusion to a world of abstract values (Danger, Virtue, etc.) but loses the ambiguity specific to play and joy, it conceptualizes and clarifies; along with it, the narrative establishes itself in the semantic universe of M1, without any opening out, recourse, or destabilization toward M2. The narrative no longer is an intrinsic, immanent, sacred invocation of joy and joi, it becomes psychological. Henceforth the lady actually is its object, and the psychological twists and turns of the other's capture and seduction are opened up as an exploratory field for the narrative. The Romance of the Rose illustrates very clearly the toppling of lyricism over to narrative, metaphor to allegory. With a benefit, however, which is the bringing to the fore of the aggressiveness specific to amatory interaction. Joi is no longer in evidence. Courtliness has become seduction and possession, incantation has become realism.

It is with Sade that this narrative logic reaches its peak, thus bringing novelistic writing to a head in the very motion through which Sade discloses its nonspoken aspect in massive doses: jouissance as a putting to death of the other and the Other.

In the transition from song to narrative, from joi to psychological realism, allegory plays an essential part. Let us follow this movement more closely, within courtliness itself.

From Song to Narrative

Song and poetry split up in the fourteenth century following a complex process out of which narrative emerged, in verse to begin with, finally in prose. That evolution comprises two main features, upon which medieval scholars have lately placed much emphasis. On the one hand, courtly themes are replaced by descriptions of ordinary life, and as discourse became commonplace and oriented toward daily events it was accompanied by an outburst of satirical zest that took the place of earlier idealization. As soon as we had objects of discourse external to the incantatory utterance, such objects became prey to a more or less subdued aggressivity. Repeated stories based on trifling details of the "good life," and eventually the pejorative image of the Lady (Alain Chartier's "La Belle Dame sans Mercy," 1424, or the works of Jean Molinet, 1481, etc.) sum up rather well this aspect of courtly completion. Nevertheless, the most profound transformation of the courtly world may be witnessed at that level of rhetoric where song gives way to narrative.

On the other hand indeed, the text, which was first a narrative song[6] that remains articulated by stanzas and lays rather than by the specific logic of the narrative, eventually became a sung narrative. The latter unfolds in the time appropriate to the action of a third person ("he") who supplants the lyrical "I." Previous to or contemporary with the great courtly romance, the sung narrative first follows church ritual: the Eulalia Sequence (ninth century), the songs of Etienne and Alexis (twelfth century) blend through the themes of suffering and through song the internal experience that remains refractory to a univocal verbal designation and the discourse on an external reality henceforth more and more prevailing. Outside the courtly field, resorption of the song took place very soon, and as early as the first third of the twelfth century there appeared a verse narrative without song (1120 and 1140–50). Two kinds of discourse predicated on different economies thus became contemporaneous: great courtly romance and verse narrative without song. As if a revelation of amatory state identified with incantatory state conflicted, within the same society, with the designation and mastery of an outside, tranquilized by the observing, no longer passionate, position of the subject of utterance. Collective epic (basically the chanson de geste) or individual story, the narrative, once extricated from song, became the novel. Even if the first novels (notably those by Alexandre d'Albéric de Pisançon, 1130) seem intended for song, the great novelistic venture, as the work of Chrétien de Troyes (1160–90) bears witness, already was a laboratory for the prose that sprouted beyond the enjambments and syntactical daring of verse narrative.

The narrative, therefore, on account of its specific logic based on the adventures of a third party, ruled out the convolutions of the (amatory) utterance act from the foreground of the texts and turned the novel into the privileged domain of propositional acts. Even if many texts keep going back to their own meanings, such self-referentiality no longer has anything to do with the performative enthusiasm of incantation as it celebrates its joi in the great romance. Self-reference became didactic, explanatory, moralizing; it aims not at jouissance but at communication and education.

Social changes have certainly presided over the path that tore the troubadour—subject of his passion and song—away from loving subjection to an Other at the limits of faith, and turned him into an author removed from a reality in which he could participate as actor, but whose meaning, in the last instance, he holds. I shall not go into this matter. I shall merely emphasize the key role of allegory in the true revolution that swept through the twelfth and thirteenth centuries and toppled over an Ego affectus est into an Author's position.

The Novel of Narcissus in Love

The Romance of the Rose in its two versions gives us the whole dynamics of that revolution. The first part, written by Guillaume de Lorris (about 1236), more poetic and enigmatic, is made up of 4,058 lines peppered with allegories that are brought together in a narrative set according to the devices of the "encounter" and "dream." The poet writes to his lady in order to earn her favors and tells her about a dream he had when he was twenty. As commonplace of medieval rhetoric, the dream, according to Guillaume himself, goes back to Cicero's Dream of Scipio and Macrobius' commentary. Here, however, the dream narrative blends two discursive worlds: one where, according to the poet, the art of love is included in its entirety, and the other is that of the sequence of ordeals undergone by a hero who does not appear so much as the subject of his passion than as an individual who is both author and actor of his story. The story itself, which is no more than the framework of the dream narrative, is relatively simple. The poet, who has reached a wall protected by fearsome characters (Hatred, Lust, Sadness, and Poverty) on a fine morning in May, knocks at the door. A kindly young lady, Trivial, opens the orchard of Diversion for him (also a traditional topos with classical connotations going back to Tibullus, Christian ones recalling paradise). There, a company of young ladies are frolicking with their lords: Beauty, Wealth, Generosity, Candor, Courtesy, and Youth. The poet joins them and then, walking through the garden, arrives at a spring, at the bottom of which two crystal stones reflect the garden. A sign informs him that this is the fountain that in times past swallowed up Narcissus. The poet-hero, however, looks more closely, and doubtless helped by the reflecting stones, sees an image . . . that is not his own: a rose bud. A violent desire to pick the flower—symbol of the loved object—makes him understand that the God of Love, with his golden bow, has just shot him with the five arrows that prompt one to love (Beauty, Simplicity, Candor, Company, and Fine Appearance). The ten commandments of courtly love are taught to him afterwards. They comprise a true handbook that sums up the precepts of courtliness: taking care of one's appearance, avoiding servility as well as arrogance, being temperate and modest in one's language, appearing cheerful, playful, generous, never speaking ill of women and owing them absolute respect, etc. The early episodes of the quest, however, change this allegorical compilation of courtly love into an adventure story. Within and throughout the narrative, allegories are transformed into characters who further the quest (such as Fair Welcome, son of Courtesy, or Candor and Pity) or prevent access to the Rose (such as the guardians of the Tender Rose, Danger, Shame, Fear, Evil Tongue, and Jealousy). Having succeeded in obtaining a favor (the gift of a green leaf close to the bud, and then even a kiss), the lover in fact causes his friend's misfortune: the Rose is confined to a castle's keep. He himself is assailed by "characters" who attempt to divert him from his passion: Reason pleads against love, while the Friend advises cunning so as to gain favor with the guardians . . . We are fully in the domain of logic, argumentation wins over passion. Separated from his beloved, the lover complains, and Guillaume de Lorris stops right in the middle of a monologue.

A powerful "I," yet overwhelmed by love, expresses himself in a text that is as much narrative as it is poetic, contrary to what may have been maintained. The allegorical "characters" are not always aspects of the Lover, but they often point to his conflicts with external forces, which nevertheless always keep him within the space of fin amor. The main theme of the text appears to be not the psychological initiative of a free individual nor his obstacles but the very space of passion—amatory space. The wall, the orchard, the spring mark its borders and may be seen less as stages of a psychological experience than as features of love's territory, which a lyrical "I" marks out and tames. A space-time conflict appears to brace the text, and it may be felt in the opposition between a designation discourse that sets the courtly code and annotates its significance, and a narrative discourse that marks out, for its part, the temporal sequence of ordeals. The significance that comes out of this, precisely, is hazy enough not only because it is personal, elaborated on the basis of the narrator's own phantasmagoria as much as, if not more than, references to the courtly canon in the strict sense of the phrase, but also on account of the very ambiguities of the text. It has already been noted that "characters" do not necessarily use the language one might expect of them because of their names. Moreover, the signification of the vocabulary and sentences often remains ambiguous and the meaning, although "literal," depends on interpretation. Even if characters are conceived according to a novelistic tradition already established by Chrétien de Troyes, a polyvalence of meaning appears intentional and seems to have been programmed by the symbol of the crystal stone, which pluralizes and pulverizes the monolithic image of the garden at the bottom of the spring. The quest of the Rose seems also and always a quest of "covert" meaning transcending literal meaning. For that reason, Guillaume de Lorris' text is still close to the great romance: although the Rose is an object external to the Lover and surpasses the spring of Narcissus, the poet is on a quest of poetic meaning itself, from allegory to allegory, gloss to gloss. If there is a Rose, it is nevertheless a vision that is internal to the luminous refractions of the narcissistic world. The "literal meaning" in fact is not that at all. In Guillaume's very eyes, what is involved is an elaboration (shimmering of crystals) at the core of the essential Love-space of writing and coextensive with its deployment. The space of love is the space of writing, the poet seems to say, and within it any signification, like the "narcissistic" vision of Dante in his Paradise, is no more than a fiction. Any signification is thus an approximation, but also an analogy—an allegory—of the only true meaning, which is love and also poetry. What is involved, in fact, is the elaboration of a space or of the mechanism particular to a fate that incorporates us, not a psychological adventure. That is what is suggested, for instance, by the narrative's submission to the image of the quest for an object-of-meaning. More strikingly yet, is that not what we are allowed to glimpse through the motif of the working mechanism that generalizes the narrative in Froissart's Orloge amoureuse?

Character as Allegory, or Moralizing

Allegory is precisely located along the stress line between lyrical utterance, of which song is the culmination, and narrative utterance. We have seen how, with Arnaut Daniel, the very vocabulary of courtliness was the consequence of an unstabilized metaphoricalness, playing on the tension between the erotic and elevated semantic universes, and on a series of other dichotomies as well. Extended all along the courtly discourse of the great romance, that metaphor becomes stabilized in its elevated, literal meaning. Such a stabilization removes us from the utterance act as subjective performance and as joi or jouissance and leads us to read courtliness as a semantic code of values and rules. Guillaume de Lorris continued, however, to seek the polyvalences of that semantic universe, as if such a polyphony, restored by means of his gloss, could be the equivalent of incantation. Nevertheless, in spite of that eventual polyphony of the figurative, literal meaning comes loose and attempts to assert itself as controlling the narrative flow by way of personification. Having become a character, "Virtue," for instance, points to its meaning independently of the gloss through its own words and actions. We are no longer on the level of the utterance act, nor on that of a semantic code, but within the narrative that acts and justifies itself, at the same time action and interpretation. Allegory having become personification takes the narrative into the story on the one hand, into moralizing ideology on the other. Allegory is a lexicalized metaphor that reasons and acts in order to give itself a meaning. Would narrative be the linear (in time) and didactic (through ideology) supplement of a metaphor completed as allegory?

Jean de Meung, who "continued" the Romance of the Rose (1275–80) carries out that fulfillment of allegory into didacticism through the motion that takes him out of amorous courtly space into the time of an adventure and the aggressive seizure of the object. "Fin amor" is dead, long live procreation, which alone is worthy of interest, and eventually pleasure, which is merely its wile. In this gigantic mass of eighteen thousand lines, allegory remains the only point in common with Lorris' text. But it has been entirely stripped of its metaphorical tensions and is henceforth a means of education, a model for the writer's philosophical or cosmogonic conceptions. This does not mean that Jean de Meung no longer believed in allegory. Quite the contrary, with him allegory culminated in normal fashion, following through on its own logic, which it already bore with Guillaume de Lorris and earlier, less didactic writers. Simply, social-historical forces, which imposed naturalism and knowledge against mysticism and the Church, expanded excessively in this philosophical writer those rhetorical potentialities specific to personified allegory itself. The first French scientific and philosophical poet, Jean de Meung, with his seventeenth–eighteenth-century aura, actually opposes Courtliness and Love with the worship of Nature and Genius, whose ancient resonances are well known, and both of which doubtless come straight out of Alain de Lille's De planctu naturae. The worship of nature, which owes a great deal to twelfth-century Platonists and thirteenth-century Aristotelians, is nevertheless not unaware of God, even if it rejects sin as well as grace. Internal space closes up to the benefit not of a journey in the folds of the soul and of its lining, the utterance, but of one bent for knowledge and possession of the outside.

Satirical Narrative: An Object to Be Conquered

Situated outside the utterance act, the Lady need no longer be exalted: an object to be conquered, in the same way as the rest of the world, the feminine character is a target of satire (Jean de Meung doubtless owes a lot to Rutebeuf and thirteenth-century satirists). Neither Christine de Pisan's indignation nor women's anger with Jean de Meung up to the beginning of the seventeenth century[9] could have any hold on a discourse from which Reason, prevailing more and more, was to banish Love and Joi and, with them, not woman (was she ever truly the main preoccupation of courtliness?) but the very possibility of idealization supported by the possible-impossible meeting with the other sex. With woman turned into an object of carnival or satire, this signals the end of a given mode of idealization that the secular West had acquired with the troubadours and trouvères. The struggle against God and the progress of realism, along with that of science, include on the negative side some deficiency in the idealizing possibility. The Renaissance did not feel its damaging effects, sustained as it was by the assertion of a humanistic and rationalistic ideal, and also by laughter, the giant, fratricidal twin of the ideal and inseparable from its throne, which it keeps overthrowing. Not until the advent of Romanticism did courtliness affect us again, but this time as an impossible, if not baleful, ideal. As nostalgia at best, most often as melancholy, but never again as joi.

VIII

A Pure Silence:
The Perfection
of Jeanne Guyon

WHEN Jeanne-Marie Bouvier de la Motte (who was to marry Guyon du Chesnoy) was born in 1648, France was already in the process of becoming Cartesian. Jeanne was only one year old when, in 1649, Descartes published his Treatise on the Passions of the Soul, in which, being a determinist and a materialist, he asserted that the "main seat of the soul" was a "small gland in the brain." The trend leading to the subordination of passions to thought, already in evidence with Thomas Aquinas, was masterfully completed in the Cartesian corpus, which extols the supremacy of thought (art. 50, 74) and knowledge (art. 139) over passions. A little later, Pascal, in his Discourse on the Passions of the Soul (1654), emphasized the involvement of reason in love; he bore in mind love's true, lucid vision of truths, not its blindness. The subordination of love to true knowledge contrasts with the medieval thesis postulating a truth that only stemmed from the love of God. The dethronement of faith by reason is accompanied by a mutation of love, which slips away into knowledge. The history of philosophy can trace in minute detail through the texts of those thinkers how the Christian subject, constituted by his reference to God explicitly identified with love, yields to the subject of cogito ergo sum.

Quietism and Politics

In that context, the mystical experience of Jeanne Guyon, quietist founder of the Brotherhood of Pure Love (1690, with her friends Chevreuse, Beauvillier—Mme de Chevreuse and Mme de Beauvillier were Colbert's daughters—and Mortemart), seems archaic. What is more, she was condemned as a heretic by a Church that was, along with Bossuet, enamored of rhetoric but also of reason and reliable institutions. Jeanne Guyon was first held, in 1688, at the Paris Visitation, for seven and a half months. A special court that was convened in 1694 to examine her doctrine sentenced her to detention after Bossuet had given the impression he had acquitted her.[1] Jeanne was confined to the Vaugirard prison and then the Bastille from 1696 to 1703, and was authorized to live by herself only after 1706. She died in Blois, in 1717, at the age of 69.[2]

Quietism, apparently so gentle, so childlike, so innocent—at least in Jeanne Guyon's version—is somewhat an obscenity in the midst of a century that included Louis XIV, Mazarin, the Fronde, Versailles, Vauban, but also the austere wars of the no less austere Jansenists, the dragonnades against the Protestants . . . A century whose major rifts as well as the options of opposing blocs did, in spite of all, move in the "direction of history." Jeanne's experience, which, it is true, attracted part of the nobility and did not fail to take on political connotations (her associates in Pure Love had connections with the anti-Protestant League and the devout party) remains nevertheless thoroughly extravagant.[3] Reactionary in the sense that it was a reaction against the most rational aspects of faith, her humility, which glorifies the annihilation of self in the love of God, in fact originates in a vitality and a confidence in one knows not what deep wellspring of subjectivity, which are linked with areas of childhood but also recall a non-European culture, thus short-circuiting an entire civilization of thinking adults.

The Stain

It is true that this peculiarity emerged in the midst of a heretic current that was able to set against official and increasingly reasonable faith an opposition, minor to be sure, but nevertheless that of a group. In his Report on Quietism (1694–95), directed against Fénelon beyond the latter's friend, Jeanne Guyon, Bossuet placed this woman who, according to him, played the prophetess, within the framework of quietism, founded by the Spanish-born Italian priest Miguel de Molinos, who was condemned in 1675.[4] Bossuet reproached quietists with wanting to "proceed toward peace through annihilation," a peace that would give them fusional access to a "God who was pure, ineffable, abstracted from any particular thought, in eternal silence." Another characteristic of quietists, consecutive to the major objective of annihilation and peace, earned them the most objections: it consisted in believing that "certain stains might be a means God used to raise a soul to the highest degree of annihilation in Himself." Such a recommendation in favor of stain necessarily implied a de facto abolition by quietists of the category of sin and could only encourage what the Church and the social consensus considered amoral. The "impossible supposition," discussed by Bossuet, established a link between the theory of love as renunciation and the subtle recommendation of amoralism: it would amount to thinking that, "if the impossible happened," and God wanted to damn us, we should consent to it. Far from being alien to certain aspects of Christian mysticism (Francis of Sales, but also Dionysius the Areopagite), this aspect of quietism does not seem to have assumed with Jeanne Guyon the paroxystic and even less so the orgiastic forms other quietists indulged in. Whatever might have been said about the daring of her low-cut dresses or the overly intimate terms on which she was with her first spiritual adviser, Father François de la Combe, a Barnabite (they traveled together), or even her suspicious relationship with Fénelon (the ambiguous phrase, Fénelon's "amica," uttered by Abbé Bossuet at the Vatican, appears to have been decisive in the papal condemnation of Maxims of Saints, if only in a "brief" in 1699), Jeanne Guyon appears to have kept her desires to the level of unconscious or preconscious fantasies.

"Their Sublime Was Amalgamated"

One must read the account of her dreams, as she candidly reveals them in her Life,[5] or the criticism to which Bossuet subjected them, in order to detect a woman's erotic longings. The whole enigma of this strongly amorous person lies, on the contrary, in the alchemy she subjects desire to, so as to extract from it only "pure love." Even if she read Peau d'âne, Don Quixote, or Molière, she at once placed her relationship with Fénelon in the sublime—a relationship that led to the disgrace of the Duke of Burgundy's preceptor and hurt his career if not his fame. Saint-Simon, who never missed a thing, saw this remarkably well and wrote:

In those days, while still unknown, [Fénelon] heard of Mme Guyon, who has since caused so much talk in society and is too well known for me to discuss her particularly. He saw her. Their minds appreciated each other, their sublime was amalgamated. I do not know if they understood each other clearly within that system and that new language one saw coming out of them subsequently; but they convinced each other that they did and the bond was established between them.

Everything is marvelously right in that sketch: "their sublime was amalgamated," "I do not know if they understood each other clearly within that system and that new language . . . ," the remark concerning "too much talk" provoked by Jeanne, the touch of offhandedness if not misogyny that causes Saint-Simon to write that he will not "discuss her particularly." Everything is marvelously right, except perhaps, "He saw her." For, as one reads her correspondence, it was she who saw him and led him, so he could amalgamate with it, into the sublime. "Some time before my daughter's wedding," Jeanne later recalled, "I had met M. A. d. F. (the Abbé de Fénelon), as I have already told.[6] . . . We engaged in several conversations concerning inner life, during which he made many objections to me. I answered with my usual straightforwardness, and I have reason to believe that he was satisfied. As the matters of Molinos then caused considerable to-do, the simplest things aroused suspicion, as did the expressions most frequently used by those who had written about those matters. That led me to explain my experiences to him thoroughly." The ensuing correspondence is prolific on Jeanne's part, very moderate on Fénelon's. Her own enthusiasm, which immediately assumed a maternal stance (when not a wife's role, but a spiritual one), inevitably views him as a son and prodigal child upon whose mother it is incumbent to insure the spiritual future and fame.[7] Fénelon is more cautious, indeed more critical: "Nothing equals my cool, hard affection for you" (but is there not, at the core of "pure love," something cool and hard, without effusion, with no consideration for one's own advantage?). And again: "You still care for the respect of decent people. . . . The ego I have so often discussed with you is still an idol that you have not smashed." And finally, writing to Mme de Maintenon (1696): "I have never felt any natural liking for her or her writings." The least one could say is that this statement is a plain disavowal of "pure love," then fully in disfavor from both Fénelon and Jeanne's standpoint, with the latter about to be interned. Was this simply a human, all too human, self-defense on Fénelon's part, or was the naturalistic moralism of the Duke of Burgundy's preceptor less deeply at odds than has been thought with the theological rationalism of the Great Dauphin's Gallican tutor?[8]

Beyond individual histories, however, one will remember "their system," "their language" (Saint-Simon). Which ones?

A Renunciation of Desire and Representation

If it be true that quietism sought, and claimed to have found, a state of love for God with no concern for rewards or punishments, a state of indifference without quest for either personal satisfaction or desire for salvation, an act of contemplation that takes into account neither the attributes of God nor Christ's humanity, as was made clear in Innocent XII's famous "brief," in that case Jeanne Guyon was a quietist in the essential aspects of its doctrine. She was so when advocating renunciation, annihilation of the self, reached by seeking a childlike state and culminating in the apotheosis of nothingness. She was so, too, on account of her marvelously optimistic and jubilatory confidence in the continuous presence of God who would give of himself to those who loved him disinterestedly, whose presence was direct transparence. She was even more so in her refusal to consider punishment and hell, convinced as she was in her lovingly complacent contemplation that sin was avoidable. She is so, finally, when, in order to attain a death-for-the-self in the amorous presence of the Loved One, she recommends nonmediated communication, not only outside method and thought but also outside language, reaching its peak in pure silence.

Childhood is the most sensitive and objectively conceivable condition of such "pure love." Following the death of her father and her daughter in 1672, Jeanne took the Child Christ in "mystical marriage." When in 1690 she set up the Brotherhood of Pure Love, it is also called the "Order of Associates in Christ's Childhood." Its members are known as "Christofflets" (those who wish to carry the Christ Child) or "Michelins" (those who are so small they cannot walk and are carried by their master, Saint Michel). Obviously Jeanne prefers the "Michelins," the more impoverished ones, those who abandon themselves to the most helpless state of childhood, those who come closest to "the loss of all personal advantage or reflection." "I suffer cheerfully, like a child," Jeanne would say later, referring to her attacks at Thonon, or elsewhere. An afflicted and yet happy childhood becomes assigned to the ideal to the point of being lost in it, and it loses all possibility of representation. The apotheosis of such a progression may doubtless be found in those pages of her Life that describe the transformation of the Self into an unnamable nothingness, without sight or thought, and yet glorious in the knowledge that it is within the Other's love.

Lately I have been able to speak hardly or not at all of my moods. That is because my state of mind has become simple and unvarying. The heart of that state is a deep annihilation that finds within myself nothing nameable. All I know is that God is infinitely holy, just, kind, happy; he holds within himself all that is fine, I hold all that is wretched. I see nothing beneath myself nothing less worthy than I am. . . . The good is in God, my only share is nothingness. What can I say of a state that is always the same, with neither design nor variation? For hardness, if that is what I have, is equal for me to the most satisfying state. Everything is lost in immensity, and I can neither will nor think. It is like a droplet of water lost and spoilt in the sea: it is not only surrounded by it, it is absorbed. It cannot see itself in that divine immensity but it discerns objects within God, without perceiving them in any way but through the heart's penchant. With respect to it, everything is gloom and darkness; as for God, everything is light, which leaves nothing out of its ken without knowing what it knows nor how it knows it. . . . Therein is neither clamor, nor pain, nor sorrow, nor pleasure, nor uncertainty; but perfect peace—not within the self but in God: no interest in the self, no memory, no attending to oneself. That is what God is within this created being. Its lot is misery, weakness, poverty, without thought either for its misery or its dignity. If one believes there is some good in me, one is mistaken and wrongs God. Everything good is in him and for him. If I could feel contentment, oh it would be because he IS WHAT HE IS AND WILL ALWAYS BE. If he saves me it will be gratuitously, for I have neither quality nor dignity.

I am surprised when one has any trust in this nothingness. I have said so. And yet I answer when questioned without worrying whether my answers are right or wrong. If wrong, I am not surprised; if right, I am careful not to take any credit. I go without going, without design, without knowing where I am going. I want neither to go nor to stop. Will and instincts have vanished: poverty and nakedness are my lot. I neither trust nor mistrust, in short nothing, nothing, nothing.[9]

"One who loves perfectly," Jeanne wrote Fénelon, "loves perfectly only because he is thoroughly dead himself."[10] Pure love she described as being "work without work," "passive night," "deprivation of everything," "demise," "disappropriation."[11] Understandably, when such "blessed indifference" contaminated [the young ladies' school at] Saint-Cyr the authorities were disturbed. Mme de Maintenon, who at first had been attracted, drew back; Mme du Perron, of the opposing party, denounced "this so-called resignation before God's will, which led one to accept one's damnation as candidly as one wished to be saved, for this is what the notorious act of renunciation that was taught amounted to, after which there was no point in bothering about one's eternal fate." The well-known logician and grammarian of Port-Royal, Pierre Nicole, was no less harsh: "Quietism is a cleverness on the Devil's part; he wished to abolish all of God's mysteries and attributes, through which man's salvation was effected. Unable to succeed, he discovered the secret of at least destroying them in the memory of men, by allowing feigned men of the spirit to adopt a method that consists in no longer thinking about them."[12]

A Protection Against Judgment?

Indeed, the annihilation of one's own attributes, the muffling of desires, wills, personal concerns, in short the death of the Self, does lead, as logical correlative, to make of God a simple, dazzling presence, also deprived of attributes. By stripping her ideal of attributes, Jeanne above all deprives it of judgment and punishment, turning it into a God that is purely and oblately loving, one she can but praise and glorify. Neither fear nor even pray. "Everything I saw taught me to love you. . . . If it rained, I wanted all the drops of water to change into love and praise. . . . I became united with all the good that was done on earth, and I would have liked to have the hearts of all mankind in order to love you," she yet writes in her Life in the midst of so many setbacks, illnesses, desertions, and movings that did not appear to affect her. The apotheosis of such praises may be found in Torrents (a theological poem written before 1684), but especially in her Explanation of the Song of Songs ("Solomon's Song of Songs interpreted according to its mystical meaning and the true representation of internal states," in her Examination of the Holy Scriptures, begun after 1685, published from 1704 on). The analogical and sensual discourse of the biblical Canticle lends itself easily, in Jeanne's reading, to emphasize the continuous union with the Loved One, but her reading does not help to reveal its tension. Flowers, turtle doves, seasons are for her evidences of the divine presence with which we are bathed and saturated. Such osmosis finds in perfume (the amatory theme par excellence, a Baudelairian theme) the most powerful metaphor for lovers' union. "Your name is an oil poured out," the text reads, and Jeanne goes on with that divine presence, "like a balm poured out that increases imperceptibly as it spreads more and more; and with a scent so excellent that the beginning soul finds itself completely filled with its strength and smoothness." Bossuet rebelled against the self-confidence of such a person who pretentiously believed herself worthy of divine love, who even shiftily equated herself with God through a love that was so unmediated, so fully shared. Jeanne was unruffled: "There are those who say that such a union belongs only in the other life, but I am certain that it can take place in this one; the difference being that in this life one possesses without seeing and in the other one sees what one possesses."

How can one be so sure of possessing the invisible that loves us?

Hell Is a Dream

Jeanne did not believe in hell. The certainty that hell is perhaps only a dream is one she held since she was nine years old, when she confessed to her schoolteacher that she had, until then, "had her doubts about hell," before having a dream. "It appeared to me as a place of fearful darkness where souls were tormented." This place of dreams, these throes of the unconscious are what she preferred not knowing about. What is extraordinary is that she succeeded. Not without some effort, however, for she admits that if to avoid suffering one must quench desires in the state of renunciation, well, "One must hold firm to renunciation," without listening to reasoning or reflection.[13] A "will not to will" would be required in this hollowing-out of hell (of the unconscious?), which prompts us to seek its imaginary motivations.

A Semiological Struggle: Are Feelings Nameable?

Finally, the whole semiology advocated by Jeanne was refractory to the standard conception of signs and their usage. Bossuet, who made speech into an art, understood this. With indignation, he noted that instead of judging feelings by the words that are at the start and then prevail, Jeanne "judges words by the feelings." In short, she located a mystical "hidden meaning" on the near side of language. In his Instructions on the States of Orison, a book secretly influenced and at any rate inspired by the debate with Guyon and Fénelon, Bossuet maintained that any state considered nonrepresentable is actually nameable. It would seem that, through Guyon, Bossuet was fighting against Protestantism. He thus missed the void that was the true object of Jeanne's orison: he missed the experience where the Self is emptied in order to become fully a Subject, equal with her beloved God (in her Life Jeanne says she is wife and mother of Christ, indeed Christ himself, and that appalled Bossuet. . . .).

The orison of "pure love" had its precedents in Jeanne de Chantal and Francis of Sales, whom Jeanne read when she was thirteen. The orison is a tacit loving discourse that, as a first step, gets rid of method, reason, and the very rules of judgment, and is finally carried along by a kind of nonknowledge and restraint. "O Lord! Will they teach methodically how to make love to Love itself!" is a cry we read in Le Moyen court. And the following: "It should be noted that virtues must appear to have entered the soul with no difficulty at all; for the soul I have in mind does not think about it, since its entire occupation is a broad Love, with neither grounds nor reason for loving. Ask this soul what it is doing during orison and throughout the day: it will answer that it loves. But what are your grounds or reason for loving. It knows nothing about it, has no experience of it. All it knows is that it loves and longs to suffer for what it loves. But perhaps the sight of your Beloved's sufferings is what leads you to suffer? Alas, alas, it says, I do not think of it, and they do not come to my mind. Then it must be the desire to imitate those virtues that you detect in him? Alas, I do not think of them. Then what do you do? I love. Is it not the sight of your Beloved that tears your heart away? I do not look at that beauty. What is it then? I do not know. I do feel a deep wound at the bottom of my heart, but it is so delightful that I rest in my sorrow and turn my pain into pleasure."

The Melancholy "Nothing"

Nevertheless, it would seem that suffering, illness, the withdrawal into herself experienced by Jeanne had led her thought to reel even more toward nothingness. "It was during this illness, Lord, that you gradually taught me there was a means other than speech in order to converse with created beings that were completely devoted to you. . . . In an ineffable silence. . . . The language of angels. . . ." Those "silent communications," so peculiar to Jeanne, thrust the subject in its most secret, most archaic narcissistic folds; and if it believes it succeeds in transmitting them, it is because its partner's identical narcissistic plunge lends itself perfectly to projection and identification.

And yet this passionate advocate of "silent communication" was a voluble writer. A copious correspondence, a Life, treatises, and especially that first text, so wordy, symptomatic, and indeed torrential, her Torrents,[14] show that language, for this contemplative of the void, remains a site of pleasure.

An Erotic Object: Language

If her jouissance is the immediate conveyance of an empty Self to the blazing ideal, her pleasure resides in speech and writing. Jeanne writes mainly to and for Fénelon, but her addressees may multiply or vary so that writing constantly remains. A spirited writing, often loose, without concern for style, with interim successes to be sure, but without aiming for strictness, laxist. Repetitive, stereotyped in both form and content, the writing of her poem Torrents, for instance, is somewhat intoxicated, trancelike, automatic. Jeanne was aware of it and fully realized that she could be seen as "mad." Perhaps she did not know that writing enabled her to mark out that internal space, as painful as it was amorous, and whose hell she was not too eager to scrutinize. This is especially true for Torrents, written at a crucial moment in her life, when, stripped of her wealth by her family and the Nouvelles Catholiques of Gex, very close to Father La Combe, Jeanne is in the midst of a physical and psychic crisis: this was in 1683, and she was to be confined, as already pointed out, at the Visitation in 1688.

Writing is perhaps the "semblance of willpower" that enables one to do without a practical, moral, or active will in order to "hold fast to renunciation." Such holding confers authority to the Self, which can claim "emptiness" provided it simultaneously proceeds with a true work of pinpointing and reconstitution by means of the text. Such a writing of phantasmatic consumption and destruction of meaning amounts to something like associative speech set adrift. . . .

"My Mother Considered Me Quite Tall for My Age. . ."

It is tempting today to seek in Jeanne's early childhood the reasons for such an amatory economy. All one finds are objective misfortunes and subjective feelings of superiority. Born after an eight-month pregnancy ("my mother had a terrible fright"), Jeanne immediately becomes ill with sores in the legs and back, and the resulting abcesses lead to gangrene; she is believed to be done for, but she recovers; she is put in care of a nurse like all children in those days; barely weaned she is again removed from her family and sent to a convent; when seven years old she enters an Ursuline convent with her half-sisters and later, after clashes with them, she turns up at a Dominican convent where she catches smallpox. Her sole moment of happiness appears to have been at age fifteen, when she fell in love with a cousin. Her parents, however, soon put an end to that romance and arranged a wedding, without her knowing who the man was even as the marriage contract was signed. The husband was a man much older than she, wealthy and generous, but fussy and unbearable for his young wife. She became the mother of five children, two of which died early. Her oldest son caught smallpox in his turn, and she was stricken for the second time; both remained disfigured. . . . And that is only a minute part of her trials. One should note above all an absence of rebellion and indeed a kind of early "quietism" (perhaps simply imagined after the fact by one who, when writing her Life, had already become a quietist), with which she accepted her adversity. One bitterness does persist, however, which the quietist "hold" could not erase—toward her mother, who was absent-minded, cold, if not cruel. "My mother considered me quite tall for my age, and more to her liking than usual, and she thought only of showing me off, having me seen by people, adorning me well. . . ." "She loved me a little more because I suited her. She could not refrain from preferring my brother over myself. . . . for when I was ill and I found something to my liking my brother would ask for it, and even though he was well it was taken away from me and given to him."

A cold mother, solitude, and a conspicuous, unjust, cutting deprivation. . . . Was healing such early wounds, etched in her flesh to the point of provoking an abcess in her back and gangrene in her legs, was that what Jeanne sought after when she imagined—and succeeded in changing that imaginary construct into reality—a fully loving and directly accessible God through preverbal, childlike orison?

"With No Other Will Than Her Father's. . ."

While her parents were absent they were at least not repressive. One day, even, she was discovered playing in the street with children who were not of her station, and this shows that supervision was not what it should have been. Jeanne moved about within a space bereft of love, but she did not suffer from a tyrannical superego. In such a world of solitude and neglect, one idealized being remained: her father, who seems to have been, according to what she has told us of her childhood, the person most watchful of her. Quite symptomatically, she noted that her model, Jeanne de Chantal, "always appeared to have no other will than her father's." Did Jeanne, then a young woman full of verve and spirit, not put herself in a similar situation when she completely gave in to her father's will and married, in short, and with some satisfaction, that very paternal will, since she accepted the arranged wedding without knowing who the husband was?

Everything appears to have been set up well, too well to allow a wounded narcissism to slip through the net of the superego's law by means of extrafamilial structures (nurses, convents, etc.); this might have permitted setting up defenses that are basically rooted in what I have called an imaginary father, prerequisite for the Ego Ideal, the place where the Ideal Ego is made up. Is not "pure love" a perpetual slippage from emptiness to Unity, from the Self that exists only in Him to Him who is an empty Self, within the permanence of Union? Moreover, every time Jeanne emerged out of the deeply neurotic state of her marriage, she was sustained by a father, a Father of the Church in such instances, but who was not in a comfortable position. That would first be La Combe's part and finally, in a much more brilliant fashion, but ever breaking away from official dogma, the part of Fénelon. Tears, headaches, temperamental incompatibility with her husband and his family—that whole reality of her marriage doubtless rests upon objective reasons, which do not make it less painful, and reading, any more than orison, cannot really take care of it. It was not until Guyon's death (1676),[15] until she established a relationship with La Combe and then Fénelon, that the stability of "pure love" could materialize . . . under her pen.

Silence as Artificial Mother

Moreover, Jeanne Guyon's passion for silent communication seems directed at the area of narcissism precociously set and consolidated through potential idealization but keeps affect confined within an unnameable mother. An elusive mother, not only because she is pre-Oedipal but because she is affectively devastating: too hurtful or too far removed. Silence eases the pain of such a maternal lack. It reconciles the subject with the sorrow ploughed by that lack; it covers it up, tames it quietly; one might say that it heals [panser] even though it does not think [penser]. Silence as an artificial mother. Sustained by the ideal, "I" is then able to make the pain its own, neutralize it, ease it. Without masochism and without paranoia, which would imply subjection to a stern law rather than love for and of a Third Party. Where the hysterical woman suffers because she sets the affect that is her mother's prisoner within an unspeakable homosexual erotics; where Dora becomes ill, flees her analyst, hates men, and in short does not cease being obsessed with her mother's sex, the other woman's sex—that is where Jeanne chooses withdrawal. Not war, nor even a quest for the initial object any more than for "others." She aims at and succeeds in consolidating the primal border between the not-yet-Self and the Other: it is the verge of narcissism, of the emptiness that laps against the ideal, the promised land of primary idealizations. . . . Was it, as Françoise Mallet-Joris said in her moving book, sympathetic toward Jeanne,[16] because she was a wholesome young woman, natural, full of life and spirit? Contrary to the nearly jovial recreation of her own person that Jeanne proposes, her writing rather suggests someone who has been tossed about, constantly hesitating between dereism and action, of dream and social pressure. She engaged in extravagant efforts to plot out for herself a parcel, if not of reality, at least of the real. An impossible real, for which she does not even have a clear title, which is not "her own," but where she "holds herself" thanks to her imaginary devices of "silence" and "pure love." It is the most faithful, the strongest possible working-out of her frailty, where body and senses run the risk of toppling over into a black, unspeakable affect. One is bound to note that the social and ideological framework, although more and more constraining for people who are so intimately rooted in narcissism, nevertheless allowed for its working-out, heretical as it may have been. That father, all those dissenting fathers. . . . Today the analyst should also be able, for a while, to be the support of our Ego Ideal. . . .

The Father's Version [Père-version] as Perversion of Language

Finally there is this war, peaceful and yet so violent, against the henceforth dominant cult of representation, and even more so, of rational representation. Beyond Bossuet, it is with Descartes that Jeanne clashes. Opposing Cartesian thought, which claims that the dream itself finally is a representation that is identical with the one that makes up the idea,[17] loving Jeanne, the one carried away by logorrhea to the brink of aphasia, nothingness, the unspeakable, sets up something else—but what? It is merely the hysterical fantasy of something on the near side of language, sacrificial harbor of maternal loves, source of symptoms and anguish? Or is she proposing something more, thanks to the "work without work" constituted by orison but also to the fantasmatic hollowing out constituted by writing? Perhaps a possibility of stretching the borders of the nameable beyond the boundaries set by a discourse of methods and ideas: by transposing into signs not so much the experience of a subject as fulcrum of reason, whitewashed analogue of the creating God, as that of a loving subject, an array of constant presences and absences, a dialectics of losses and fullnesses. . . . Such a subject is archaic at the end of the seventeenth century, and yet so modern in its insistence on symptoms and our impossibility in naming so-called narcissistic affections. Jeanne pointed out its territory as a defeated activist confronting triumphant opponents who had the future on their side. The immediate future. Others, before her, had been able to put discourse together with greater talent but also with greater sociohistorical understanding: Theresa of Avila, John of the Cross. . . . As for Jeanne, she is not without appearing silly, and at any rate allowing herself to be won over by a glance, our glance, which picks out symptoms more readily with her than with her predecessors: all those brotherhoods, all those frivolous activities, all those letters that are both secular and theological, half amatory and half edifying. . . . She falls between two codes: she turns her attention to her narcissistic dramas in which she joys through a perversion of language, and she is also taken by her century, ready to topple into reasonable activity. Or simply into setting beside it an acceptable reverse: childhood, spontaneity, and not forgetting worldly preciosity, which she fluently absorbs in some of her texts.

Let us read her, however, with the love that is deserved by those who have been defeated by causes whose future lies before them. Their problems are ours—in other guises, with other solutions. . . .

For the confrontation of the subject with what is nonrepresentable comes under, one might say, an exposure of narcissistic structure that, without collapsing into aphasia or psychosis, clings to the ultimate crest of its substance, to what constitutes it as a "zero degree" of subjectivity: the imaginary father. This means that the cult of the irrepresentable is a cult of the imaginary father—the one who loves us, not the one who judges us.

The West knows another approach to this structural necessity: instead of worshipping him, the Son storms the agency of that imaginary father. Father and Son, socialization of narcissism, a success where the psychotic failed: such would be the artist's task, as he filled the churches with sparkling images. Including the troubadours and Joyce, the artist tried to name what is closest to the unnameable along that loving curve made up of narcissism. The naming that results is necessarily exploded, pulverized. The homosexual libido that underlies the irrepresentable cult of the imaginary father is absorbed there as it exposes itself, it is consumed as it is sublimated.

A simple story might yet constitute the modern and suffering version of Jeanne's "silence."

Keep Talking, or the Disappointed Speech

Susan goes into analysis, overcome by "destiny" she thinks. A father who died in a Nazi camp has bequeathed her no more than the memory of a sunny day when he took Susan on a bicycle ride: she remembers the warmth of his body and a sense of clutching. Dead? Not quite: for Susan, this mythical father is merely "traveling." For years after the war she waited for him, not believing he would return, but just the same. And yet one may think that she believed she had found him again, since she has, for some time, been living with Roger, a handicapped person of her own age. Sublime and already aggravated Samaritan, without for that matter being able either to leave him or to resign herself to being his eternal companion, Susan speaks abundantly and ardently—it is a black strength that never ceases expressing itself, without blanks, without punctuation. But without saying anything either. A weariness fills that feverish discourse, a futility that is often conscious and lucid: "I bustle about but I don't believe in it, that isn't it, I make believe, there is like a distance, a hollow space between what I say and what it is. . . . But I don't even know what it is. . . ." Before Roger, Susan lived with her mother: an insane passion, total adhesion, and continuous anger. "I make mistakes when I speak," Susan says; "I always say 'confusion' instead of 'scission,' and vice versa." That slip of the tongue, which betrays a whole drama of individuation, is accompanied by mother and daughter having their own language, a private language, which is shared by none of their acquaintances: German. It is the mother's tongue, which the daughter understands but does not speak. Nevertheless, the passive consumer of the mother tongue is able to change the excitement of that maternal embrace into the neutral, distant, disappointing, and yet existent mastery of her social activity. Susan is an interior decorator and a business woman: everything for looks, touch, and money. . . . "But I'm going to tell you, it leaves me cold." And frigid.

There is a strange chiasm in hysteria (a chiasm, as one knows, is the crossing made by optic nerves: simply a coincidence), which recalls primary narcissism. As if the subject had settled at the very place where drives skidded into a psychic inscription leading to a mental symbolic organization. Placed at such a crossroad the hysterical person can only waver. On the mother's side, he or she again finds the unspeakable drive stimulation. On the father's side, he or she settles in language and the symbolic while ballasting herself with her body. In short, the verbal overabundance of the hysterical person is an aphasia. . . . The symptom (pain or anguish) that may show up as an attempt to solve this unbearable chiasm by establishing, instead of the narcissistic crossing, a fastening of the drive motion to the very body, reveals the failure of the drive/body splitting, which initiates the setting up of psychic space.

The impression the hysterical person has of holding "a futile discourse, an empty discourse" (feminists have turned this into an activist ideology by asserting that discourse was "male chauvinist" and rebelling against it) may be better understood, it seems to me, in the light of primary narcissism as described earlier, in One, "Freud and Love"; an object on the one hand, an imaginary father on the other, a narcissistic formation wedded to both and resting on the void constituted by the exquisite differentiation of drive motion into psychic inscription. (See section titled "An 'Immediate' and Objectless Identification" in One.) The narcissistic parry is an attempt to confront emptiness with the object, in order to fill it with drives, of either gratification or disgust. Such a narcissistic parry may also assume the guise of an imaginary flight that sets the symbolic register in motion (hence feverish activity, etc.). Yet such activism remains "futile," "empty": like an exile. Susan often stages similar narcissistic triumphs through a seemingly masochistic scenario: she is the third party to the fun enjoyed by a friendly couple, mom and dad, who naturally use her and then brush her aside, but with respect to whom she definitely has the feeling of being the "center," a "nonentity" perhaps, but which allows each one separately and both together to be Everything—to joy.

The vanished father, the traveling father is apparently the dynamic cause of the hysterical person's elated disappointment. He is neither repudiated (making one psychotic) nor dead (obsessive), but a father in the "past conditional" who "might have" but "is not. . . ." A touch of . . . Possibly. . . . But no. . . . Susan's tragic story should not conceal from us a more common situation: the hysteric's father is always already a "dear departed" from the mother's desire, an absent one from the grieving maternal field, always already disappointed, in comparison to which the imaginary father of the future speaking being does not carry enough weight; in comparison to which he may clear himself only by withdrawing his case, by admitting he has no place.

Within such a context, the analyst is expected to "smooth over" the subject's narcissism. On the side of abjection it is the mother's transverbal impact that is obviously summoned in the transference, through the onrush of a stimulus that withdraws from language and extends beyond it. That unrepresentable affect is often seen as a complaint lodged by hysterical discourse against a language that "does not get to the bottom of things." It may also be experienced as imaginary pain or as true somatic suffering. Nevertheless, the turnstile of narcissistic chiasm may direct transference toward the imaginary father. The subject is then assigned, through idealization of the analyst, to the father of his or her individual prehistory: man and woman, father and mother, a kind of bisexual being. All levels are thus brought into play within a seductive discourse, before the representatives (verbal or behavioral) of this agitation appear severed from the affects, null and void in the judgment of Oedipal authority, into which the imaginary father has not been integrated.

Serving as stimulation-shield by strictly maintaining the analytic framework; arousing the flow of drives by interpreting the idealizing transference within the analytic framework—that is what allows the analyst, in a first step, to have the analysand erect a dynamics of his imagos and, through that very process, a dynamics of the signs that bespeak him or her. It is clear that such a dynamics, which aims to give meaning back to disappointed discourse by having it encounter drive motions (Triebregung), goes through a restructuring of narcissism, whose deficiencies are doubtless the basis of what appears like a disappearance of representation.

IX

Baudelaire, or
Infinity, Perfume, and Punk

"On the vaporization and centralization of the Self. There is no escaping that."

"Love is a liking for prostitution. . . . What is art? Prostitution."

(Fusées)

WITH BAUDELAIRE (1821–67), the text prevails over amatory experience, explicit as it may be, and a reading of it identifies two shores: on the one hand, a passion (it has been called "sadistic")[1] that destroys bodies; on the other, elated veneration of an inaccessible although absolute and necessary ideal. Baudelaire as lover, Baudelaire as a Catholic, Baudelaire as a mystic: they may not be the same but they constitute a text wholly founded on metaphor.

Narcissus Before the Court of Philosophy

Hearkening to Baudelairian intimacy in a text made famous by a controversy with Georges Bataille,[2] Sartre discovered in the poet the "dark and lucid tête-à-tête of a heart that has become its own mirror." So here we have Narcissus before the court of philosophy, condemned for having gone no further than "introspective consciousness"—a simple "abortive outline" of duality that was unable to attain the condition of a rebellious philosophical consciousness. In short, Sartre demands that Baudelaire, even though labeled as Narcissus, reflect according to "secondary systems," one might say, and even assume philosophical or political positions. As a result, he misses the true Baudelairian world where war is being waged against abjection[3] in order to maintain a precocious Self, mean because delicate, loving because separate. In the presence of so heartrending an account of our amatory identity's archeology as found in Baudelaire's poetry and suffering caused by separation and decay, it is a misinterpretation, to say the least, to suggest that the poet should have proclaimed to the face of his family and society that "their bourgeois virtues are atrocious and stupid." The Baudelairian corpus is fully affirmed and exhausted within the metaphorical conveyance: it has no other "position" than to raise and shatter meaning. Was there really an "abdication" on the poet's part when he supposedly chose to side with the executioners (the cross, the Academy)? Or on the contrary did he not assume an unbearable stance between animality and Law, in which sadistic, weary figures reveal the deep logic of meaning's difficult, violent emergence in the speaking being?

And yet this "failure" produces a "human oeuvre," and such an inauthentic act, in Sartre's view, redeems his misanthropy through a "humanism of creation." Now, matters being more complex, it is the "Christian imprint" of this "humanism" that henceforth poses a problem. And Sartre contrasts it with the "sexual abnormality" of Gide who, on his part, supposedly asserted an other morality. Note, indeed, the Catholic solution for jouissance with Baudelaire: less moral and even less moralizing, it causes sin to exist within the law, the unnamable within the baroque passion of signs, the infinity of the creator within the narrow, asthenic finiteness of created beings. Nevertheless, and beyond that ideology, for Baudelaire, as happens more commonly with poets rather than prose writers, it was the process of writing that produced, along with uncommonness, the very possibility of his jouissance. It is there that his individual difference, his spleen or suffering, can be experienced, not as ossified but as elated—a burning crest between infinity and nothingness. It harbors but a single enthusiasm: that which accompanies the advent and recasting of signs. Such a process recalls that of a childhood perpetually remolded, the "childhood" indeed so often claimed by the poet, which critics tend to identify with the tender age previous to the act of writing: it is perhaps nothing but a body totally eroticized, as is the body of the real child, but in this case by dint of having written itself into the multiplicity of its levels, moving from drives to ideals.

"Childhood recovered at will," that "child [who] sees everything as a novelty; he is always intoxicated"—is that any different from the archeologist-writer of our narcissisms, torn between ideal and abjection, image and emptiness, infinity and lack of meaning?

Still the question arises: what is this ideal agency of soundness and morality but also of generous support that appears to be the ultimate addressee of such writing, which is nevertheless elated by evil? Joseph de Maistre provided Baudelaire not only with theocracy as a global framework for a possible society but also his understanding of the prayer that brings together, as Georges Blin accurately pointed out, the will of the orant and a self-idolatry yet regulated from outside: prayer is a "dynamics entrusted to man." Baudelaire, however, reflects that system within his own dynamic and, from theocracy, he retains only the absolute necessity for an Other, a pure place of being that need not be inhabited by an actual being. "Even if God did not exist, Religion would still be holy and divine" (Fusées). In similar fashion, at the time of his religious crisis of 1861, he wrote his mother, "During three months, through a peculiar but merely apparent contradiction, I prayed at all hours (whom? what specific being? I absolutely do not know)" (letter dated April 1, 1861). Throughout his writing there remains that straining toward such a place: it is an agency he needs in order to write his loss and his spleen—a principle whose position assures him that he is a "hyperbolic soul" fully in a state of "levitation" (Art romantique), which is strongly suggested by such titles as "The Fountain" or "Fusées."

The paternal function? More precisely the place of the "imaginary father" (see section titled "An 'Immediate' and Objectless Identification" in One), which allows the narcissistic structure to unfold and bespeak itself. If Baudelaire is being looked at by someone who makes him exist in his delicate identity, that other is indeed none other than the symbol itself: ". . . through forests of symbols that observe him with friendly glances." He extolled that loving Other in one of the versions of "Le Vin des chiffoniers" ["The Ragpickers' Wine"]: "Grandeur of the kindness of the one whom everything names."

The affliction of narcissistic passion with a mother as perfumed as she was tyrannical was then stated, thanks to the contract fulfilled with "the kindness of the one whom everything names," as a blossoming of signs—as a painful, ecstatic manipulation of language, as "flowers of evil."

On the contrary, when we exhaust bodies and souls in sexual fury, with neither respite, trace, nor symbol, we are often disappointed lovers, and our discourse, emptied of meaning, reveals, beyond maternal ascendancy, the wretchedness of our ties to our fallen fathers. So it was with Justine . . .

Justine or the Dried Branch

She averts her eyes from mine during the first meetings, blushes from the roots of her hair to the depths of her provocative V-cut dress, or bursts into a nervous giggle when her discourse, in spite of herself, leads her to speak of her anxieties. Added to her perverse phobia there is a keen mind of which she is obviously proud but of which she is more and more deprived by inhibition. A once brilliant scholar, Justine "destroys herself" in unbounded sexuality ("I won't come out of it with a whole skin") that exhausts her and leads her to the brink of slummerization.

Justine says she knows the root of her condition: a mother she is ashamed of, an adventuress, an insatiable destroyer of lovers who are increasingly younger and whom she wishes to share with her daughter—the latter being more or less fascinated or frightened by the proposition. "Stifling," "disgusting," the mother supposedly has let Justine catch a serious skin disease in her early childhood, and this severed her from any other contact. Later, Justine stood aloof from that "boundless horror" by devoting herself to her studies. "I had to do something that she didn't understand, I had to get out of her world." The "exit from the world" also turned out to be, as if a mirror image of the maternal "shame," an erotic overreaction into which she threw herself headlong for the intense joy of men and women.

She describes the details of that unbridled erotics with perverse reticence but also with an authentic sense of decency. For her symbolic means are split from a body that founders in Eros, and they sensibly stand back from those orgies in which only the skin loses its wholeness: "When I come out of them my skin hurts." "My skin is sore and painful."

In fact Justine suffers, as she says with the help of transference, because she is not loved and loves not. "I cannot find the right distance." A recent lover, Louis, a drug addict, "all considered, prefers his own mother." The most recent one, Bernard, behaves like a woman during the sexual act and "seeks only pleasure from me, but doesn't let me sleep at his place." Justine has cloistered herself in a small room, which she leaves only for her more and more wearisome orgies, but she has lately begun to live in it in order to write poems. Her father ("a pervert, a weakling, a failure, whose only thought was to seduce me") used to call her little girl's sex "my Muse."

Out of that father's nonexistence, and he was a nonentity in the mother's eyes, Justine extracted an image: the only paternal work, a laurel he had planted in the yard, is henceforth completely dead. Meanwhile Justine has a dream. In an auto jammed between a woman-man and a man-woman, suffocating in that confined space, she hears a loudspeaker announce her father's promotion to the position of Chairman. The "stupidity" of that dream first makes her laugh, then she is moved by her desire to have her father "promoted." From that moment, abandoning the more or less masochistic auto(eroticism) of her orgies becomes Justine's ambition; she does not set out in search of an ideal image of her father (that person's real nature apparently does not lend itself to it), rather, she aims for the true creation of a loving relationship to the extent that it supposes an object that can be idealized. Let the other not be absolute hell, be it ecstatic or built on repeated erotics, but let him be capable of a modicum of sublimeness. Let the sublime exist. Justine became aware of it when, visiting her childhood home, she discovered that her father's laurel had only one dried branch, and everywhere else was bursting with sap and greenery. And all that in her absence.

The analyst, along this journey, was not supposed to be a loving "good mother," nor to play the part of a stern "true father." Rather, to be situated as a stranger—giving an interpretation that was sexed and distant at the same time, punctuating Justine's suffocation less with her silence than with actual speech. A speech—a fount of time that defers phobic anguish in the face of erotic outburst (of the container; the skin), and binds affects within an alien object, third party, bestower of meaning (of form, image, idea) at the very spot where passional fury is unleashed. Is not the sublime the form of that fury?

Even though psychoanalytic speech operates in the same spot, this does not endow it with the sublimity of art (which makes us see or hear fury but, precisely on account of such miming, cleanses us of it by displacing it into a style), nor the dignity of mastery (which bans fury and rests its power upon that repression). Momentary, essentially unfinished, neither oeuvre nor law, sound psychoanalytic interpretation, when it affects and relieves sexual fury in order to displace it and have it exist as such but also to expend it within the level of meaning—that interpretation creates love's time and space.

Constructivism and Pain: The Poetic Sign

When Baudelaire placed symbolic principle above everything else as prerequisite for his poetic creation, he placed himself in opposition to the Surrealist trend that, for its part, turned the body into an absolute.[4] Purely abstract, mathematical so to speak, Baudelaire's infinite is somewhat constructivist: it allows him "to poetize" in the etymological meaning of "to make." "All is number, the number is in all, the number is in the individual" (Fusées). All art is a marking-out of space, for the arts "are number and number is a translation of space."[5] When, in Artificial Paradises, he rebelled against the "depravity of the taste for the infinite," he aspired to be God through the very construction of the poem, which should move away not only from sight and lowly passion but also from signification so as to rival music. Sight then becomes the "inner ear" and the ear is "inner sight" in order to assimilate that wholly numerical construction known as music.[6] Did Baudelaire really proclaim the death of sensation, or was it a supreme encoding of the signifier as close as possible to the sensation—a "worship of expanded sensations expressed by means of music" (My Heart Laid Bare)?

Bearing sublimatory effort and erotic lasciviousness, such a music is, like Baudelaire's art in general, close to pain: "The violin rips like a blade seeking the heart"; or again, "Sounds of an irritating, tender music / Like distant cries of human pain" (The Solitary's Wine).

Endlessly to utter the symbolic and the imaginary's infinity, such is no longer Narcissus' objective but that of the artist who creates himself through numbers and signs. According to Sartre, Baudelaire's infinite is something nonfinished, "what cannot come to an end," "almost reached and yet beyond one's grasp." One might argue, however, that the infinity of the poetic conveyance of meaning, the undecidable connotation of poetic language, are closer to the notion put forward by Baudelaire the theorist. If it be true that Baudelairian man is "a conjunction of two opposite but equally centrifugal motions, one of which goes upward and the other downward," it is easy to see that this tension is precisely what Baudelaire brings about in and through his esthetics, and more restrictively in and through the very rhythm of his metaphors, abyss and at the same time infinity of meaning?

Baudelaire is wholly a being of signs, and he never appears to have been "substantially with God."[7] "But my heart, never visited by ecstasy / Is a theater where one awaits / Always, always in vain . . ."[8] If the subject of writing is equated with God only through an exquisite manipulation of language, that God is not a Self. No internality, constituted by the introjection of loved parents, can fill it. The milestones of his identity remain external: they are symbols and only allow one to mark out borders that open out on the two separate domains of the infinite and of its ruin. Consequently, the constructor of infinity is the constructor of nothingness. "Morally and physically as well, I have always had a feeling of the abyss, not only the abyss of sleep but the abyss of action, dreams, memory, desire, sight, remorse, beauty, number, etc."[9]

Amor Mortis: A Sham

In this perspective love is not only the reverse side of death but it relies on death: "Love is seated on the skull of Mankind" (Love and the Skull). Overcome by Narcissus' emptiness, the loved one is herself stripped of her passion, devitalized, skeletonlike. She has thus been made less dangerous than a mother who desires and abandons. "Imagine a tall feminine skeleton all decked out to go to a party. . ." (Salon, 1859). One can see then how love may be imagined as a "restrained, mysterious, shrouded love, in the guise of a canoness" (Fusées).

To say that it is also a "prostitution," like art, constitutes more than a paradoxical reversal of the first proposition. There is, with the canoness, as with the prostitute or the poet, a dying out of crude desires, a transfusion of the subject into seeming, which is accompanied by problematic and protected jouissance originating with an Other lover—an imaginary father who shelters and enlightens at the same time. It is he who allows the wounded Self to live on for the sake of One meaning . . . Elsewhere . . . Prostitution is the negative of sublimation: art's nocturnal sister.

"For passion is a natural thing, too natural even, and it consequently inserts a cutting, discordant tone into the area of pure beauty; too casual and too violent, and it thus scandalizes pure Desire, graceful Melancholy, and dignified Despair, which dwell in the supernatural regions of poetry."[10] The Baudelairian beauty worship is a worship of sublimation itself—a process that neutralizes the body, passions, and everything that recalls, more or less closely, the family/cradle of desires. Within fantasy, such sublimation goes hand in hand with rigor mortis or frigid stiffness, doubtless because "corpse" and "frigidity" are part of the protection against eroticism, which in the final analysis is always family-centered. These morbid and cold themes amount to a certain degree of ebbing of sublimatory realization, or else its neurotic psychological negative.

On the contrary, when signs fully assume infinite correspondences, when they turn into amalgamating nebulae, condensing perfumes moreover so frequently evoked by Baudelaire—then rigidity evaporates and the Self, vaporized in generalized metaphoricalness, becomes the ground not of "lowly" passion but of jouissance itself, which has been fully turned into beauty. This should be understood as sign or rather a conjunction of signs, an undecidable signification of metaphorical games: "Something rather vague, leaving the way open to conjecture."

Writing filters jouissance, while fantasy ("carrions," "skeleton," "frigidity," etc.) and the existential attitude it brings about (spleen, acting the dandy) are intended to check it. Not to eliminate it but to hold it back. Guilt feelings would be the cause of that restraint, the assumption being that there is something in poetic and phantasmal economy that is unacceptable for the other, something the other rejects. "Sentenced to death for a forgotten offence. . . . I do not question the accusation. A major offence not explained in the Dream."[11] Is it that precocious exaltation of the Self, remembrance of early childhood (cf. "Morality of the Toy") that Baudelaire dreads? One of the arrangements between offence and diabolic thirst could be found in that ironic, feigned, fake call to the mother through illness or evil, which so often appears to be a mere sham. "How much I suffer to see you suffering; what could be more true and believable—but deep down I consider all this as sheer overstatement";[12] "I am and have always been both responsible and dissolute";[13] "I have a diabolical thirst for sensual pleasure, glory, and power."[14]

Carrion and Paradise

Two amatory objects punctuate the Baudelairian text, and for numerous commentators they recall the mystical experience.

On the one hand the abject: woman/nature, lowly body, carrion, rot. Thus, ". . . a loathsome carrion / on a rock-strewn bed" (A Carrion); Wretch, who bind me to you close," "your vampire's waiting corpse" (The Vampire); "I shall strike you without anger" or "And yet you will be like that filth, / Like that frightful stench" (A Carrion); likewise, "As eager worms assail a corpse" (I worship you . . .); or, contrary to the Virgin, the poet's mother, "Chosen among all women / to be my wretched husband's shame" (Benediction); finally, confronting George Sand, whom he saw as the incarnation of the Devil, the poet "cannot think of that stupid being without some quivering of horror" (My Heart Laid Bare). "The trouble with love is that it is a crime one cannot commit without an accomplice" (My Heart Laid Bare); "Love in its sentry box, / Darkly waiting in ambush, bends its fatal bow. / I know the instruments of its old armory: / Crime, horror, and madness!" (Autumn Sonnet).

On the other hand, an idealized height that the intellect calls God ("God is the only being who, in order to rule, does not even have to exist," Fusées) and who, subjectively, calls forth the memory of a sublime mother—sublime because loved in the shadow of an imaginary, pre-Oedipal father. Thus, "I have kept the form and the divine essence / Of my decayed loves" (A Carrion), "Drawn from the sacred font of primal rays" (Benediction), the "Mother of memories, mistress of mistresses" (The Balcony), "Dazzles brilliant as the Dawn / And consoles like the Night" (Altogether), and she is like those "Bright stars whose brilliance no sun can wilt" (The Living Torch). "There was in my childhood a time when I was passionately in love with you: listen and read this without fear. . . . I was always alive in you, you were solely in me. You were at the same time an idol and a pal" (Letter to Mme Aupick, May 6, 1861). "I feel that I am missing something. . . . It is you that I miss. I miss that presence, someone with whom one can speak of all sorts of things, with whom one laughs without embarrassment of any kind." "An early taste for the world of Women, for mundi mulieris, with all its flowing, sparkling, perfumed paraphernalia, makes for superior intellects."[15] "How far away, that fragrant paradise" (Moesta et Errabunda), etc.

Such Baudelairian splitting has been seen as embodied in concrete amatory relationships: on the one hand, sex with Jeanne Duval, crystallized love with Marie Daubrun and Mme Sabatier on the other. Its baleful, aggressive, sadistic impact has particularly been stressed: "There is in the act of love a great resemblance with torture or with a surgical operation";[16] the "sole, supreme voluptuous pleasure in love lies in the certainty one has of doing evil."[17] Finally, critics have deemed themselves struck by his "impotence" (Sartre).

It would, however, be frivolous to see in Baudelaire's negative image of woman simply an Oedipal, incestuous revenge against the wife of General Aupick. In more basic fashion, the "carrion" refers to a limitless, nonsensed body, harrowed by a crisis that one might call deeply narcissistic. Indeed, if one grants that in literature as in dreams that of which "I" dream or "I" speak is what "I" am, then Baudelaire's abjections are just as many blurrings between subject and other—corruptions of an identity that is acknowledged as impossible.

Nevertheless, that abjection is sustained, exists, and is expended through a contract that irremediably constitutes the one who speaks as sign, as letter, as text. "God is a scandal, a scandal that yields a profit" (Fusées). If there is mysticism or mystery in Baudelaire, this is where they lie: in the possibility for that dilapidated Self to call itself metaphorically "vaporized" thanks to the contract upheld with the Other. It comprises, through broadened metaphoricalness, the whole range of psychicism and takes the place of primal repression. Generated by the devices of language, such a "vaporization" for that kind of identity shaking could only result in the downfall of psychology. It is known, since Freud, that the latter is the discourse of the Self as projection of the body proper, indeed of its surface (its skin). Here, on the contrary, a subject centered "over there," elsewhere (see the Invitation to a Journey: "There, all is order and beauty. . ."), changes the discourse of the body-Self's decay into . . . perfume.

With Baudelaire, perfume has fusional connotations that condense the intoxicated memory of an invading as well as invaded maternal body—which are doubtless the sublime reverse of aggressive ejection. But perfume is also and especially the allegory of the pulverizing of meaning and language, the pulverizing of one's own identity. "Old working-class suburbs, to me everything becomes allegory." Now it is the very dynamics of allegory, or, more restrictively and sharply, the dynamics of metaphor, that constitutes the nucleus of Baudelaire's mysticism.

Swedenborg's influence, as well as that of Louis-Claude de Saint-Martin and especially Joseph de Maistre, is to be taken into account for the experiment that instituted "correspondences" and a universal symbolism. Moreover, the writings of Edgar Allen Poe, E.T.A. Hoffman, and Thomas De Quincey, among Baudelaire's favored references, emphasize the reversibility of sensations and images. Finally hashish and all drugs along with it leads to the mixing of perceptions when, all borders being confused, "From time to time, your personality will vanish."[18]

As with the metaphysical reasoning that deduced from the "ad unum" principle the consequence that the predicates of phenomena could be stated only by means of analogy, Baudelaire, stretching out for the logical possibility of One Other, a generous sustainer, considered his own discourse as a network of analogies. Hence creation is seen as a "nearly divine ability" to imagine "the secret, intimate relationships between things, correspondences and analogies" and for that very reason, "positively related to infinity."[19] And again: "I have always enjoyed seeking in external, visible nature examples and metaphors that I might use to characterize impressions and joys of a spiritual nature."[20] "Reasoning man did not wait for Fourier to be born before understanding that nature is a verb, an allegory, a mould, a work in repoussé, so to speak. . . . We know it on our own and through the poets,"[21] "things have always been expressed by means of reciprocal analogy, since the day God uttered the world as complex and invisible totality."[22] In the universe tensed between the void and infinity, love is also, it is above all an analogy: "In that beautiful country, so peaceful. . . would you not be surrounded by your analogy, and might you not, to speak as the mystics do, gaze at yourself in your own correspondence?" (Invitation to a Journey, prose poem).

The Amatory Metaphor: "Spiritual" and "Antithetical"

Nevertheless, Baudelaire clearly shows that the motivation as well as the ultimate evidence of those "mystical metamorphoses" are to be found in the economy of art itself, particularly of poetry.

Every metaphor points to the crossroads where the writer (or the lover) finds himself magnetized on all sides, toward Flowers and toward Evil, torn between the "language of flowers" and that of "mute things." For every metaphor precisely is a fusion (identification) of the figurative with the figure; it is at the same time "elevation" (De Maistre said "levitation," which is so favorable to Baudelairian ecstasy; see The Fountain, Elevation) of meaning, through mingling significations, toward the infinity of connotation and the void of nonmeaning.

Fusion: in opposition to the simile in which both terms remain intact, the metaphor reduces duality without for that matter excluding any of the parts ("Every contradiction is reconciled").[23] What is more, the Baudelairian metaphor is often governed by the "spiritual," abstract, semantic focus of metaphorical condensation; consequently the concrete and the abstract of a common metaphor lose their specific consistencies and give the impression of extended symbolism, generalized idealization. "The universal correspondence and symbolism, that repertory for every metaphor," Baudelaire writes in Romantic Art,[24] thus precisely shedding light on the well-known sonnet, Correspondences. As an upshot of Baudelaire's metaphor, it is less the spiritual that is imagined through identification with a sensorial object than the object that allows itself to be grasped through identification with the spiritual. Thus, "This furniture, these flowers are you. They are still you. . . these peaceful canals that reflect the depth of the sky" (Invitation to a Journey, prose poem). Or, "Your dress shall be my desire," "And from my jealousy . . . I shall contrive to fashion you a Mantle . . . lined with suspicion," "I shall make you from my Respect a pair of fine Slippers" (To a Madonna). "Your head, your gesture, and your air / Are beautiful like a beautiful landscape" (To One Who Is Too Cheerful). Indeed, Baudelaire speaks of those "moral feelings that are transmitted to us by a visible being," and of "the moral meaning of color, outline, sound, and scent" (Romantic Art).[25]

Moreover, the abundance of antithetical metaphors ("Nature is a temple where living pillars": nature vs. temple, living vs. pillars) brings to the fore Baudelaire's propensity to put together numerous texts on the basis of the binary logic that so delighted structuralists.[26]

Baudelaire often asserts his universe's dichotomy as the equivalent of an impossible love: "I am like a painter whom a mocking God / Has condemned to paint, alas, over darkness" (A Phantom, 1); or, "I am like the king of a rainy land, / Rich but helpless, young and yet very old, / Who, scorning his tutors' fawning, / Is bored with his dogs as with other animals" (Spleen, 3). The contradictory logic of Baudelaire's metaphors foreshadows Surrealism and takes root in a fantastic or baroque tradition he likes to evoke when speaking of Pieter Brueghel or Grandville: "Visions of a diseased mind, feverish hallucinations, sudden changes as in dreams, strange associations of ideas, fortuitous and sundry combinations of forms."[27]

Is not metaphorical condensation also the infrastructure of Baudelairian time: the instant that includes eternity but may also be pulverized by death, as metaphor is composed of infinite meanings and of the absurd? "I want everything, all at once" (letter dated December 20, 1855); he also wants to "win Paradise at one stroke" (Artificial Paradises), and "see three minutes in one" (Fusées); likewise, the supreme benefit of drugs is, according to the poet, to save on "the work of time."

In fact, such antithesism appears to be the revelation, on a logical plane, of a splitting between Self and meaning, a splitting that produces infinity: "I see only infinity, through all the windows" (The Abyss). The limit, which constitutes the univocal units of language as well as their system, is tolerable only if bearing infinity, to which the poet aspires as much as his accursed women, "seekers of infinity." The lovers' orgy, like writing, is glorified for remaining unsated but stretched out toward infinity: "Who cares if you come from heaven or hell / . . . / If, with your eyes, your smile, your foot, you open the door / On Infinity I love and have never known" (Hymn to Beauty).

Beyond the absence of pleasure that the act of writing may nevertheless produce and renew indefinitely from one text to another toward the infinity of meaning and readings, this "Infinity . . . never known" is also the horizon of the emptiness that "hollows out the Heavens." But before displaying horrified pity for the writer's sexual impotence, one should see that this involves the semantic emptiness that shatters each metaphor with enigmas and rests perhaps on a fundamental narcissistic disappointment where meaning as condensation, as well as poetic statement, finds its origin. "Emptiness (the feeling of indefinite emptiness)" is the title of an intended poem by Baudelaire.[28]

Synaesthesia: The Reign of Perfume or the Invisible Metaphor

Nevertheless, metaphorical condensation is a "process stretching over the whole course of events till the perceptual region is reached."[29] It is precisely there that metaphor becomes metamorphosis or synaesthesia and the infinitized meaning of the enigmas it produces turns to strictly sensuous, corporeal jouissance. "O mystic metamorphosis / Of all my senses merged in one!" "When all things enchant, I know not / If anything gives me delight" (Altogether), Baudelaire writes. And elsewhere he explains the disappearance of the object of love, which gives way to a true objectivation of the subject: "Your eye fastens upon a tree . . . something that would be a most natural comparison in the poet's mind, will become a reality in yours. First, you will attribute your passions, longings, and sadness to the tree; its moaning and swaying will become your own; and by and by, you are the tree itself."[30] It is a paroxysm of identification, in which, without a stable object, the lover identifies with his contemplation; he passes into it, he is it—the artist, like the poet, is both "cause and effect, subject and object, mesmerist and one entranced."[31] Every metaphor that one modestly takes to be a putting into image of an abstract term or one made commonplace is in fact pregnant with such a metamorphosis in which the subject becomes stable only by identifying with a heard, seen, touched, tasted, or smelled object. The acme of such a process, which recalls Freud's "primary identification," is, according to Baudelaire, bliss, the kef of the Orientals, where "man has become God." Would metaphor be the continuous celebration of primary identification?

The infrastructure of this state of writing (as one says: the state of love) is synaesthesia: a condensation of infrasigns, of semiotic indications that have a meaning without for that matter having a signification. Spread over the various perceptual ranges (hearing, sight, smell, taste, touch), they borrow their jouissance from one another in order to create expression and distinctive sensation: "Her breathing makes the music / As her voice emits the scent" (Altogether). But that exchange is also contamination and condensation. Synaesthesia: a metaphor in a language that is being destabilized, a language that is not yet, that already no longer is. One must once more reread Correspondences:

There are perfumes as fresh as children's flesh,
As soft as oboes, as green as meadows
—And others, corrupt, costly, and masterful,
Having the power of infinite things,
Like amber, musk, benjamin, and frankincense,
Which sing out the rapture of spirit and senses.[32]

Perfume: An Invisible Condensation

Perfume, a Baudelairian theme par excellence, the sublimation of rejection (of sadism?) and carrion ("Tyrannical Circe with her dangerous perfumes," The Voyage), becomes, at the conclusion of this sonnet, a metaphor of the metaphor that broadens its sway to include synaesthesia. Perfume: a blazon of correspondences that, far from instituting an esoteric hierarchy as happens with spiritualist authors, become condensed into words and feelings, thus pulverizing the identities of all. A bomb. An atomizer.

Perfume is thus the most powerful metaphor for that archaic universe, preceding sight, where what takes place is the conveyance of the most opaque lovers' indefinite identities, together with the chilliest words: "There are strong perfumes for which all matter / Is porous. They seem to penetrate glass" (The Flask).

Even when sensuous delight is triggered by a glance, the image is fleeting and elusive: the loved object does not allow itself to be grasped, for, basically, one cannot even look at it. "A flash of lightning . . . and then night! Fleeting beauty / Whose sudden glance gave me new life / Shall I not see you again until eternity? / Elsewhere, so far from here! Too late! Never, maybe! / For I know not where you flee, nor you where I go, / You whom I might have loved and who knew it! (In Passing). Baudelaire's world is a citified world and is essentially darkened by the absence of sunlight; it is merely a veiled, curved gaze, governed by the fire of passion: ". . . not a remnant / Of sun, not even near the horizon, / To light those wonders, / Which shined with a fire from within" (Parisian Dream). "Magical, profound charm, with which / Restored past intoxicates us within present time! / So does the lover, on the beloved body, / Gather memory's exquisite flower" (A Phantom, 2).

If truly the whole of Proust's universe may already be found in those lines, one will notice how, in very specifically Baudelairian fashion, metaphorical ambiguity is a motion that is hidden from view and, "with both eyes shut," conjures up the olfactory osmosis of an early age. Coming before the visual image, fusion with the loved woman leads the metaphor that has been called heliotropical toward its zenith and its collapse: toward the sun that bedazzles instead of shedding light, like an outsider who gives shape and yet works havoc. "When, with both eyes shut, on a warm autumn night, / I breathe the fragrance of your welcoming breasts, / I see prosperous shores unfold, / Dazzled by the rays of a monotonous sun" (By Association). Elsewhere, when he evokes an "aromatic forest" that suggests a distant past as much as a distant world, Baudelaire specifies, "As other souls float along on music, / Mine, O my love! swims in your perfume" (The Head of Hair). "And my subtle soul, caressed by the ship's roll, / Shall find you again, O fruitful idleness! / Endless rocking of fragrant leisure!" (The Head of Hair).

Nevertheless, such a world of jubilatory vaporishness is on the verge of extinction, for its ecstasy also contains a threat of death: "In your petticoats, fragrant with your perfume, / Bury my aching head, / And breathe in, as from a withered flower, / The mellow stench of my defunct love" (Lethe).

Further still, woman is ferocious, a feral, devouring beast, pure animal desire, and for that very reason dangerous to those who protect themselves from her through the mist of signs they call Beauty. "Uselessly your hand fondles my swooning breast; / What it seeks, my dear, has been plundered / By woman's claws and savage teeth. / No longer seek my heart; the beasts have devoured it" (Conversation). "Beauty, harsh scourge of souls! It is your will! / With your eyes of fire, sparkling like lights at a feast, / Burn up the tatters of flesh the beasts have spared" (Conversation).

Loss of identity emerges at the core of such an exploration, accompanied by the fear of being swallowed up in pleasure and nonconsciousness of sleep: "I fear sleep as one fears a deep hole, / Brimful of uncertain horror, leading I know not where" (The Abyss). Each Baudelairian love has that taste of death at the same time as it tastes of the sea / the mother: "These vows, these perfumes, these endless kisses, / Will they be reborne out of an abyss that we may not probe, / As rejuvenated suns ascend the skies / After they have been cleansed in the depths of the sea? / —O vows! O perfumes! O endless kisses!" (The Balcony). The scented cloud of intoxicated amorous identity is a field of violence, wounds, and devastation: "My heart is a palace ransacked by the mob, / There they get drunk, they kill, they come to blows. / —A perfume is wafted about your naked breast!" (Conversation).

But then where is the object of love in a metamorphosis that keels over from putrefaction to incense, from deadly sensual pleasure to knowledge ("ecstasy made of voluptuousness and knowledge")? The atomized Self can only be centered or written as metaphor. Conveyance. Metaphorein. Not a transfer toward an object but "levitation," lifting the self toward an invisible Other.

Baudelairian love might be the ability to utter evil as a putting to death, as a corruption of the Self. Metaphor as damaging the Single meaning, as symptom of its toppling over into infinity, is then the very discourse of love: the place where God topples over into Satan and vice versa.

Merely Appearances

The problem of the dandy remains.

It may well be that it is tied to "antinaturalness, artificiality, frigidity," as Sartre points out. A precociously wounded Narcissus might need a witness in order to set a specific image in the glance of that other; at the same time that authenticating glance turns him into a thing, settles him, deprives him of the delightful closeness residing in the uncertainty as to whether he is subject or object—and all that accounts for the ambiguity of the very notion of the dandy with Baudelaire. "The word dandy," he wrote, "implies a quintessential character and a subtle understanding of this world's entire ethical mechanics; but, on the other hand, the dandy yearns for insensibility, and that is why [Constantin] Guys who, for his part, is ruled by the unsatiable passion to see and perceive himself, sets himself violently apart from the dandy."[33] The provocative and exhibitionistic dandy would, in short, be closer to that lining of wounded narcissism that displays itself in feelings of solitude and emptiness—those temporary freezings of death drive. Putting together for oneself an aspect—an image—as artificial as it is shocking; an image that, on the one hand, signifies an absence of signification, and on the other a strenuous joke, a furious challenge to those naive advocates of authenticity who let themselves be impressed by such silly antics; such a behavior, nevertheless, is not simply a sociological uneasiness on the part of the "parasitical" artist in a bourgeois society. More profoundly, such "punk" behavior before the fact points to the bankruptcy of "one's own and proper," the death of the "authentic" within a subjectivity fully oriented toward a pre-Oedipal internal landscape, under the yet desired and hated shadow of the ideal. Merely provisional and empty compositions of an undecidable, fluid, ephemeral identity as in the unsettled waters of Narcissus' spring, but of which the poet becomes the explorer and, through language, the builder. In poetic art, the victim becomes a creator of his condition: by setting it to verse he brings forth, for him as well as for us, the painless, anaesthetized defense of a permanent suffering. The appearances put on are the bandages over unbeing, the anaesthesia against narcissistic pain. To say, as Sartre does, that it is a matter of "choice"—Baudelaire would have created himself such as family conditions had in fact prefabricated him—is inaccurate to the extent that it could not have involved a deliberate choice through which he would have been free to disregard some solutions and favor others. Does one really choose to be a poet rather than a philosopher or an engineer? "In art, and this is something one is not sufficiently aware of, the share left to man's will is much less important than people believe."[34]

The dandy's frigid parade with seeming, his taste for uncathected finery, is doubtless also an attempt to slip from feigned virility to an equally feigned femininity, only thus finally neutralized. In this domain of transference where identities are carried away by their inappropriateness, pretending to be a woman (those flowing locks, pink gloves, colored nails as well as hair) is not really a transition to homosexuality. What is lacking is the erotic obsession with the sadomasochistic quest of an object of the same sex. The dandy decked out as a woman is in fact rebelling against whatever (in others or in himself) tends to believe that the feminine, the maternal, is essential. Nothing is essential outside the seeming, the sign, the text, which have consumed affects—such is the dandy's facade; and that stifled cry, in its elegant and ecclesiastical rigidity, is one aspect of his struggle against maternal, feminine abjection (the "carrion"), from which he ceaselessly separates himself. The femininity the dandy allows himself is a symbolic creativity—poetry itself—that recognizes that its cause is external, even if he appropriates it to himself and uses it. God, or Joseph de Maistre's theocentric state, are perfectly adequate to the seeming's authority, which knows it depends not on opinion but on an external symbolic agency.[35] On this account, recognizing that symbolic dependency constitutes the true acknowledgment of a certain femininity that is inherent in each subject. It is probably thus that one should understand the following sibylline phrase in Fusées: "On the feminineness of the Church as reason for its omnipotence."[36] Under the influence of the Church, under the influence of the moon, but also as elected by the symbolic power: it is a stern but equally loving Other that the poet imagines in order to remain in the condition of writing.

The dandy's attitude has been described as a post-Flaubertian reaction to the aristocratic yearning of the artist, that "good for nothing" in the bourgeois world who seeks a symbolic community of the idle; it has also been asserted that in the painful case of the Baudelairian "choice" such an artist would accept only an elite of extravagant dissidents, even shattering the possibility of a group, noble as it might be—but to say that is to subject the intelligibility of writing to the sociological context of the industrial era. Far more fundamental, the motives and purposes of writing, and particularly of modern writing, which explores the dramas of one's own distinctness, are essentially solitary and asocial—closer to vaporizing or wandering psychosis than to neurosis, which imposes on the individual the erotic problem of the socius.

Romanticism, especially its late variants, following upon the institutional and religious crisis inaugurated by the French Revolution, put into words an unheard-of instability in the individual. Formerly, the powerful Earth, Sovereign, and God myths put a damper on wanderings at the borders of the speakable and visible. Or else, when that subjective instability could be spoken, miraculously and provisionally harmonized codes, such as the courtly code, took charge of it. Facing the major trends of realism, the "atomizing of the self" was enunciated only under the aegis of the divine ideal, through ecstasy or music. Nevertheless, a society based on productivity and need (of which Sartre's statements on poetry constitute the most forceful symptom) could only discredit the amatory experience and consign the writing of jouissance to the "parasitic" level. At best, that society saw it as a pretentiously aristocratic, decorative, or religious parasite, archaic at any rate, a relic of the past. If through a writing that is synonymous with the amatory condition—an experience at the limits of the identifiable—the writer can find no other place in the bosom of bourgeois society than that of a refugee at the side of nonproductive nobility or of the Church, which protects fetishes under the symbolic umbrella, we can only interpret that as an indictment of that very society rather than the evidence of the writer's error or "failure."

In such a context, Bataille preferred calling himself "guilty," claiming the right to sin, rather than claiming the right to assert the amatory condition. His victimized position, however, reveals the truth when it shows how social agreement, the more so as it emphasizes efficiency and the production of material goods, is put together by excluding—imbuing with a sense of guilt—the discourse of jouissance that is coextensive with the amatory condition.

Sublimation

Let us then think of Baudelaire in an open temporality: this prisoner of his century has written at least two thousand years of amatory history, his text is also atemporal.

Now two major figures of loving discourse emerge within our tradition. One works with entities that have been stabilized in the Oedipus complex. Through the passion-inspired torments caused by the amorous narcissistic crisis, the subject/object frontier is nevertheless maintained, under the tutelage of the ideal that allows the two narcissisms of the loving partners to merge while preserving their own distinctiveness.

The other discovers a wounded, pierced, bleeding Self that attempts to stave off its losses, or overcome its losses, through erotizing its parts or its rage. Perversion—from voyeurism and exhibitionism to the erotization of waste and sadomasochism—then proposes its screen of abjects, fragile films, neither subjects nor objects, where what is signified is fear, the horror of being one for an other. This is the "tyranny of the human face," for a modern "Heauton Timoroumenos" ("I am the wound and the knife!").

That hell, however, possesses its re-verse: the metaphor and the metamorphosis through which the pangs of love become a condition of writing. Writing as an emptying out—or infinitization, or indefinitization—of perversion. It has become, in that very process, a-psychological, or worse: inhuman, satanic, or angelic. Would metaphor be, on the near and far side of angels and satans, the successful discourse of an amorous pervert who, out of his unstable objects, produces a cloud of meaning, and thus transfers his solid, suffering, and parceled body to the scented sublimation of a language in the throes of condensation?

If such is the case, what triggers this discourse, what causes the metaphor to exist?

One thinks of Joseph-François Baudelaire, the poet's father, priest, philosopher, civil servant in the Senate. . . There remains, nevertheless, an enigmatic area of darkness. The unknown. Like a metaphor.

Stendhal and
The Politics of the Gaze:
An Egotist's Love

"Indeed, love, for me, has always been the greatest of concerns, or rather the only one."

(The Life of Henry Brulard)

A Politics of Passion: The Red and the Black, The Charterhouse of Parma

FOR LOVE TO EMERGE in the writings of Stendhal (1783–1842) out of the tragic and ironical magnificence of a political affair, what was perhaps needed was the France of Charles X—obsessed as it was with the violence of the Revolution and Napoleon's wars as much as with the haughty nobility of the Middle Ages.

This is not to say that Balzac, with the likes of his Rastignac and Chouans, had not already accustomed us to that. But within the complexity of the human comedy, Balzacian love seems to dominate the game no more than money or the flesh. On the other hand, in the "Chronicle of 1830," published by Stendhal in his forties, the trial of Antoine Berthet (as recorded in La Gazette des Tribunaux in December 1827) gave the man who wrote On Love (1822) the pretext for subtly displaying love as the intimate, supreme stage of politics. The political import of Stendhal's The Red and the Black doubtless also lies, as has often been stressed, in the "mirror" that he carries along through the social life and anxieties of Paris and the provinces, including the estates of the Jura mountains, the salons of Paris, or aristocratic plots. More intrinsically, however, a metamorphosis of amatory feeling, which, from being romantic or mystic, becomes a political love, expresses the novelist's fundamental standpoint just as it reflects a new mentality. The one Stendhal foresaw as dominating in the twentieth century. "As for me, I have a lottery ticket whose grand prize amounts to this: I shall be read in 1935."[1]

Religion had been corrupted, hypocrites were everywhere. Amatory alchemy would therefore not take refuge in an ecstatic extolling of the adored or loathed other. Madame de Rênal's phobic spontaneity and mystical outbursts amount to an archaism that will be swept away in the political second half of the novel; they will recover their spellbinding power only as stand-ins for death, facing the guillotine. In fact, eternal love is historical just the same, and with The Red and the Black its new complexion would be moulded; its features were borrowed from the cold and calculating, ambitious and underhanded logic that went with conquest of power in the state.

Eight years later, in The Charterhouse of Parma (1838, published in 1839), there appeared Fabrice, who, like Julien Sorel, dreamed of Napoleon, but this time not by the side of the lady he worked for and who was to be conquered; instead, caught between his mother and his aunt, he saw only one opportunity for his life—succeed in his career and/or be appealing to women. Nevertheless, when he realized that Sanseverina loved him, the ambitious young man decided to avoid her without offending her in order "not to jeopardize his future." Since power is dependent upon women, everything rests upon the love one pretends to have for women, but which in fact is destined to power alone. But how does one tell pretence from reality? Love and politics were thus to become one . . .

Such politicization of love doubtless brings its dynamic close to the sadomasochistic test of strength specific to the Platonistic homosexual Eros—and Julien Sorel, as lover of Mathilde de la Mole, readily acknowledges the presence of masculine traits in her. Nonetheless, the array of ploys and pretences put forward in order to insure the sway of one lover over the other—Stendhal often described them as "enemies," and Mathilde was carried away at the thought that Julien might be a Danton—was supported by a sea of fondness where tears combined with the physical charms that feed passion.

Strangely, the carpenter's son from the Jura mountains lacked a mother; the reader is fully cognizant of the father's gloomy, perfidious miserliness, and even of the torments the younger Julien suffered at the hands of his brothers and enemies since his earliest years; but what happened to his mother in all that? Madame de Rênal's lot was to fill the lack, and she did this admirably—voluptuous origin of the first adolescent passion, mellow sensuousness of death's painful moments. The Duchess del Dongo, Fabrice's mother, was also quite subdued, and she delegated her function, but this time in an even more incestuous fashion, to the paternal aunt, the fateful Gina del Dongo Pietranera Sanseverina Mosca, whose multiplicity of names brings her close to the masked character Stendhal imagined himself to be. The sublime Sanseverina embodied an "instinctual love," familial and archaic, infinitely more authoritarian and virile than was Madame de Rênal's; she masterfully managed the schemes, deceptions, expediencies, an entire politics keeping passion in check, to serve her nephew's ambition. Where did Sanseverina's will or desire end, where did Fabrice's will and desire begin? Who was the other's weapon? Or were they both pawns of Love/Enterprise such as Stendhal's novel created it, a narrative of war and conquest, crimes and prisons, blood and masks, in other words, of "modern" love? "When in love with a woman one asks, 'What do I want to make of her?'"[2]

Deadly passion, death passion—that is the dependent reverse of the stratagem love that nevertheless structures Julien's and Madame de Rênal's universe as much as it does the one of Julien and Mathilde and, yet more insidiously, Fabrice's love as it is played out between Gina and Clelia. Nonetheless, its deceptions and expediencies let themselves be overcome by the vertigo of maternal voluptuousness. In truth, political love does not believe in God, it goes so far as ironically to pulverize the sacred spontaneity of amatory feeling. But it always glorified itself with a secret mother's aura.

Before returning to this maternal aspect of political love, let me first attempt to bring out its properly expedient or diplomatic workings. The ambition that inspires it relates it to a Don Juanism more anxious to conquer than to possess. Loving, for Julien Sorel, means acting like Napoleon, being strongest. Hypocrisy, initially at least (the lover sets off on the conquest of his prey from a base of utter coldness, without any sensuousness, without excitement, as if to defy the impotence of that fellow whose misfortunes Armance relates), turns Stendhal's lover into a Tartufe of affect. Never indeed has love been less affectionate, never so fully resolute. And never has it been so explicitly love for the Other, under the secular trappings of power or glory, before being love for a woman. Julien did not love his employer spontaneously, and if he became interested in Madame de Rênal it was to make sure that, on the battlefield assigned to him in the park of Verrières or that of Vergy, lacking a true Napoleonic struggle, he could be like Napoleon. For Madame de Rênal's delightful error, which, like many intuitions of jealousy, points to the truth, was to believe that Julien's real mistress was the person whose portrait was hidden in his mattress. That person, one will recall, was none other than Napoleon himself . . .

Sanseverina's political genius was indissociable from her amatory genius and vice versa. The visible aspect of love, in The Charterhouse of Parma, unfolded of course on account of Fabrice's quick-tempered heedlessness during his duel with Giletti; above all, however, and in keeping with the very architecture of the novel, it unfolded at the court of the kings of Parma thanks to Sanseverina's calculated coquetry. Stendhal has split his erotic universe: Sanseverina waged the political war in society, Fabrice took refuge in the secret of a masked tenderness for Clelia, within the confines of the fortress. The court is one stage, the fortress another, diplomacy versus prison, battle versus solitude: the lover is nevertheless caught in a game of hide and seek, he has no hold on his object. But two games are available to him: politics for and with Sanseverina, impassioned observation for Fabrice, Clelia's tender but far from passionate seducer. For basically both aunt and nephew have "a political heart."

Love's lightning bolts, however, tear to pieces this game of mastery and calculation; and it so happens that women, rather than men, experience them. Madame de Rênal, Clelia, and even the austere descendent of Boniface de la Mole are beings who do not know what is happening to them—surprised and carried away by an uncontrollable love. Julien, too, discovered that, "in fact, he was passionately in love with her [Madame de Rênal]," and Lucien Leuwen, when Madame de Chasteller was introduced, lost his voice so that "the little he said was practically unintelligible." This disturbing abyss of passion nevertheless opens up only on the surface of calculated politics. The latter doubtless is inescapably characteristic of the time, but it is equally so as phantasmatic carrying out of the power seizure that alone interests the egotist—and he calls it "love." With Stendhal the lover is a lover of power.

A Pretence

Such a love of seeming and masks, of pretences and approximations that set aside the unutterable—and unachievable—of the great maternal passion is most clearly stated in the intimate pages of a diary in which polyglottism shatters the French language and, by means of a pre-Joycian polyphony, expresses the essential lack of authenticity on the part of an actor or diplomat lover: "Je pourrais have a fair woman of the society, this is necessary for loving absolutely Vict.[orine], même in the case nel quale troverei in lei quel alma, grande e veramente amante, che forse ho sognata."[3] As if, since no mistress can be The Mistress, no language could grasp amorous meaning: they (women and languages) are all interchangeable, but the foreign one holds the supreme fascination power—a lure nurtured by enigmas and inaccessible charm.

Are we dealing with sensuous love, or rather is such love of Woman's magic power (and Stendhal chose English to say it in a roundabout way but quite directly in fact, for the foreign language removes the awkwardness of original passion) the love of glory? "I have learned to know myself and I saw that it was at the door of Memory's temple that I must knock in order to find happiness, and with me love would be the only passion not driven away by the love of glory but would be dependent upon the latter or at best could encroach upon it only momentarily."[4]

Crystallization as Fantasy

"Would you rather have had three women or written this novel?" Stendhal wrote in the margin of The Charterhouse of Parma. The dilemma, which is not as rare or as absurd as one might be tempted to believe and ceaselessly haunts the writer and the work, found its resolution in the very existence of the novels. The core of Stendhalian love consists in this knot of writing and approached, taken, or "missed" women; it is called crystallization. Indeed, when reading Stendhal, one notices that what might reconcile one's "having had" women and "composed" a novel is crystallization love. Without becoming confused with either erotic passion or literary craft, that state of love makes its way through sex, language, and writing, and in the final analysis boils down to fantasy.

Such unachievable love (because every object is disappointing) by dint of being absolute (there exists an ideal object) goes hand in hand with the egotist's language, which is as inadequate for expressing the depths of the self as it is the absolute pretence through which the actor-diplomat attempts to reach them.[5] On the strictly linguistic plane, one might look for, in Stendhal's polyglottism (and in the multiplicity of names as well), the token of his repudiation of language—and its hypostasis as well. Concerning the lover's style, a scorn for common language has been noted, a noble exile into one that tries to be scientific or simply precise, as well as a kind of "metalinguistic" attitude on the part of Stendhal who is as distrustful of unimaginative words as he is trapped by stereotypes. The egotist's "false self" can be deciphered in such an idiolect and the impotent's dynamics doubtless is carried out in a writing that is dampened to the level of the cliché but also cherished and polished down to the passionate depth of the unutterable. Yet it is not in the "small units" of language that Stendhal's amatory genius shines forth—he who wanted to "get out of" his century in order to extoll a love that "lives in storms," a "convulsive"[6] love that might relate him to Shakespeare, Corneille, Mozart, and Madame Roland. It asserts itself especially in a transverbal product, the representation of amatory interaction: in the image of what can-and-cannot-be-done in love. In fantasy.

The technical term, appalling to a number of lovers of the crystalline palaces that shelter the Beylist soul, refers, together with Stendhalian "crystallization," to a fascination with the visible and the lure of magnifying refractions. Like the famous "tree branch stripped of its leaves by winter" that miners throw into the salt mines of Hallein, near Salzburg, and retrieve a few months later, "covered with sparkling crystallizations,"[7] the lover concocts an image of his beloved all the more beautiful and removed from reality as his mistress has been inaccessible, frustrating, hardly allowing herself to be seen. "So long as one is not at ease with what one loves, there is crystallization entailing an imaginary solution."[8]

From his childhood on, Stendhal had a passion for the theater, he fell in love with an actress, Madame Kubly, wrote on painting, filled his intimate writings with drawings. Theatrical, voyeuristic, this master of fantasy appears located on the other side of the world from poetic, metaphorical, amatory writing. Indeed, his loves, set by a gaze, do not lead him to Baudelairian synesthesias where scents, colors, and sounds blend. Staying on the surface of the beautiful rather than in its depths, frozen in tender adoration at the closed doors of feminine eroticism, no opium-addicted sampler of passionate vertigos, Stendhal described himself in terms of Georges Cabanis' melancholy person who forgets both himself and his object but never ceases to pursue it."[9] Seldom has one come so close to the elusive, "metonymic" logic of the object of desire in fantasy.

Nonetheless, Stendhalian amatory fantasy may be understood only if one adds to it, as a "lining," the metaphorical "object" of idealization that establishes Einfühlung (see One, sections titled "Einfühlung: Identification with a Metaphorical 'Object'" and "Dynamics of the Ideal"). Does not each loved woman's body convey sublimity itself: the upheld belief that the sublime, happiness, the absolute loved one might exist, even if the failures along the way cast an ironic light on such an absolute?

The arena of Stendhalian loves thus unfold the lovers' psychic and sensitive space, beginning with exchanged glances and proceeding with lightly touched hands and bare shoulders—one will recall the warm or cool evenings in Madame de Rênal's garden and the strategy of distances that Julien suppresses or imposes on his mistress . . . But the ritual of power or submission is played out in the realm of the invisible, the subjugation germane to amatory politics is effected in a silent rapture between the two amatory spaces. Eventually, "crystallization" turns out to be less theatrical than transported toward the unnamable, full of implications, bent in the direction of the unutterable ideal, sustained by the momentum of the idealizing metaphor and thus opening out toward the allusive depths of metaphorical meaning . . . But is that motion anything but the opening up of the visible toward psychology as mystery?

In Stendhal's nevertheless realistic universe, everything indeed depends on interpretation—perhaps more than elsewhere, more than the naive reader might believe: it depends on the identifying projection required of the reader. Whether more risqué or more modest, interpretation of amatory meaning always remains undecidable. But it is indeed the undecidable that determines the sequence of Stendhalian events rather than such-and-such a truth of the partners' acts or feelings. Nevertheless, so that there might be love, there must be someone for whom the interpretation of that undecidable universe is always ideal. Someone for whom a small detail, in spite of doubts, eventually means only the lovers' possible merging. That part is usually played by the feminine lover. Madame de Rênal ends up no longer mistrusting Julien's fondling; instead, she right away endows it with a necessarily courtly, ideal, coded metaphorical meaning . . . The mistress is in possession of the zenith of amatory meaning, within the space of which the hero can act out his yet essential pretence . . . She believes in it, he plays.

The Obstacle and the Gaze

Located in the space of the metaphor's interpretation, fantasy love does not seek out satisfaction but feeds on obstacles that are challenged by the eyes.

Indeed, deprived of satisfaction, the lover soaks up the loved one through his gaze, which becomes the strongest conveyer of amatory affect, and at the same time its most powerful distancer. Desire, translating the intensity of subjective feeling into the visible, takes refuge in both an esthetic and amorous worship of beauty (Stendhal wrote On Love in 1820 [published in 1822] after his History of Painting in 1817). Stendhal might have borrowed from Cabanis the importance given to the image as accurate sign of emotion.[10] Have lovers not, however, from time immemorial, given sight the role of first messenger of amorous thrill? Stendhal, that lover of mirrors ("A novel: it is a mirror one takes along on the road"—The Red and the Black), knew what the archeologists of the "mirror stage" have disclosed. The visible other is beautiful because we project on him all the affection that we feel for him, while he leaves us tragically alone, in a state of "fiasco," on the other side, barely able to crystallize in him (or in her) the portion of our autoerotic convulsion we can no longer taste or touch; for, henceforth, the sight of an other exists for us. Delightfully confusing subject and object, which also remain indistinct,[11] the visual worship of the loved woman is a reverie that the language of music might best express,[12] which any clarification (necessary, doubtless, because it is defensive) could obviously only destroy.

The visual absorption of the loved object nevertheless amounts to its destruction, as it were, its total submission to the lover's gaze. In love, there is no seen beauty, properly speaking: the sight remains, the glance of the seer: "From the moment when you begin to interest yourself in a woman, you no longer see her such as she really is but such as it suits you . . . only through the eyes of this young man who is beginning to love."[13] "Indeed, love, for me, has always been the greatest of concerns, or rather the only one."[14] And this is immediately followed by a definition of love completely under the ascendency of the gaze, the one looked at looking in turn, the jealous reflection of indiscernible images, kaleidoscope of jealousy: "I have never been afraid of anything, except seeing the woman I love give a rival an intimate gaze."[15]

In the elaboration, a banal one for all that, of scopic erotization, Stendhal has the privilege of emphasizing its frustration aspect—the agent of idealization. In short, it would force the postponement of the erotic act and introduce amatory time, which would thus be none other than the fortitude of idealization.

The obstacle that calls forth "wild-eyed doubt" would be its essential mainspring, and it will be noted that all of Stendhal's mistresses make themselves desirable by dint of holding out. That is particularly the case with Metilde Visconti, the former wife of General Dembovski, whom Stendhal met in Milan in 1818; the passion he feels for her provided the basic framework for On Love. In this "affair," through Metilde's sole cold and iron will, a "prosaic soul" if there ever was one, Stendhal was very soon limited to two visits per month. Later, if we can believe the writer's correspondence, he considered asking her to let him come "four times a month for half an hour," or even "a quarter of an hour one of those evenings." An earlier relationship, a rather stormy one, with Angela Pietragrua, was no more successful. The impetuous Italian thought of a scornful device that tells a great deal about her knowledge of Stendhal's desire: she reduced the writer to his voyeur's role by letting him watch through the keyhole her love-making with others . . . But one could also mention Stendhal's love for Countess Pierre Daru, or later for Countess Curial; his love for Giulietta Riniera, whom he asked to marry him in 1830 and who married someone else in 1833; or finally for the mysterious Earline, shortly before his death—they all include that share of sought-after or created obstacles that turn such affairs into "battles" or "wars," which are the more "crystallizing" as the unsatisfied affect wanders and is appeased only in the imaginary realm. The previously unpublished chapter from On Love, "On Fiascos" (a supplement to Michel Lévy's edition in 1853), justifies the lover's sexual failure in the presence of a person "who, to him, is like the Divinity and arouses in him both extreme love and extreme respect";[16] for "if the soul is engaged in being ashamed and overcoming that shame, it cannot set about having pleasure." If love is a fantasy, it takes in affect as its unrepresentable hell, at the risk of obliterating it . . .

Then one understands why physical love might be followed by disgust and be tinged with murderous violence. "Every love comes to an end, no matter how violent it might have been, and the most violent ends more promptly than others. After love comes disgust; there is nothing more natural; then people avoid each other for a while."[17] And referring to Angela Pietragrua: "That did not remove a gnawing gloom. I went home furious, this means that I would have been pleased to tear apart bloody remnants of flesh if I had been a lion . . . because I would have been solaced by performing an act of power."[18] The fiasco's reverse, this vengeance love is above all the upbeat moment of a permanent melancholia: "Had she [Angela] not loved me, I would have experienced dreadful moments. . . . She loves me, and tedium takes hold of me."[19]

Stendhal in love confesses that his life could be summed up in a list of feminine names whose initials he would inscribe on the dust, as Zadig had done: "Virginie (Kubly), Angela (Pietragrua), Adèle (Rebuffel), Mélanie (Guilbert), Mina (de Griesheim), Alexandrine (Petit), Angéline, whom I never loved (Bereyter), Métilde (Demboski), Clémentine (Giulia), and finally, for a month at the most, Madame Azur, whose given name I have forgotten, and unwisely, yesterday, Amalie (Bettini)."[20] "Most of these charming persons," Stendhal added, "did not reward me with their favors; but literally they have occupied my entire life. And they have been replaced by my books."

The subtle tasting of defeat, a delight in failure more than victory, whose military flavor captivates but does not satiate: "The remarkable and rather unfortunate aspect of this, I said to myself this morning, is that my victories (as I used to call them, my mind full of military matters) have given me a pleasure that was less than half as great as the deep misfortune caused by my defeats."[21]

Besides, the fiasco is simply the price of loving bedazzlement: the ambitious lover may well set forth a whole military strategy, he does not eliminate the eternal Werther who might well confess to being impotent but is also bashful. "But I went too far; instead of being gallant I would become passionate with the women I loved, almost indifferent and above all without conceit with the others; hence the lack of success, the fiascos. Perhaps no man at the Emperor's court has had fewer women than I have—and people believed I was the lover of the Prime Minister's wife."[22]

Unsuccessful lover, that is, aesthete; less an active creator than a delighted contemplator of the fine arts as he was of women. The love of women as one of the fine arts, which one enjoys without necessarily practicing them. "My habitual state of life has been that of an unsuccessful lover, loving music and painting, that is, enjoying the products of those arts rather than practicing them awkwardly."[23]

The Master Woman

The masochistic delights that the Salzburg tree limb was called upon for metaphorization make the following question incongruous: who is the true object of Stendhalian love? For it is quite obvious here that truth can only reside in the imaginary realm. For if a stately and yet incestuously desired mother looms behind his quest for images that are as voluptuous as they are postponed ("I wanted to cover my mother with kisses, I wished there were no clothes . . .";[24] Stendhal noted that while loving Alberte de Rubempré, for instance, "to the point of frenzy," his "manner of hunting for happiness had not changed at all"), we have no choice but to note that such a feminine figure is immediately and at the same time that of a masterful woman and of a dead woman.

One must understand the Stendhalian mistress in the strongest sense of the term. Master is what she is, and the phallus has rarely been so regally embodied in the female lover. Courtly erotics already knew that the Lady was a moon reflecting the solar power of her father or husband, and the Surrealists' "wild love" came close to acknowledging its homosexual backdrop.[25] And yet the Stendhalian lover loves the woman who transmits no male power. He is in love with an authority that is purely feminine; one might say that for Stendhal it has a feminine essence, in a world of ghostly men (decadent bourgeois, pallid aristocrats, or else quarrelsome, bloodthirsty bandits, more or less archaic or panic-stricken . . .). What attracts him is woman's virility pushed to the extent that it scares him. The evidence for that? In addition to the stately Italian women, there are these observations: "A mistress who has been desired three years is truly a mistress in the full strength of the term: one approaches her only with trepidation, and I would tell all Don Juans that the man who trembles is never bored."[26] "At most, the sanguine man may only encounter a kind of moral fiasco: when he receives an invitation from Messaline, and at the moment of entering her bed, he begins to think of the fearsome judge before whom he is to appear."[27] On the other hand, do not proud women, the most desirable ones, yield only if they can identify with the men their lovers succeed in conquering? "Haughty-natured women yield with pleasure to men they see behaving intolerantly with other men."[28] Clorinde or Bradamante, Mathilde, Gina, Clelia herself, are the true powers of the "affair" in which the voluptuous novelist of psychology takes pleasure in acting out the part, slighted by conceited people, of a lover apparently defeated, conquered, suffering, in one word, passionate in the manner of Werther. He thus believes he can escape the conceit Stendhal calls French and despises—the conceit of a Don Juan always claiming to play the enviable part of the conqueror . . .

The stance of the Stendhalian lover—who adores a virgin, forbidden mother—is indeed more infantile, more adolescent. The atheist who marvels at Corregio's Madonna blessed by Christ (Rome, Naples, Florence) writes: "With tender souls, fear of God's judgments is expressed through love for the Madonna; they cherish the unfortunate mother who experienced so much pain and was consoled by such surprising events—her son's resurrection, the discovery that he is God, etc."[29] Fascinated as much by the son's divinity as by unfathomable motherly love, Stendhal bows before the evidence that "human weakness has a need to love, and which divinity ever was more worthy of love" than the Virgin Mother who is, consequently and miraculously, "more God than God himself."[30]

Werther as Author

Is not the bedazzled child, however, a king, or even God, including—or especially—when he has "little success"? "With all those [the women of the lover's list] I have always been a child; thus I have had very little success."[31]

His amorous journey seems to have been impelled not by the dark captation/castration fear that makes Don Juan rush from one object to another, but by a fully oral and visual thirst dominated by the world of a mother who refuses to give herself to him. Does the mistress refuse? Well, I shall be the master of the scene I crave: I shall have her in my eyes, my mouth, my tongue. Such amatory fantasy in fact turns his Werther into an author of heroines. The writer then dons the mask of ambitious men (Julien Sorel or Fabrice del Dongo) in order to be the passion of Madame de Rênal or Sanseverina. In order to be a feminine passion, failing to have it—to dominate it; ". . . in love, to possess is nothing, what counts is jouissance."[32] That everlasting precocious adolescent is neither Madame de Rênal nor Sanseverina, as Flaubert might have claimed to be Madame Bovary. Sparkling, spirited, "champagnized," according to Stefan Zweig, Stendhal is the most French of psychologists, perhaps because he locates himself fully in the intervening space between the passion for a woman and that of a woman . . . He identifies neither with Her nor with Him: his amatory psychology harnesses the pulverization of identities in the field of scopic passion nonetheless dominated by feminine power. In that in-between space where the fantasy of adolescent love is elaborated, love is under the magical, dominating, and metamorphosing power of the lovers' gaze. Clelia vows not to see Fabrice, whereas in a strange text the privilege of the gaze controls—is it ironically?—the intensity of amorous feeling.[33]

In such a world of gazes, vision-love translates the projection of the lover to his mistress' place. Under the ambitious man's mask the writer appropriates the passion that would be the essential secret of the superwoman, and that absorption turns him into more-than-a-man, a superman. With Stendhal, real women, those that are to be loved, are like Corneille's characters, they are the masters par excellence, imaginary phalluses. She frustrates me, hence I see her from a lover's point of view; I see her for my sake, hence I am the eye that writes. Writing—more than Stendhal's famous self-confidence that irritated some of his contemporaries[34]—is the ultimate proof of overcoming feminine phallic power. A writing of psychological twists and turns for the sake of appearances. Amatory psychology of a specular uncertainty that becomes stable only when it is assured of reproducing—aping—the impressing image produced for the other.

One has often been ironical about the "unfortunate girls" who were "missed," according to Stendhal's own rather smug acknowledgment. His conceit doubtless required that he linger over this more than Wertherian weakness in order to assert, as a lining and a counterweight, his pen's magical power. What matters in love is not so much the sexual performance than the fantasy of almightiness, which women, adorned for the benefit of sight, are in good position to represent; and writing, in its own way—herein a rival of feminine masquerade—can stage it even more masterfully—through women, of course, but in sovereign fashion. Superior writing: ironical and solitary. Would Werther's stance undergoing a fiasco be an ultimate wile to allow the Don Juan of writing to assert himself?

A Putting to Death of the Feminine

There is, however, the dead woman. Henri Beyle lost his mother, with whom he confessed to be in love, when he was seven, in 1790. In On Love he evokes the story of Madonna Pia, killed out of jealousy and "so beautiful even in the arms of death." There is Beatrice Cenci, minutely described; the interest for the discovered Italian coffins; the death of Clorinde that is attractive; the deaths of Madame de Rênal and of Clelia; "I am desperately sorry on account of Métilde; she is dying; I preferred her dead rather than unfaithful."[35] Those few examples suffice to remind one that the dead woman is inscribed in the Stendhalian imagination as the vanishing point of amatory desires. A resurfacing of the precociously lost mother? A Baudelairian celebration of deathly frigidity: a worship of the insensitive, inpenetrable, hence definitely phallic woman? With those dead women, there may be above all a specifically Stendhalian manner of idealizing the loved one as she loses her characteristic of being an other—an other sex—and unites with the lover's desired power. The object-sight and the subject-glance, intertwined, indistinct, become fused in a placid worship of the object of desire as if it were a beyond: objectivized and infinitized by death. With Stendhal the dead woman is not a corpse, any more than Beatrice was with Dante. The dead woman is the untouchable absolute that serves as halo for the forbidden mother. The dead woman may also be, moreover, jouissance as nostalgia: at hand but lost forever, impossible.

There, in that precise spot, woman (the other, the object) has been abolished, yet to be born again in that maximal crystallization constituted by postmortuary love where the writer projects the almightiness of his desire to possess her, to possess himself beyond her. His women, masterful and dead, remind us that phallic idealization is built upon the pedestal of a putting-to-death of the feminine body.

But exactly who has been put to death in this merging of the gaze with the seen object? The loved one? or the "feminine" of the lover: his unnamable passion, his Wertherian, narcissian weakness, obediently coiled up around the invisible maternal continent, previous to the mirror stage?

To pride himself in exuding cruelty, childish cruelty at any rate, is not unknown to Henry Brulard: "My earliest memory is having bitten in the cheek or forehead Madame Pison du Galland, my cousin, wife of the witty deputy at the Constituant Assembly. . ."[36] And this summing up of the small boy's love for his mother, a reader of Dante, who "was portly, absolutely pure," "was quite pretty," "perfectly noble and serene in her features": "As for me, I was as criminal as can be, I loved her charms furiously."[37]

But above all one will note certain resemblances between this final appropriation of the dead woman by a writing that kills her but survives her and the logic of the pseudonym.

Masquerade

Through his fondness for other names Stendhal flees the burden of Grenoble and Beyle and masks himself under a series of false names worthy of the Thousand Nights and a Night. Those masks are quite obviously as many aggressions for their addressee ("you will not recognize me," such is their challenge) as they are defenses of a secret intimacy ("the core shall remain unnamable") eventually wilting into a multifarious, puzzling identity. "Would one believe me? I would wear a mask with pleasure; I would change names with delight. The Thousand Nights and a Night, which I adore, occupy more than a fourth of my mind. I often think of Angelique's ring: my supreme pleasure would be to turn into a lanky, blond German and in such a guise go for a walk in Paris."[38] Social protocol views multiplication of names as a series of repudiations, all of them inauthentic. Nonetheless, the creation of novelistic characters becomes the most faithful expression of that masquerade, in fact unveiling the aim of pseudonymity: to impose the infinite power of a writing that kills the author and, out of that sacrifice, to generate masks henceforth caught in the dialectics of an unending negation and reformulation.[39]

Putting to death the Name of the father, the Stendhalian pseudonym is above all the sacrifice of French resonance (Stendhal is the name of a small German town).[40] The French name died so that the author might be reborn, and, through the wonders of the French language, he would then mould amorous heroes and heroines who challenge hazards and death. Like fantasy, the pseudonym is a mastery that hides a secret. What allows itself to be seen (such as phantasmatic crystallization but also the name invented by its own author and called a pseudonym) lets one keep for himself, in the impersonal darkness of drive, the unnamable imprint of an obscure passion. A mother thus kept sheltered alive: living dead? Is it not this archaic, dead loved one that the writer resuscitates when his statuesque, self-controlled heroines—Sanseverina, Clelia, Mathilde de la Mole—allow their sublime weaknesses to show beneath the helmets of their masterful womanhood? The madness made up of Stendhalian women's unreasonable courage is perhaps the moment when the dead mother comes to life as death drive—as passion. Even the provincial, modest gentleness of Madame de Rênal, which might be unconsciousness of her "sweet sensuality," bursts out in the boldness of her passion for Julien . . . Like the miraculous awakening of a sleeping person, like the resurrection of a living-dead one, hostage to dismal provincial life . . . But eventually to lead her toward death . . .

Between Two Women

The splitting of Stendhalian love appears in another light in this constant of his erotic world—the lover positioned between two women. Julien Sorel between Madame de Rênal and Mathilde; Lucien Leuwen between Madame de Chasteller or Madame d'Hocquincourt or Madame Grandet; Fabrice del Dongo between Sanseverina and Clelia, between Sanseverina and Marietta, between Sanseverina and Fausta, and, to begin with, between Sanseverina and his own mother . . .

These feminine "duets" are not merely two variants of the loved woman: the passionate and the cerebral one, the archaic and the modern, the motherly and the virile, the noble and the vulgar one, etc., which would allow Stendhal to weave his chart of love, measuring out his preferences for courage, or violence, or risk, or restraint . . . They are used not only to stage jealousy, whose role is doubtless dominating (along with frustration, absence, refusal, fear) in the dynamics of "crystallization." With Stendhal, being "between-two-women" also suggests a safeguarding strategy. In order not to be swallowed up by one of them, the egotist grants himself at least two. An eternal stratagem of masculine sexuality obsessed with castration, maintaining two poles of crystallization[41] doubtless satisfies Stendhal the voyeur. The single passion, Stendhal's narrative seems to say, is deadly and leads to confinement and unrepresentable confusion. Women may become swallowed up by it, like Madame de Rênal, the abbess de Castro, Clelia. But the egotist, impelled by the ambition that, on occasion, turns out to be love, owes it to himself to remain on the surface of the visible. As if a woman were visible only when compared with another. Furthermore, he feels authenticated, he recovers his identity when he sees himself in an other woman's glance—and this at the most dangerous moment, when he is clutched by her double, a rival or an accomplice. It does not seem as though it were a Don Juan-like desire to conquer that drives the Stendhalian hero to play off his eroticism with two women partners. Rather, it is the fear of letting himself be annihilated by a passion that might jeopardize his ambition. The duets of mistresses were meant to be the fetishized steps on the ascent that turns a possible passion into a game: the power game.

With a similar purpose, the classification, the mathematical ordering of those ladies, or even military considerations in the machinations of amatory campaigns are nothing more than stratagems aiming to thwart the gaze's bedazzlement, the loss of sight (and of self) in amorous rapture. "To view them in the most philosophical way possible and thus attempt to strip them of the halo that makes my eyes go wild, bedazzles me, and deprives me of the ability to see things clearly, I shall order these ladies (mathematical terminology) according to their various qualities. I shall thus say, to begin with their customary passion, conceit, that two of them were countesses and one a baroness . . ." "I seek to destroy the charm, the dazzling [the use of the visual term will be noted: "dazzling," blinding by light] of events, by thus considering them in military fashion."[42]

Nevertheless, if being between-two-women allows for a calculated flushing out of jouissance, the hero's tragic fate—and jail or murder are its specifically Stendhalian symbols—unveils the death threat's eventually insuperable permanency. Love had, from the very beginning, destined the lovers to die; love is death.

Mortal Man and the Female Being

Finally, the feminine duets of the Stendhalian Eros make man appear as an exchange object among women. As if, in Stendhal's eyes, the social code were based on conniving or competing feminine homosexuality, which, from behind the scenes, would govern the careers of ambitious men. Further still, and even if the love affair in that world is often triggered by the masculine hero, like the vain Julien, feminine passion and daring, challenging morality, eventually settle the rules of the game. Stendhalian love is a matter for women: one might recall that Madame de Rênal's first glance at Julien caused him to be mistaken for a girl. The Stendhalian lover is secretly a lesbian.

Stendhal as woman, Stendhal whose body is that of a woman—he admits it in one of those bouts of excessive introspection, apparently inspired by Cabanis, but for which he is doubtless the sole, supreme source, visual as well as sensory. "My skin is much too fine, it is a woman's skin (later I always got blisters after holding my sword for an hour); I scrape my fingers, which are quite nice, for no reason whatsoever; in short, the surface of my body is feminine. Hence, perhaps, an insuperable horror for what seems dirty, or damp, or blackish."[43]

Chastity prevails here as it does over everything else and Stendhal's lesbianism is never acknowledged—while Auguste Rodin for instance almost explicitly confesses his own and appears as a cognizant voyeur and proud to be so when he displays embraces between women in his drawings. Stendhal's impotent man, on the other hand, who launches out into love only out of duty and ambition—doubtless to ward off in advance a possible fiasco—tells us nothing about the couples' love-making. Granted this is characteristic of the time. But one must note how emphatically Stendhal shows that Madame de Chasteller's chastity kindles Lucien Leuwen's idealizing fire more than does Madame d'Hocquincourt's licentiousness; or his condemning Madame de Lavenelle's sexy remarks that screech, to the egotist's ears, "like cork being cut by a knife."[44] For the egotist, loving the "physical man" is out of the question, nor should one say it in French: Italian would be much more suitable.[45] It will be noted that when the nights of Julien with Madame de Rênal are described, the only detail of their love-making is Monsieur de Rênal's snoring. Furthermore, when Julien goes up to Mathilde's room we are given minute descriptions of his handling the ladder and the risks incurred, but a single, terse sentence informs us of the lover's sexual relationship: "Today, one of those gentlemen could barely speak with her a few moments before she had a question to ask Julien, and it was an excuse for keeping him close to her. She became pregnant and told it joyfully to Julien." As a compensation, one will note that the correspondence with General de Vervaques's wife is filled with endless descriptions that, obviously, keep us as much within the lying conceits that are at the opposite extreme of passion as within the area between-two-women . . .

Stendhal the Feminist

Was Stendhal a feminist? In sections of On Love he advocates Destutt de Tracy's program of reforms commending free choice in marriage, possibility for divorce, and education for women. He demanded, in brotherly, generous fashion, an equitable education for women, and he proclaimed that "all the geniuses that are born women are lost to the public weal; whenever chance gives them the means to prove themselves, see how they attain the most difficult skills." Stendhal doubtless is one of those liberal minds still very rare in his time. Such a "feminism," however, is sustained, as we have seen, by an idealization of woman, who is set up as a phantasmatic ideal and becomes, like a compendium of death drive, a magnet of identification for the writer. Since the writer is the one who names the unnamable, woman is his imaginary analogue as she courageously sails between body and mind, passion and society. More distinctly yet in the direction of bringing together Stendhalian femininity and writing, his women lovers write: one thinks of Madame de Rênal's disastrous letter, suggested, it is true, by her confessor, but which still remains the true generator of the novel's conclusion and hastens Julien's downfall; one also remembers the writing invented by Gina to correspond with Julien . . .

Stendhal, an atheist, does not even believe in love since he lodges it in a hollow of his vain rakes' ambition. But he believes in Woman with a capital W: "The influence of women is much too great in France, the influence of Woman much too restricted."[46] His heroines are not partners whose otherness might allow the heroes to come into their own. They have the strength of destiny, the power of ancient divinities. Through their secular side, they secure or destroy the social power of their lovers, followers of Don Juan, while through their nocturnal side they bring about the madness and death of their Werthers.

Stendhal's Belief

It was doubtless necessary to have Simone de Beauvoir's enthusiasm and her will to sanction women's longing for power to assert that Stendhal "loves women in their truth" and "does not believe in feminine mystery" since "no essence defines women once and for all."[47] Such an interpretation of Stendhal does not linger over Stendhalian sexuality—nor over that of the novels or the intimate writings. It keeps only the first level of the image Stendhal offers—courageous heroines unconsciously rebelling against their environment. And forgets to mention that the favorites of Stendhal, a liberal, atheistic Frenchman, were all archaic women: Catholics, aristocrats enamored with medieval values, irrational Italians. Beyond the superficial image of emancipation, Stendhal seeks in women that erotic difference that the second generation of feminists, so close to the suffragettes, has not claimed at all. Motherly, driven by passion, deadly nonrepresentable, and yet at work in the major actions of the novelistic subject—of the Subject?—the otherness that can indeed be metaphorized by calling it feminine is fetishized by Stendhal and turned into a secular idol. What is there left to love in a world where fathers are derisory? There is Woman, and yet with irony, and even if the "affair" is doomed before it started, a fiasco . . . All it takes to make up The Catechism of a Rake (1803) . . .

Because he confined idolatry within the love of the feminine as the opposite extreme of religion, Stendhal unveiled the secret working of belief. Moreover, the dualism of his erotics, anchored as it is in the quest of phallic power embodied in a Woman and exercised by warlike man right up to the bedroom, makes the Stendhalian world more complex than the feminist vulgate suggests. "There is not one of us who would not prefer, for a lifetime alliance, a servant over a blue stocking."[48] The aphorism is not only an echo out of Molière, it is also an impugnment of the leveling effects of democracy. Did this liberal enamored of pious Italians harbor a muffled Catholic passion in the manner of Chateaubriand? The second volume of On Love suggests, at any rate, that love contradicts democratic principles, and emancipation needs to safeguard mystery. Does not Clelia betray her liberal father for the sake of Fabrice's love? But Stendhal, a forerunner of Tocqueville,[49] fascinated by and in a state of fiasco in the presence of masterful women, does more than point to the contradictions between liberal thinking and the demands of feminine autonomy. He expresses, in more simple and intimate fashion, the erotics of a man who seeks in love a value that might shelter him from anguish. The pseudonymous Stendhal remains ambiguous, too, facing feminine emancipation: whether grandiose or ridiculous, feminine emancipation is like the final, sublime jewel in the ambitious man's discourse. But it is swept away by passionate explosion (Sanseverina) or by gentle, intelligent restraint (Madame de Chasteller). In the world of crime constituted by Stendhalian love,[50] the Ancien Regime heroines and the soothing or violent mothers have the upper hand over the modern young women, educated and emancipated. If there is a Stendhalian feminism, it consists precisely in the worship that suggests that feminism is perhaps our last religion, that of the woman with authority. The primordial mother, absolute mistress, is not dead: she urges us on to love, to death . . . Let us accept our fiascos with a sense of humor, with love . . .

XI

Bataille and the Sun,
or the Guilty Text

"NEITHER SUN NOR DEATH can be stared at without blinking," La Rochefoucault wrote, and Georges Bataille evokes that statement when the character in My Mother who assumes the part of a narrator relates his mother's sexual excesses. Henceforth an accomplice of a maternal desire as intense as it is degrading, now the plaything, now the victim of the mother's erotic stagings, whom nothing can satisfy except death, he nevertheless gives us this paradoxical assertion of his crimes: "Death, in my eyes, was no less divine than the sun, and my mother, through her crimes, was closer to God than anything I had seen through the window of the church."[1]

A Fading Out of Meaning: The Obscene

One might be inclined to attribute Bataille's erotic experience to a Catholicism that was taken on to the limit of its sin-laden logic and would lead to its internal reversal. This doubtless important aspect of writing, with Bataille, cannot overshadow the universal logic it contains. When he writes, "Perfect darkness alone is similar to light," he confronts us with the unfolding of an antithetical metaphor that fuses two opposing semantic fields (darkness, light) and, through the tension caused by such nonsynthetic combination, produces a nonsense effect, a state of shock. Far from being nothingness, however, the paradoxical moment of the antithetical metaphor that is here brought to the fore is the occasion of its maximal affect. As if the erotic blazing of subject and meaning, in the same way as the blinding sun or unbearable death ("Neither sun nor death can be stared at without blinking"), discovered, as maximal encoding, the junction of the metaphorical process with the antithetical. Metaphor, we are told simplistically, makes things visible. But can the unbearable, death and the sun, or incest, be made visible? How might one make visible that which is not visible because no code, convention, contract, or identity holds it up? Besides, is it really a matter of making visible the unrepresentable, which here seems to be the unleashed passion of a mother who knows no taboo? Figurative language, literature, then owe it to themselves to measure up to that invisible and also to its drive intensity. They must produce a fading out of meaning at the same time as a conveyance of meaning. . . toward what? Toward a point where the turmoil of passion persists, a passion that took hold of the loving subject before the naked, sublime, or disgusting body of the loved one.

As long as we speak, we shall never get rid of repression. But if, since Freud, a modicum of censorship has been lifted where desire, pleasure, and love are concerned, the major rhetorical question still remains: what language is to be ascribed to this lifting of censorship? Naming the sexual act as such, in its organic reality, says nothing of the amatory relationship as a questioning, unsettling process for its subjects. The narrative must then assume a double function. First, it becomes obscene; it follows fantasy as far as possible down to its innermost perverse recesses. In My Mother Pierre first becomes Rea's lover, a friend of his mother who offered her to him herself. Later he falls in love with the sadomasochistic couple constituted by Hansi and Loulou, also friends of his mother, who, for the young man, stand for the entire array of what he fantasizes as an almighty, devastating, both aggressive and victimized feminine sexuality, but eventually autarkical like an ancient God, for it has no object. As the mother says,

I do not know if I really love women. I believe I have never loved anywhere except in the woods. I did not like the woods, but I loved immoderately. I have never loved anyone but you, but what I love in you, make no mistake about it, is not you. I believe I love only love, and even in love only the anguish of loving, I have never felt it except in the woods or on the day when death . . . .[3]

The Immoderate Narrative

Nonetheless, the simple designation, the univocal naming of perverse relationships, their "scientific" description, do not measure up to the "excessiveness" specific to the anguish of loving. Precisely in order to respond to such excessiveness, the narrative becomes incoherent: anticipations, insertion of letters, of musing thoughts foreign to the brutality of the erotic scene, etc. The technique recalls that of the picaresque novel, or Sade's fiction, and here it is practiced within the space of a short narrative. It is thus condensed, not justified, not amenable to verisimilitude. Hence it evokes the twilight state of a consciousness perturbed by desire. Moreover, as no enigma, sexual as it might be, remains within this generalized obscenity, "metaphor" as poetic figure is out of place along with its retinue of idealizations and mysteries. Nevertheless, the strained motion of condensation is taken up again in order to nourish the field of "paradoxical meditation." The courtly or romantic metaphor gives way before that paradoxical meditation. A meditation on the sublime, the essential magnet of love: meditation on God. It must nevertheless and indeed be paradoxical, for when the sublime is revealed through its obscene, aggressive, destructive, deadly, or simply painful and abject medium—it becomes degraded, breath-stopping, laughable. "What is there to laugh about in this world outside of God?" "Most often it seems to me that I adore my mother. Could I have ceased to adore her? Yes: God is what I adore. And yet I do not believe in God. Am I then crazy? What I only know: if I laughed in the midst of tortures, fallacious as this might be, I would answer the question I asked while looking at my mother, the one my mother asked while looking at me. What is there to laugh about, in this world, outside of God?"[4]

The contemporary amatory narrative thus tries to convey at the same time the idealization and the state of shock germane to amatory feeling: the sublime is this neither-subject-nor-object entity that I have called "abjection." Erotic fantasy merges with philosophical meditation in order to reach the focus where the sublime and the abject, making up the pedestal of love, come together in the "flash."[5] The contemporary narrative is basically not a technical performance as the new novel wanted us to believe through its persnickety fragmentation. The contemporary narrative (from Joyce to Bataille) has a posttheological aim: to communicate the amorous flash. The one in which the "I" reaches the paranoid dimensions of the sublime divinity while remaining close to abject collapse, disgust with the self. Or, quite simply, to its moderate version known as solitude.

To guide us through that experience the narrative becomes literal, through the unveiling of sexual fantasy. Disjointed, unstructured, mere free association, a drifting, a meshing of narrative events. Furthermore, the account becomes meditative, thus taking up theological or philosophical thought to derive support from it or dismantle it. The result of such operations, however, is the transference and condensation of a magnified, extensive epiphora. An opening out toward the nonmeaning of passion or the affect without sign. The height of representation and extreme realism thus end up, when they are caught in the logic of contradictory meaning conveyance (sexual-scientific-philosophical etc., sublime-degrading, etc.), evoking the invisible. Now the invisible that was God is, precisely, within the dramatic experience of the thinking animal, the obscene—the offstage, the unrepresentable yet making its presence felt in the flaws of the texture (language, discourse, or narrative) that represents.

If the metaphor ever was heliotropic, enamored with supreme solar meaning, it vanishes from the contemporary narrative. Thus comes to an end the movement that refined and extinguished meaning, which impelled the extended metaphor in automatic writing, where it plays the part of a shooting star rather than a sunflower (heliotrope).[6] The field of the epiphora, or transference and condensation of meaning, ends up by opening out the surface of signs toward the unrepresentable that subtends them and, if it is the unutterable share of the amorous current, needs discursive and narrative strategy in order to manifest itself in intaglio. Written eroticism is a function of verbal tension, an "in-between-the-signs."

Uttering the Sexual Thing

So what happens to the metaphor? It changes into the variant of condensation called the narrative ellipsis. It is also reduced to multiple clues scattered along the narrative suggesting that "I," a perverse, sensual lover, sees the thing (no longer Romeo's sun, nor God—Res significata, but quite crudely the mother's sex) straightforwardly and without embarrassment but cannot utter it wholly. The real cannot be uttered as such. Such a restraint is neither due to the powerlessness of melancholia nor to the frigid repression of censorship. On the contrary, when desire fully feeds amatory idealization, its flow causes the speaking being to fly off the handle, and during the subsequent language ordeal the sign of the unspoken becomes the most intense equivalent of erotic flaring-up. Since the metaphor is the sign of unbeing, it reaches its peak and its completion in a deferment of meaning, at the very moment the narrative clarifies certain erotic stages of that unbeing.

Such a condensation, in an unconstrained narrative, becomes the reverse of wordiness: it is the blank space between the lines. Thematically, it is an admission of weakness (Bataille calls himself "guilty") as an inseparable lining of jouissance. The apogee of literature affects us by displaying its impossible within itself. Sole witness, not of the sexual indulgence so much exploited in commercial art, but of what Bataille calls a "sovereignty." A love that shelters and infinitizes the exquisite and painful weakness of passion. I mentioned this before: the narrator in My Mother speaks La Rochefoucault's sentence while evoking his mother's sexual excesses. Sun, sex, incest can indeed not be stared at unblinkingly; they must be seen obliquely, through an erotic, meditative narrative—an amorous one. A theological and philosophical tale, with aspects borrowed from Thomas Aquinas but also from the libertines, such a narrative meditation indefinitely extends the amatory experience's connotations, thus counterbalancing the deferment of that connotation through the advent of obscene meaning. In short, the metaphor has given up its journey toward the invisible or the dazzling. Henceforth it unfolds as a jubilatory, guilty cavalcade between meditation and obscenity, fullness of meaning and its hollowing out. . . Metaphysics has been resolved into conveyance, transference, a perpetual motion of senses and meaning. . .

Thanatos

The analyst might wonder how to describe such an amorous subject of whom the narrator, in My Mother, gives us an exquisite example. Perverse? Paranoid (". . . I had the feeling of being like God")? Obstinate and obsessed believer in an almighty feminine libido that would be the equivalent of a maternal phallus? The father's Oedipal enemy, henceforth condemned to imagine only homosexual partners for his mother and to feminize himself, passive and nearly victimized. Such labels have the disadvantage of suggesting another love that, for its part, would be free from perversion; they also hide a key question of amatory dynamics. If it is true that in amorous alchemy sexual drive undergoes the idealization tied to the narcissism it is known to harbor, what happens, in love, to the reverse of Eros—Thanatos? In short, what is involved in the obscene narrative is an encoding of death drive, which Freud told us preceded both object and love. That narrative leads Thanatos between the signs, by means of the thematic of passion and death on the one hand, the clash between heterogeneous discourses and semantic fields on the other. As the other, the mother, is impelled by a libido that is less Eros than Death, she provides us with the focal point of that subjective and discursive dynamic. Picasso's women, and those of De Kooning, like Bataille's My Mother represent the wild wager to omit nothing concerning that death-mother, to catch hold of her, frontally or obliquely, but to catch her just the same within the grid of their work. Distorting her to the point of extravagant ugliness and excitement. "Oh grit your teeth, my son. You resemble your prick, a prick streaming with rage that clutches my desire like a wrist."[7] In short, we are dealing with a mother who knows no taboo, she is preoedipal, the archaic holder of my possible identity. Potentially psychotizing. In that sense the obscene narrative is a heroic attempt to settle scores with that particular mother; consequently, it is the most extensive sublimation of psychosis. Perversion, in short, is not simply the necessary lot of the neotenic person; it is the first defensive territory that the subject sets up against Death, insofar as it seems to him to have its source in the very source of life—in the mother. "Love is as strong as death," is what we read in the Song of Songs, and recent commentators believe this sublime love song to stem from funereal orgies.[8] Those explorers of the psyche, whom we call writers, travel to the end of the night where our loves fear to tread. We remain merely perturbed by the style's intensity, for the unconscious has its obligations. . . A style—witness to the loss of meaning, watchman of death.

XII

Extraterrestrials Suffering
for Want of Love

The Crisis

THE ANALYST IS by definition tuned in to the crisis: the analytical contract issues from an inevitable discontent. The existence of psychoanalysis thus reveals the permanency, the inescapable nature of crisis. The speaking being is a wounded being, his speech wells up out of an aching for love, and the "death drive" (Freud) or the "unbeing" (Lacan) that are coextensive with human nature determine, if they do not justify them, the discontents of civilizations.

Such a view is not necessarily a product of pessimism ("one can do nothing in the face of crisis, that is the way it is"), nor disparagement of the present moment ("there is nothing new, things have always been like that"). Psychoanalytic fundamentalism nonetheless shifts the question's viewpoint. On the one hand, periods and societies that believe they are outside the crisis appear, in the psychoanalyst's eyes, symptomatic: through what miracle of repression, idealization, or sublimation has the discontent with "splitting" been stabilized, or even harmonized within a code of believable, sound, permanent values? On the other hand, in the Jewish and Christian West, the analyst is able to identify a perpetuum mobile that, nurturing the crisis basic to the speaking condition and sustained by it, creates the problems the West alone can resolve, although it never succeeds in doing so; and yet, it symbolizes them indefinitely in defilement and by passion, both henceforth universalized. In that case, where is the crisis? What makes up its painful image? Surely not a consciousness shattered by the unconscious, for the former can only recognize itself there, coil up, and speak of it, thus producing a new discourse, baroque or Joycian, witness to an "internal experiment" or to the shaping of a "theater of cruelty." If art may resemble a crisis, it is above all a resurrection. The crisis exists only for mirrors that are enamored of stable images; for calculators that are panic-stricken by swirling markets and currencies, for stabilizing consciences that believe in the contemporary divinity known as a "balance sheet." The analyst is neither an artist nor an accountant. Between the two, he is one of the last images of the fullness of passion.

What are analysands complaining of, dwellers in giant cities who have lost their bearings? Can we isolate The contemporary sickness, the one that colors the end of the twentieth century and slips it into the third millennium?

The Abolition of Psychic Space

To say that hatred or death drive prevail in the complaint more than inhibited desire or a mistreated eros is right but insufficient. Freud already knew it, from Beyond the Pleasure Principle (1920) up to Civilization and its Discontents (1929) and Analysis Terminable and Interminable (1937). After him, and independently of him, it is remarkable that women analysts, from Melanie Klein to Sabine Spielrein, have not ceased emphasizing that morbid component of the psyche. Resistant to the notion of castration, a woman perhaps accepts it only when confronted with a dying body (her child's in the worst of cases). Moreover, the dramas of individuation demand of her such a violent rejection of the mother, and by the mother, that in the hatred of the loved object a woman immediately finds herself in a known and intolerable country.

What analysands are henceforth suffering from is the abolition of psychic space. Narcissus in want of light as much as of a spring allowing him to capture his true image, Narcissus drowning in a cascade of false images (from social roles to the media), hence deprived of substance or place: these contemporary characters are witnesses to our being unable today to elaborate primary narcissism.

Neither screen nor state, primary narcissism is already a structure, previous to the Oedipus complex, which operates on the basis of three terms. The central node of connection and disconnection, fullness and emptiness, positions and losses, represents the instability of the narcissistic subject. He remains there, attracted on the one hand by the magnet of primary identification, which is a father imagined to be loving, "father of individual prehistory,"[2] the seed of the Ego ideal; and on the other, by a magnet of desire and hatred, fascination and disgust, constituted by the archaic mother who has ceased to be a container of needs but not yet made up into a taboo object of desire: neither subject nor object, an "abject"-mother, a place of warding off and differentiation, an infection. The breaking up of Christianity has left those three agencies in suspense.

The image of the Virgin—the woman whose entire body is an emptiness through which the paternal word is conveyed—had remarkably subsumed the maternal "abject," which is so necessarily intrapsychic. Lacking that safety lock, feminine abjection imposed itself upon social representation, causing an actual denigration of women; this in turn gave rise to increased antifeminism but even more so to a strong reaction on the part of women who were unwilling to bear, in narcissistic fashion, the representation of their own rejection of the maternal, which no available secular code could now guarantee. The first feminist generation rejected, through the "women-as-object," the narcissistic wound constituted by maternal sexuality, and countered it with the image of the virile activist who was less a libertine than a monitor; the second advocated a centripetal, mitigated, soothed feminine sexuality, before unearthing, quite recently, under the guise of romances among women, sadomasochistic havoc.

In similar fashion, the lack of a secular variant of the loving father makes contemporary discourse incapable of assuming primary identification—the substratum for our idealizing constructions. Thus orphaned, the homosexuality of a man seeking a feminine stance facing the other man encounters only its immediate erotic fulfillment. As to the woman, she lacks a go-between in order to assimilate the supposedly paternal Law and finds herself thrown into paranoia.

Between those two absences in contemporary discourse, Narcissus does not have his own territory. Without a paternal value, he is the negative version of a homosexual, potential but empty of desire. Handed over to an abject mother, he can have no recourse to the holy virgin to liberate himself of her. Worse yet, as an emancipated modern, he does not dare give himself the right to fight her. Like John, he walls himself in, is inhibited, distraught, hermetic, wracked by nightmares, ready to plunge into drugs if he could allow himself to be a happy Narcissus. His thought escapes him, his speech appears to him as empty as his body. "There are blank spaces between each word," he says in sibylline fashion. He specifies that his words do not hold together, for there is a void that breaks them into syllables, purloins them, and blow them up before they settle down between himself and those he speaks with.

As for Juliet, she throws herself into orgies that leave her cold, humiliated, full of rage and always ready to become ill in order finally to put a stop to the merry-go-round of a life controlled by a perverse father. The latter, a left-wing activist, gave his daughter to understand that stealing was part of the class struggle—until she got caught, thus punished for the father's greatest pleasure (secret, of course) and the mother's greatest shame. "I am trying to write," says Juliet, "but that isn't possible, for I have no place to settle down for that."

It is the paroxysmal consequence of a death drive that no object succeeds in hemming in, no idealization in displacing; the closeness to a psychosis that is more and more perceptible under the appearances of obsessions and hysteria—granted. But the frequency of psychotic symptoms in the area surrounding neuroses and perversions points to a deep challenge to psychic space, of which psychoanalysis, its revolutionary explorer, has inherited the whole Western, speculative history.

Narcissus: My Fellow Being, My Brother

At the risk of simplifying, let us consider that this now destroyed Psychic space was constituted at the waning of the Ancient world with the advent of the Christian era. The myth of Narcissus and its neo-Platonistic elaborations on the one hand, Christly passion on the other, mark out the outline of that space. Ovid's Metamorphoses gave us the first complete version of that myth. Narcissus, that perverse child, appears in it as the first modern antihero, the nongod par excellence. His murky, swampy, invisible drama must have summed up the anguish of a drifting mankind, deprived of stable markers. The imposing body of Greek sculpture becomes scattered at the time of the other crisis constituted by the waning of the Ancient world, which gives rise to the unhealthy and hardly tragic story of an ordinary being who knows neither what he wants nor what he loves. A novitas furoris is what we read in Ovid, a new madness. But who is the model for that? The one in love with his fleeting reflection is in fact someone deprived of his own proper space. He loves nothing because he is nothing. When he realizes that the other, in the spring, is merely an image of himself, Narcissus, unable to withstand such represented property, commits suicide. He is resuscitated, however, and not only as the flower bearing the same name, which takes his body's place. Narcissus is compensated for by the genius of speculative thought, starting with Plotinus and up to the Fathers of the Church who rehabilitate the narcissian concern for one's own proper space, beyond the condemnation of the narcissistic error. Torn away from the Ancient city-state (polis) now falling apart, thrown into the civilized world (oikoumene)—for which the present-day equivalent would be advanced mass-mediatization—man, seized by an unnamable solitude, was called upon to withdraw into himself and discover himself as a psychic being.

We have seen what efforts were made by Plotinus to build up the dignity inherited from ascetic solitude. His autarkic divinity proceeds by way of reflections, it explicitly follows the narcissian dynamic of inessential and yet necessary reverberations and rebounds, for they stem not from Narcissus but from the One. These thoughts then bind together in its unity, through the immanence of transcendence, the very motion of reflection, thus erecting a new psyche. The Western soul is no longer that of a Platonistic "Eros pteros," obsessed with the supralunar world, but that of a thinking space fastened on the same through the intermediary of the One, both origin and light of thought. Western innerness thus asserts itself, monos pros monon, establishing the space of psychic solitude. The dismal encounter between Narcissus and his death-bearing because fleeting image has been replaced by hands clasped in prayer—by being alone-with-oneself. Mythic tragedy has been changed into meditation and introspection. Henceforth there is an inside, an internal life, to be contrasted with the outside. Plotinus is on the ridge of that separation, the solar outside of the One making up the contemplative inside of the Wise man, without any otherness.

To Love or to Think

Christly passion, by bringing in the impossible, has jolted and opened up narcissism's calm contemplation, neutralized by the autarkic divinity's thought. Sin and passion indicate to Narcissus, who gazes at himself in the Beyond, that everything is not heavenly in this hell, and the son of God himself may be abandoned by his Father. The One is an Other in the agape of the Cross. Theological guile, however, did not forget that salvation, eventually, took over from narcissism. Not only must one love the other as one loves oneself, but God himself is amenable to our love only to the extent that we love our "own and proper" good. In connection with Amor sui we have reread Thomas Aquinas, who, in the wake of Augustine, already preaches that the right way to love is "to love oneself," "for and because of God." We have thus noted that without any selfishness, which is indeed a very secondary phenomenon, the Angelic Doctor advocates recognizing one's "own proper good" as the only possible access to the "being good," which is God. Such an appropriation of God and, conversely, the deification of one's own proper good, is an awesome dialectic, through which, thanks to a creating, donating Third Party, the Church promises salvation to narcissism, which is henceforth entitled, thanks to God, to withdraw into itself.

As long as the Western Self could think of itself as an Ego affectus est, with Bernard of Clairvaux for instance, its psychic space—introspective container of primary narcissism—remained safe and was constantly able to integrate crises. The heroism of an Ego cogito, which Descartes brought in along Aquinas' whitewashed [i.e., more philosophical than theological] tracks, led to the conquest of the outside, neglected in the narcissian salvation device. The outside of nature, to be subjugated by science; the outside of the pleasurable object, to be dominated through the sadomasochistic dynamics of libertine erotics. Galileo and Sade are the heroes of that conquering epic, whose ruin was heralded by Senatspräsident Schreber. In the eyes of this jurist, as interpreted by Freud, a person who, in the wake of humanism and the bourgeois revolution, experienced the impossibility of a stabilized psychic space (his own was shattered by rays and fundamental language), the unbeliever's world is established only through mystical delirium—thus always in a recourse to God, but a recourse that is henceforth devoid of meaning, insane. We have not escaped from the dilemma: Galileo and the revolutionary Sade on one side, the mad legislator Schreber on the other. The discontent always arises out of a repudiation of love—of the Ego affectus est.

There has been too much stress on the crisis in paternity as cause of psychotic discontent. Beyond the often fierce but artificial and incredible tyranny of the Law and the Superego, the crisis in the paternal function that led to a deficiency of psychic space is in fact an erosion of the loving father. It is for want of paternal love that Narcissi, burdened with emptiness, are suffering; eager to be others, or women, they want to be loved.

One has wrongly picked a quarrel with Freud in matters of sexuality: he supposedly did not understand women, he repressed his homosexuality, remained a Jewish, "uxorious" bourgeois. . . . Freud's discovery, which opened the royal road of sexuality, actually bears on the unbearable aspect of psychic space. An unbearable psychic space, hence laden with illusions, hallucinations, lies. . . Let us think once more about all his outlines, sketches, topographies, and territories that he does not cease proposing and renewing, from the Project. . . (1895) to Moses and Monotheism (1939). Lacan picked it up again, precisely in that place, to suggest, with the help of Borromean knot and topology, the vistas and infinitudes pertaining to the signifier's experience, which he no longer believes to be internal but wants to maintain amenable to spatialization, totalization, as well as controllable, mathematizeable. Is that possible?

On Eccentric Seeming: Imagination as Process

The stakes of psychoanalysis—but also its crisis—are there. Are we to build a psychic space, a certain mastery of the One, at the very heart of the psychic founderings of anguished, suicidal, and impotent people? Or on the contrary are we to follow, impel, favor breakaways, driftings? Are we concerned with rebuilding their own proper space, a "home," for contemporary Narcissi: repair the father, soothe the mother, allow them to build a solid, introspective inside, master of its losses and wanderings, assuming that such a goal is attainable? Or does not the abundance of sufferers who find their fulfillment, their relaxation, and their satisfaction only in intoxication (from drugs to sacred music, which do away with individuality and sex for the sake of infinity) indicate that a psychic era has come to an end?

I see psychoanalysis rather as the instrument of a departure from that enclosure, not as its warden. Does the old psychic space, the machinery of projections and identifications that relied more or less on neuroses for reinforcement, no longer hold together? Well, it may be because another mode of being, of unbeing, is attempting to take its place. We should not attempt to give it the outlines of the "own proper self" while assuring it of our authority as psychoanalysts and filling it with the psychological meaning of our interpretations. Let it remain floating, empty at times, inauthentic, obviously lying. Let it pretend, let the seeming take itself seriously, let sex be as unessential because as important as a mask or a written sign—dazzling outside, nothing inside.

Am I seeing the European psychic space toppling over into the Japanese?[3] And asking the analyst to be the agent of a new reign of the unauthentic, the promoter of a socialist empire of "false selves"? Has not the art of all periods already blazed that trail?

To the extent that the analyst not only causes truths to emerge but also tries to alleviate the pains of John or Juliet, he is duty bound to help them in building their own proper space. Help them not to suffer from being mere extras in their lives, or splinters of parceled out bodies carried along by the spate of their pleasure. Help them, then, to speak and write themselves in unstable, open, undecidable spaces. The free associations of analytic discourse paves the way for the polylogic of such a nomination and such eccentric writing. It is not a matter of filling John's "crisis"—his emptiness—with meaning, or of assigning a sure place to Juliet's erotic wanderings. But to trigger a discourse where his own "emptiness" and her own "out-of-placeness" become essential elements, indispensable "characters" if you will, of a work in progress. What is at stake is turning the crisis into a work in progress.

Speaking, writing? Is that not again building "one's own," be it polyvalent? While waiting for social institutions to integrate such extraterrestrials, those survivors of primary narcissism, it is still in the imagination and symbolic realizations that their faltering identity will best find a way to construct itself as necessarily false—imaginary. When behaviors and institutions will have integrated the failure of representation not as a misfire on the part of the machine or a suffering of the individual, but as an illusion among others, a new adjustment of narcissism will have been effected. It will remove guilt from the stable image and divest the transcendental Unity that insures its authenticity. It will actualize the seeming, the imagination. For such an open, undecidable psychic space, the crisis will not be a suffering but a sign within a framework whose truth lies in its ability to absorb seemings. I speak in favor of imagination as antidote for the crisis. Not in favor of "power to the imagination," which is the rallying cry of perverts longing for the law. But in favor of saturating powers and counterpowers with imaginary constructions—phantasmatic, daring, violent, critical, demanding, shy. . . Let them speak, the ET's shall live. Imagination succeeds where the narcissist becomes hollowed out and the paranoid fails.

Amatory Discourses and Transferences

Now imagination is a discourse of transference—of love. Through and beyond desire that longs for immediate consummation, love is edged with emptiness and supported by taboos. The fact that today we have no love discourse reveals our inability to respond to narcissism. Indeed, amatory relationship is based on narcissistic satisfaction on the one hand, on idealization on the other. If the "crisis" of psychic space sinks its roots into the "death of God," let us remember that for the West, "God is love." Paul's agape of the cross, John's "God is love," doubtless leave us cold, but empty, too. Freud, the post-Romanticist, was the first to turn love into a cure; he did this, not to allow one to grasp a truth, but to provoke a rebirth—like an amorous relationship that makes us good as new, temporarily and eternally. For transference, like love, is a true process of self-organization, comparable to what contemporary logical and biological theories call "open systems."

Psychoanalysis thus does not inaugurate a new amatory code, following on courtliness, libertine thought, romanticism, and pornography. It asserts the end of codes but also the permanence of love as builder of spoken spaces. If it shows that the transferential or amatory principle is indispensable for a body to be living rather than being a corpse under care, it paradoxically undramatizes the amorous relationship yet cathected in transference. The analytical pact, like Faust's pact with the devil, insures your renewal, your rebirth, your youth; against that background, your necessarily amorous crises might be temporary and unessential contracts. Freud had considered suggesting, among the powerful antidotes against the discontents of civilization, the amatory relationship; nevertheless he gave that up very quickly, for, in his view, if love furnishes an overwhelming feeling of fulfilled narcissism, nothing is more hurtful than a breakup.[4] He forgot, however, in that text, to mention that the psychoanalytic cure continues to feed on a love that transcends the hazards of loves. A transference love that summons the ability to idealize at the very core of desire and hatred—hollowing out and relieving perversion.

John and Juliet precisely come looking for that ability at the analyst's, who is perhaps the only one to allow them to glimpse an abeyance of their narcissistic wounds. Without ideological, moral, or biased suggestions, but through a simple listening, lovingly absent-minded. . .

From Cherubino to ET

Thanks to Christian elaborations, Narcissus was able to rally, give himself musical and pictorial dignity, and move generations on account of his metamorphoses. Into Cherubino, for instance. The one who, in Mozart's opera, knowing nothing about love, experiences a tenderness full of desire but does not know what it is.

Today Narcissus is an exile, deprived of his psychic space, an extraterrestrial with a prehistory bearing, wanting for love. An uneasy child, all scratched up, somewhat disgusting, without a precise body or image, having lost his specificity, an alien in a world of desire and power, he longs only to reinvent love. The ET's are more and more numerous. We are all ET's.

The only common ground between the contemporary symptom and Cherubino is that the language that tames and makes us love this being uprooted from psychic space remains always imaginary. Music, film, novel. Polyvalent, undecidable, infinite. A permanent crisis.
