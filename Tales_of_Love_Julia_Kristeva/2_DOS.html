II

Manic Eros, Sublime Eros:
On Male Sexuality

A History of the Notions of Love

The history of discourses and attitudes toward love must surely constitute the most exquisite storehouse of the Western soul.

Indeed, from the beginning, Psyche speaks and displays herself only in love. Rereading Plato once more, in the Symposium (385 B.C.) and Phaedrus (366 B.C.), where mythic discourse tumbles into philosophical discourse, we apprehend the first assertive apology for Western Eros under the guise of homosexual love. Delirium, mania, power relationships, sadomasochistic violence—that sort of erotics is nevertheless reversed, at the very heart of Plato's text, and soars on a winged flight toward the supreme Good through the glowing, soothing, ebullient vision of the Beautiful. Eros—a devastating possession—became, during the fourth century B.C., a Pteros, an idealizing bird caught in the rising motion of the soul, which, though fallen, necessarily remembers its previous loftier position.

We shall see later on how, through neo-Platonic thought, an ascending erotics became internalized, how it took on the violence of mania and made inner space the reflection of an alter-ego, an idealized Ego, sustained by a new myth, that of Narcissus. Thus, thanks to love, would salvation be assured. The feathered Platonic soul gave way to a Plotinian soul bearing a narcissistic mirror. This minirevolution has bequeathed us a new conception of love—a love centered in the self although drawn toward the ideal Other. This is a love that magnifies the individual as a reflection of the unapproachable Other whom I love and who causes me to be.

In addition, another powerful current, issuing from the Bible, joined in with those strands to form our Western loves. Judaism prescribed heterosexual love by basing its ethics on the family, on reproduction, and on the chosen number of those who understand the word of the Father. Never would Eastern eroticism, even when celebrated in the most erotic Hindu or Bangali poems, equal the joyful, quivering passion of the Song of Songs. For in the East, a body joys, lays out the pleasure of its organs, swells to infinite proportions in the bursting of its pleasure, quietly dependent upon the nourishing mother. But those are pleasures whose expanse is in itself differentiated, joys devolving upon a cosmos-speech, which set themselves aflame in their elements. While love for the other, and even more so for the other sex, came to us for the first time through king Solomon and the Shulamite—a precocious yet fragile triumph of heterosexuality, tinged with impossibility.

Christianity was to develop, out of the Gospels and in its theologies, not the furnishings of Eros but those of an Agape always already ensured by a Father who loves us before we are to love him. It took on Greek erotics as disguised in the dramas of a passion of the flesh. To this, however, it added the prudence of family fervor and the tension emanating from a nonrepresentable, impossible biblical love, as fervid as it is distant, in the manner of the Song of Songs. By strongly rehabilitating narcissism, theology, notably Thomist theology, made of amor sui the linchpin of salvation-love, thus reinforcing the reconciliation of Western soul with itself for two thousand years.

More "perverse" Franciscans and Jesuits, following Bernard of Clairvaux, prescribed maternal love, by contrast with the love of the Father, and the image of the Virgin with Child became, for the entire West, the pedestal for what is most dear to love—what is most reassuring, fulfilling, and sheltering when confronting the abyss of death. Paternal, narcissistic, and maternal, Christian love finds sustenance in all fonts of individual lapses, and offers perhaps the richest mosaic of words that the human being, that precocious, possessed person, that lover, ever need hear.

Our most enduring myths of love have been hewn out from such material. Tristan and Isolde, symbols of the forbidden couple, of death-love, of flesh rebelling against the law. Don Juan, the unbelieving seducer, overrun with a passion for subduing without possessing, eternal son achieving jouissance only in the mortal embrace of a father as idealized as he is terrifying. Romeo and Juliet, the accursed children of Verona who believe they have overcome hatred even as hatred consumes them in the purest moments of their passion. Exquisite mixture of destructive possession and idealization, crest between the flow of desire and the boundaries set up by prohibition, love crosses the threshold of modern times only in literature. When theology withdraws in the face of a philosophy that grounds being on knowledge rather than on affection, rhetoric—as it does when love is first experienced—steps in to gather the passion and enthusiasm of lovers. But after the sadistic breaking forth of drive, as after the more or less inspired voyeurism of pornography, does contemporary love find the delightful proportion of spleen and highmindedness, of flash and quiet, that it needs in order to exist? And if so, what is the new proportion? The receptive mind of a disappointed Goethian Jew, living in Central Europe between the two wars, continues to be the only one to come forward as capable of fitting out—with considerable risk and uncertainty—a new space for love.

And yet the old myths continue to live within us, and the archeologists of love find in Greece and in our dreams the words of Aristophanes and Diotima, Phaedra and Alcibiades. . . Pederastic Eros?

There is of course Sappho, as well as the strange heraistrai conjured up by Aristophanes in Plato's Symposium (191 e), who are "a section of the woman" and "do not care for men." There is, fragile and eager, the demand of modern feminism to fashion a love of self. But what else are they telling us, when it isn't the same phallic exaltation, what else but a return to our childhood's maternal love, when the softness of milk and caresses change our skin into communicating vessels? This also recalls Aristophanes' androgyns, if not, in a manner slightly more sexed, the sighs of the troubadours.

Eros is thus homosexual, and homosexuality must be understood, beyond love for paides, boys, as an appetite for homologation, for identification of the sexes, under the aegis of an erected ideal. Of the Phallus. Female homosexuality follows along paths that are more circuitous, complex, invisible, invaginated, less specular in order to fire the crucible of homologation with the other; it is therefore not at all symmetrical to the male rush toward phallic domination or submission to its brawn. That, however, should not prevent one from seeing to what extent any eroticized desire (male or female) for the other is a mania for joying in a fellow-being under the illusory presence of a superior.

Pederastic Eros, Philosophical Eros

Between the Symposium and Phaedrus, the two great Platonic dialogues devoted to Eros, the philosopher's language certainly varies, but some constants also remain. Though anchored in the mythology related by Aristophanes or Diotima in the Symposium, Eros nevertheless frees itself from it immediately and asserts itself, in the very Platonic dialectic and in the actual practice of pedagogy, as initiation to the Good and the Beautiful.[1] Always and without distinction love means love of young people, love of the Good, and love of true discourse. Pederast and philosopher all at once, Eros can thus touch other bodies (Aristophanes and Pausanias, for instance, suggest that Eros also concerns women). Since Eros, however, is formulated at the very heart of philosophical discourse when it emerges as such and is therefore borne by a philosopher in quest of his being, Eros is essentially the desire for what that man lacks. Having given to understand that love is love of something (Symposium, 199 d), Socrates specifies that with love as with desire, the object is, for the one who experiences it, "that which he has not already, and which is future and not present, and which he has not, and is not, and of which he is in want" (200 e). Likewise in Phaedrus (237 d), "Everyone sees that love is a desire."

This yearning for fusion with the supreme Good, a yearning at the same time for immortality, this desire for that which is lacking and sends its emanations through the body of the young man is considered an intermediary. Plato calls it a "daemon," by which he means the messenger, the go-between, the medium of synthesis between two separate domains. Such an interpreting and synthesizing daemon is thus called upon to fill an emptiness in order to make up a complete whole, an apotheosis of the soul led in such manner by Eros as close as possible to its unification with the divine. The initiatory journey that he begins constitutes, in Phaedrus, Psyche's daemonic space indeed—intermediary, interpretative, and synthetic.

Through Eros, according to Phaedrus, as through philosophy, the fallen soul, having lost some feathers in the fray, will be feathered again and climb toward celestial or even supracelestial heights that still activate its mobility, its action, and thus, more concretely, its love. Phaedrus demonstrates to what extent Psyche and Eros are interdependent, the soul becoming the necessary space, the receptacle for amorous passion. People have often asked whether it was necessary to mix considerations on love and considerations on the soul in the same dialogue. All one need do, however, is to note the complicity between the dynamics making up the two entities, Eros and Psyche; one then understands that not only in mythology but at the very base of philosophical discourse love and soul cannot be dissociated. Now the dynamic that love and soul share rests upon nothing else but phallic appetition. We are at once in the presence of what must indeed be called the erection of a body, always already seduced, inhabited, and carried away by Power. Socrates almost explicitly says so when he himself gets carried away by a felicitous turn of phrase and compares the ascent of the loving soul to the flight of a bird: Psyche—Eros—Pteros. This is how the loving soul acts, the soul as loving, or love inasmuch as it is, for the philosopher, an achievement of the soul:

for, as he receives the effluence of beauty through the eyes, the wing moistens and he warms. And as he warms, the parts out of which the wing grew, and which had been hitherto closed and rigid, and had prevented the wing from shooting forth, are melted, and as nourishment streams upon him, the lower end of the wing begins to swell and grow from the root upwards; and the growth extends under the whole soul—for once the whole was winged. During this process the whole soul is all in a state of ebullition and effervescence—which may be compared to the irritation and uneasiness in the gums at the time of cutting teeth—bubbles up and has a feeling of uneasiness and tickling; but when in like manner the soul is beginning to grow wings, the beauty of the beloved meets her eye. . .[2]

The imagery-laden description of an erection of teeth or feathers, of warming, swelling, or ebullition, even though caught in the pedagogic motion of an idealization in which every word is the metaphor for a memory of divinity, nevertheless compels recognition of its sexual, penial nakedness. Reading it thus, in its transparent sexual connotation, one can only share the hesitancy of the Fathers of the Church in granting a soul to . . . women.

In the Shadow of the Phallus: A Sadomasochistic Psychodrama

Very seriously, however, Plato is torn between a lowly delirium-love, such as he ascribes to his adversaries, and a sublime delirium-love, such as he tries to prescribe through the Academy and his philosophy—to prescribe for himself. Some, like Wilamowitz, believe that Plato himself is breaking away from recent physical loves, thereafter condemned, and this can be seen in the successive corrections of his discourse in Phaedrus. Whatever the biographical truth, which can no longer be verified, the fact remains that the ideas of Lysias the rhetor, presented by Phaedrus and rejected by Plato, are contested by the philosopher in his first speech solely because the dialectician finds their form incoherent; but they are accepted in their fundamentals, for they constitute, in the final analysis, an apology for manic-love and, more specifically, for enslavement mania. It is the master-slave dialectic, which would be, if I may interpret Lysias anachronically, at the basis of the relationship between lover and loved one (erastes-eromenos). Domination and servitude, possession and deprivation, exploitation and allurement, all cavort in the shadow of phallic attraction; and Plato, correcting what he terms Lysias' rambling remarks, concludes just the same, "Consider this, fair youth, and know that in the friendship of the lover there is no real kindness; he has an appetite and wants to feed upon you: 'As wolves love lambs so lovers love their loves'" (241 d).

That conception will of course be amended and even radically transformed through the intervening of another delirium, but this one is upright, for it is guided by wisdom. It is nevertheless the starting point, the setoff to a tremendous effort to idealize. And one finds the spoor of the sadomasochism that animates it (so to speak) in the pathos experienced by the loving soul, now described as a winged team of horses (one good, one bad) and a charioteer. The "stream of desire," himeros, which implies at the same time a thrust forward (hienai) and a flow of particles (mere) in a current (rheo) (251 c), becomes a few pages later a dramaturgy of the struggle between master and slave, a true sadomasochistic scene. "Now when the charioteer beholds the vision of love, and has his whole soul warmed through sense, and is full of the pricklings and ticklings of desire, the obedient steed, then as always under the government of shame, refrains from leaping on the beloved; but the other, heedless of the pricks and of the blows of the whip, plunges and runs away, giving all manner of trouble to his companion and the charioteer, whom he forces to approach the beloved and to remember the joys of love. They at first indignantly oppose him and will not be urged on to do terrible and unlawful deeds; but at last, when he persists in plaguing them, they yield and agree to do as he bids them" (253 e—254 b). At the sight of supreme Beauty the charioteer pulls back the reins of desire in order to subdue the horses of the soul. The first gives in, overcome with "shame and wonder"; but the other "is full of wrath and reproaches."

And when they are near [the beloved] he stoops his head and puts up his tail, and takes the bit in his teeth and pulls shamelessly. Then the charioteer is worse off than ever; he falls back like a racer at the barrier, and with a still more violent wrench drags the bit out of the teeth of the wild steed and covers his abusive tongue and jaws with blood, and forces his legs and haunches to the ground and punishes him sorely. (254 d-e)

As we can see, Plato does not mince words to reveal the lining, or rather the interior psychic space, woven with violence and pain, of a love that is so complacently called "Platonic" in the sense of spiritual and initiatory.

The loved one, in turn, thus subject to the fiery soul of the lover, experiences anteros, a counterlove whose calm consent amounts to very filial affection: ". . . bursting with passion which he understands not—he throws his arms around the lover and embraces him as his dearest friend" (256 a). We proceed from this point on to a peaceful conclusion of amorous relationship where, thanks to the guidance of the wise philosopher, moderation and goodness win out and lead toward a happy end, the shining existence of a perfect loving couple. But one should not forget that the surge of empathic desire (pathos: passionate desire and sorrow) illustrated by the psychodrama's winged team, sustains that perfection.

Delirium Can Be Perfection

The uprightness of wisdom, guided by the remembrance of a previous truth and beauty, guarantees amorous delirium its divine character and distinguishes it from simple, shameful mania. Like the sublime delirium of prophets, or that of the poet inspired by the Muses, or even the delirium of the philosopher who turns away from things in order to contemplate essences alone, love is sublime only if it remembers divinity—that is, perfection. The coercive act that tears away manic eroticism from perversion and hoists it to the pinnacle of idealization is explicit: it is called education and philosophy. Obviously it is no more than the solitary lining of the virulent pathos characteristic of the winged team, and the soul's wild imagery in that amorous dialogue confirms the debt incurred by the ideal to the test of strength unleashed for the conquest of power over the other.

Tied to this complicity between soul and love, in peace and in pain, wisdom and slavery, philosophical discourse itself is the third facet of the same dynamic, where phallic domination is elevated and metamorphosed into apprenticeship of the Good and the True. By breaking away from perversion, while remaining cognizant of it, philosophy is at the same time a psychagogy—a loving management of souls and a doctrine of discourse. On the side of domination-love, slavery-love, and allurement-love advocated by Lysias, Plato places a rhetoric that is meant for effect, goes in for seductiveness and spellbinding, neglecting the quest for the essential. In the maieutics of dialectical discourse, to the contrary, he sees the equivalent of what, on the level of Eros, is moderation and tendency toward perfection. If such love and language virtues are reached at the cost of a prohibition, we have no choice but to notice that Platonic discourse does not repress the war between ruler and ruled that is germane to the love relationship. I would say that Plato elaborates it, as it were, by privileging dialogue as test of the true in the face of the other—as a struggle of discourse. The Egyptian god of writing, Thoth, will be put in the same category with delusion—unessential tracing of dialogical speech that alone remains true: in the Platonic hierarchy, writing, like ineffectual rhetoric, is placed on the side of perversion.

There has been a recent attempt at rehabilitating, through the motion of an archetypal writing, the violence with which rhetoric transforms the soothed language of idealization. This motion has the advantage of restoring the dignity of Judaic writing in this instance, and also of lifting the repression that, in the stance of the wise man, weighs on the sadomasochism against which amorous metaphysical wisdom is thrust, as we have just seen.

Such a rehabilitation, however, often remains forgetful of what sustains Judaic writing—the Law set forth through words that are heard as well as written, a Law that does away with the small change of partial objects bearing pleasure; this in order to lead its subjects to the crest of a jouissance where, it is true, another intoxicating danger awaits them: the paranoia of the chosen. In claiming to avoid it, in assuming therefore in Judaism only the letter without the Law, one is led back to the perversion of Greek Eros and the sophistics of its fragmentary statements. This time, however, not as an open apology for male homosexuality, but with the "deconstructivist" aspects of a praise of unutterable feminitude, lodged in the folds of being and logos. Thus repressed, meaning, which is always that of dialogue, returns nevertheless on the elementary level of politics, in which the philosopher engages, by way of compensation.

Far from any deceit, Plato speaks to us, over the heads of his commentators, in harmony with the anxieties that fill modern cities. He is situated, in the fourth century B.C., as on a rooftop with slopes descending on either side. One opens out on manic, painful, exorbitant possession, on black masses of shameful nights and bodies deriving pleasure from being ridiculed. The other leads to superhuman effort (he called it supercelestial) on the part of the soul, which, within the same phallic dynamics, tears itself away from the allurement of having to accede to the dignity of knowing and being. Plato, our contemporary, ranging all the way from Christopher Street to M.I.T.? Far from constituting a simple museum, the history of loves today is a mosaic that is stretched out, synchronized before our eyes: the choice is yours! As you please! Read your left liberal newspaper and you will see that Phaedrus and Socrates are among us. . . Just as addicted to their mania, just as eager for a political or religious ideal. . . , just as frenzily feathered, their penes straining to equal the image of a Phallus that must necessarily exist since it draws out delirious spates. . .

And what about women in all that?

There are two myths in the Symposium, one about androgynes told by Aristophanes, the other concerning the birth of Eros, son of Penia and Poros (Poverty and Expediency), commented upon and glossed by the priestess Diotima, that assign women a given place in the twilight of the idols, which was Greek democracy.

The Androgynes

Mania, in the Symposium, is less about possessing oneself (as with Lysias and Socrates himself in Phaedrus) than becoming one. As amalgamating and daemonic, such a conception of love seems more feminine and has its origin in the image of an ancient era where dual, spherical beings would move about, wholly sated with themselves, so much so that they made the gods jealous; those beings were the androgynes. This third gender following male and female consisted in "the union of the two [other sexes], having a name corresponding to the double nature, which had once a real existence, but is now lost, and the word 'Androgynous' is only preserved as a term of reproach" (189 e). Lacking nothing, all of a piece, the shape of primeval being was round, "his back and sides forming a circle; and he had four hands and four feet, one head with two faces, looking opposite ways, set on a round neck and precisely alike; also four ears, two privy members, and the remainder to correspond" (190 a). The audacious wish of those total beings was to scale heaven and set upon the gods; therefore they were punished, the gods cut them in two—and that division was a sexualization. Henceforth, each one seeks the part of which he has been deprived, and that quest is the true impetus of action as well as love. While Aristophanes seems to make fun of androgynal ambition, just the same he draws a very important conclusion on the consequences of sexualization:

"Each of us when separated, having one side only, like a flat fish, is but the indenture of a man, and he is always looking for his other half" (191 d). Here the word indenture translates the Greek symbolon and refers of course to that broken object, the two halves of which bear witness, for those holding them, to old bonds between themselves or their families; but it also signifies sign, contract, a signification that is undecipherable without its counterpart. One should understand that each sex is the "symbol" of the other, its complement and support, its bestower of meaning. Love, as tendency toward synthesis, would be precisely that which creates the recognition of signs, a reading, significations, and would thus set itself up in opposition to the closed, egg-shaped world of androgynes.

And yet if one recalls how difficult it is to observe a contract, to institute and decipher symbols, one understands that it is easier for us, instead of loving an other, to dream of ourselves as androgynes. Androgynous is not bisexual. Bisexual would imply that each sex is not without some of the characteristics of the other and would lead to a nonsymmetrical doubling on both sides of sexualization (man would have a feminine part that is not woman's femininity, and woman would have a masculine part that is not the masculinity of man). In the hypothesis of bisexuality one deals with four components, which assume at the start two different relations, male and female, to the Phallus' power. As to the androgyne, he is unisexual: he is two of himself, conversant onanist, bounded totality, heaven and earth jammed together, a blissful coalescence a hairsbreadth away from catastrophe. The androgyne does not love, he admires himself in another androgyne and sees only himself, rounded, faultless, otherless. Coalescing in himself, he cannot even coalesce: he is fascinated with his own image. We are of course dealing with the homosexual fantasy of androgynism, not with biological makeup. A fantasy that uses the names of the two sexes ("andro" and "gyne") only the better to deny their difference. Such a paradisiacal vision becomes lost when, emerging from beloved childhood, the boy or the girl are but the penis of their mothers, while realizing through adult enactment the androgynal fantasy of a hysterical genetrix. The androgyne does in reality only what his mother lives in her imagination: it is fantasy realized; the androgyne is, among perverts, the one who comes closest to psychosis. Without love, which is the love that might save him? Perhaps the love of a mother capable of understanding him, but also cutting him off, sexualizing him. . . No matter how endless the analysis, it always comes to an end—it is possible to bring it to an end—when the analysand chooses one sex for himself. The androgyne, a fixated hystero-paranoid, senses it and remains on his guard. Unless he meets a Jungian analyst, with whom he can trade stories of archetypes, the androgyne fears the speech that differentiates, cuts off, identifies. His love chatter is a panicky flight away from the joys and discontents of sexualized love. Neither tragical nor comical, the androgyne is outside of time; hence he is timeless, the vanishing point of our distracted anxieties, our incompleteness, needs, desires for an other. . . Absorption of the feminine by man, veiling the feminine in woman, androgyneity settles its accounts with femininity—the androgyne is a phallus disguised as a woman; not knowing the difference, he is the sliest masquerade of a liquidation of femininity. . .

But what about love in the feminine, outside of silence, exterior to motherhood, and lacking phallic-androgynal sham? An inspiration? Mystery?

The First Platonist Woman: A Priestess

Diotima, in the Symposium, the great priestess of Mantineia, the wise stranger whose sacrifices had saved Athens from the plague, dictates to Plato the ideal, idealized, and in that sense "Platonic" concept of love. Let us note that in the Symposium, Socrates as depicted by Alcibiades at the end of the dialogue leaves us, in spite of his pedagogical efforts, with a totally ambiguous and carnivalesque impression of a shady wise man concealing under a seductive and libidinous appearance spiritual riches that are admirable but ambivalent to say the least, disturbing and subject to the disturbances of passion. The true ideal, the ideal Socrates, is Diotima. As if a goddess, a woman, were needed to desexualize love and thus maintain only the logic of idealization in the race for phallic domination-seduction-slavery that was celebrated in Alcibiades' inebriety as well as in those sections of Phaedrus that were mentioned above.

In contrast with the possession-love that Plato works out in Phaedrus, the Symposium proposes, perhaps even opposes, a uniting-love. In each case the loved object is a lacking object; but Diotima, more feminine or maternal, reaches it by uniting with it, while Lysias and Socrates, in the pantomime of the soul, arrive at it through the master and slave game. Diotima's love is a daemon, but contrary to the Christian demon, and more peacefully than in Phaedrus, it is above all a unifying go-between, an agent of synthesis. It is also more feminine to base love less on pleasure than on procreation or creation, at any rate on the production of bodies or works aiming for immortality. Such themes of course also underlay the text of Phaedrus, whose particular nature nevertheless asserts itself, it seems to me, as dominated by the sadomasochistic dynamic of the feathered beings. As if in Phaedrus Eros were displayed in its libidinal economy while Diotima, in the Symposium, presented it more along the lines of an idealized object relationship that it takes for granted.

Between Poverty and Expediency—A Sublime Eros

The myth of Eros' birth is exemplary in this respect. Penia (Poverty, Want), who had not been invited to the banquet of the gods, waits for the remains of the meal at the garden gate; by guile, she seduces Poros (Expediency) who, overcome by wine, had fallen asleep. Let us remember that Penia is above all want of form, raw matter, and denotes the absence of any determination (for Plutarch, she is the same as hyle, blind and formless matter). She is raw mass, not yet enlightened by the gods, rejected by them, vainly craving a relationship with them—she is poor and lacking. Poros, Expediency, has a very different background; in contrast with poor Penia, he is the son of Wile, of Metis. Whereas Penia has no reference marks, Poros, proi is used in connection with roads, sky and sea boundaries, what enlightens the darkness of primeval waters and blazes a trail for the sun. Embodiment of stratagem and craftiness, Poros is tied to techne in Aeschylus' Prometheus Bound. As offspring of his mother Metis, Poros necessarily harbors those traits of hers that relate to the fox and the octopus.[3] Deliberate, deceiving, dominating the domain of the senses while her husband, Zeus, rules the intelligible, Metis is like a wily agent of the symbolic within the maternal continent—spark of wisdom, dawn of the soul in the feminine universe?

Poros and Penia were thus to beget Eros. To such opposite and complementary parents, Eros will precisely owe that daimonic nature of which Diotima will celebrate only the privileges, omitting its anguish. It should be emphasized that with "want" the "path" is inscribed in the Symposium among the constitutive features of Eros. Love, more generally and profoundly than Promethean techne or philosophical bringing forth, for it is inherent in their poros, is perhaps the supreme path—one that "knows neither device nor mediation" (Heidegger). Path of want, a want on the way, want blazing a trail for itself. But also a path wanting in devices, a path without essence. Through such an alliance of want and path, could Eros be the place where dialectic takes shape but also opens up to a daimon that overwhelms it? Love as a path that leads nowhere . . . unless it be to immediate sight, scattered totality.

We shall thus love what we do not have; the object (of love) is the lacking object. But what is that object? It is beauty, or rather the power to give birth according to beauty. Love is a creative daimon, and that is why the philosopher, lacking and seeking beauty and a work, is a lover as much as he is a creator. "For there is nothing which men love but the good" (Symposium, 206 a). "Then love, she said, may be described generally as the love of the everlasting possession of the good" (206 a). What activity, then, will deserve the name of love? "The object which they have in view is birth in beauty, whether of body or soul" (206 b). But why give birth? "Because to the mortal creature, generation is a sort of eternity and immortality, she replied; and if, as has already been admitted, love is of the everlasting possession of the good, all men will necessarily desire immortality together with good" (207 a).

Such a spiritual procreation leads the amorous wise man, the wise man and/or the lover, on an enthusiastic ascent toward the supreme vision. The latter is no longer dialectic knowledge but the mystery of its path toward what is wanting. The immediate vision that Diotima suggests is perhaps an intellectual transposition of a pagan jouissance, the dazzlement of maternal fertility. Isn't that the archetype of power, of the Phallus itself if one agrees to give the word its symbolic connotation? Diotima is it, she is that Phallus, even if she doesn't have it. She hands it over to the philosopher whose task it is to possess it, to conquer it, and to use it to enslave or educate. Plato gave us the narrative dialogue of that appropriation when he imagined the dramatic action of the loving soul in Phaedrus. But here, in the Symposium, a few years earlier, fully intoxicated by the creation of the Academy in 387, he is delighted to carry on the torch of the mother-goddess' phallic jouissance and assume its benefits by virtue of his teaching. Diotima displays without dramatic complacency a jouissance that is potentially procreative because of the very fact that it insures harmony and survival, hence immortality, for the socius. In addition to the midwife's attributes that he takes on in Theaetetus, Socrates assumes that sublimity guaranteed in the final analysis by woman—the ultimate pillar of social stability. But he adds another logic that is perverse, extravagant, dominating and flogging, desiring without being generative—that of the polymorphic pleasure of the male. Raving Eros and sublime Eros, Alcibiades-Phaedrus on one side and Diotima on the other; feathered immaturity and the wise, unconcerned matron who joys through the vision of the Other. . . Out of this Socrates and Plato effected the synthesis upon which rests love in the West. . . Let me merely point out that the inspired, joying vision of a sublime beauty, an ideality that is powerful, absent but accessible by dint of dignified procreation, comes to us from a woman. Is the amorous philosopher a disciple of a visionary daughter? The idealist is the daughter of the dazzling, invisible father. All others are perverts, unless they follow her. . . Our loves—at the crossroads.

A Single Libido—Male

Is Eros a peculiar feature of man, of the male? That is at any rate what Freud thinks when he points out that there is "a single libido, the masculine one."[4]

Combining rejections and appropriations, mastery and slavery, the libido, brawling and engrossing, reaches its idealizing power by displacing that drive dynamic onto another object, one that is different from the sexed body whose mirage, as far as man is concerned, remains constituted (regardless of his pursuit of women) by the appeal of the tumescent-detumescent penis. This other object is the object of knowledge, fallen from the sky of mastery, which, for the small boy, is the aura surrounding maternal power or wisdom. Phallic power, in the sense of a symbolic power that thwarts the traps of penial performance, would in short begin with an appropriation of archaic maternal power. Man, as he displaces his desires onto the field of knowledge, finally works out the recipes of a Diotima who relieves him of the deadly unleashing of his erotic passion and holds up to him the enthusiastic vision of an immortal, unalterable object. A tracing of the ideal mother, that object of ideal knowledge allows man to build up his ideal Ego. Based on Diotimas as solid as they are overbearing, the ideal Ego of man powerfully withstands the devastating upheavals that are to the contrary induced by sexual, homosexual passions. It is in tearing himself away from homosexual Eros, usually by means of a heterosexual partner who paves the way for desexualizing the male libido, that man achieves a reconciliation with his ideal Ego as well as with the ideal, plain and simple. There remains, nevertheless, an erotic dynamic that has become "soul": the soul of the group, the tribe, "tribal effect."[5] Such a homosexual erotics shackles the death drive and acts as a counterbalance to death. Doesn't man engage in the sublime quest for immortality by piling up ideals in order to ward off strangling death—pleasure and destruction, erection and collapse—within eroticism itself? Freud, a seemingly diligent reader of the Symposium (witness the thirteen references to Plato in his writings) identifies Plato's Eros with his own notion of the libido.[6] And he presents in detailed fashion the myth Aristophanes tells about androgynes in order to back up his own postulates concerning the death drive.[7] In an attempt to transpose Platonism into biology, Freud seems to date sexual drives, in the sense of life drives, from a rather recent period, before which the death drive was dominant.[8] Aside from any biographical bases for Freud's speculation, this reasoning becomes imperative when we detect the male libido's primary ambiguity—deadly and life-giving, devastating and idealizing, a "Phaedrus" side, a "Diotima" side, raving Eros and sublime Eros. Socrates has the advantage of bringing together, by displaying them in paroxysmal fashion, those two aspects of male sexuality. Would his metaphysical knowledge have compelled recognition with such force if it did not reveal the homosexual erotic lining that sustains it—and also his life-and-death struggle against death? Umbilicated with death in his aggressive thrust toward the desired object, warding off death through symbolic fecundity, which creates objects of wisdom, man goes round the feminine, which is his abyss and his night. Respectful and respectable love for an idealized (maternal) object spares the delights and the pangs of sadomasochism—the divine is finally a goddess, priestess of archaic power, which allows less to repress than to separate raving desire from its refinement, from its dialectic and academic education on behalf of the city-state.[9]

Laughing to Death

Laughter is probably the synthesis—a shattered, engulfed, absurd synthesis—of those two shores of male erotic experience. Socrates' irony and more so the whirligig of speeches that contradict and cancel out one another throughout the symposium, as well as the petty, piqued, and yet radically comical description Alcibiades gives of the master at the very moment a gang of carousers irrupts into the nobly pretentious setting, all this surely translates the uncontrovertible fact that one laughs only at the Phallus. On condition of being sure of having it, so as to accept relinquishing it temporarily. The obverse of such enthrallment with power, which can be more or less bitter, is the melancholy so dear to poets.

Jamming life into death, leaving no space between them, melancholy is a constant anxiety on the moral level, a painful impotence on the sexual one. Collapse of enthusiasm, sway of the abyss into which one can read the unsurmountable ascendancy of a stifling mother, melancholy drains knowledge, destroys erection. Perverse reversibility (voyeur/exhibitionist, sadist/masochist) then comes to an end with a nondisjunctive aggregate that voids both attitudes (active and passive) within the sadness of stagnation and despair. Probably because it set the bar too high, with imposed obsessive superegotic demands, the melancholy personality collapses even before it touches down. The specter of melancholy haunting the strung bow of phallic power as the reverse of its extreme tautness found shelter in literary experience from Nerval to Thomas Mann, where it became sated or cured. The belletrist's disillusioned attitude is probably a lesser form of the ailment that demystifies wisdom, beauty, style, and eros itself. Incapable of sustaining a form or object of desire for any length of time, the melancholy person spoils them as soon as they are set and dissolves himself into an intoxication without countenance. Thomas Mann, in Death in Venice, extolled that esthetic melancholy at the heart of a shameful and disillusioned homosexual passion, mentioning Phaedrus reinterpreted in the somber light of a mouldy affluence. "For knowledge, Phaedrus, does not make him who possesses it dignified or austere. Knowledge is all-knowing, understanding, forgiving; it takes up no position, sets no store by form. It has compassion with the abyss—it is the abyss." The artist, such as Gustav Aschenbach, who decides therefore to devote himself to style alone, is yet threatened anew: "But detachment, Phaedrus, and preoccupation with form lead to intoxication and desire. They may lead the noblest among us to frightful emotional excesses, which his own stern cult of the beautiful would make him the first to condemn. So they too, they too, lead to the bottomless pit."[10]

The Soulosexual

Joying in the pangs of that abyss in the moral suffering of a downfall as coveted as it is condemned, or in the physical suffering mortifying one's own flesh, can then become one of the foreseeable excesses of Plato's "winged chariot." Christian suffering is rooted in large part in that tendency of the homosexual soul—the soulosexual—to undergo martyrdom in order to maintain the fantasy that there exists a power, as well as its masochistic obverse—passivation, total "feminization." Masochism, which, we are told, is essentially and originally feminine, is a submissiveness to the Phallus that the soulosexual knows well and can assume until death in order to become the "true" woman—passive, castrated, nonphallic—that his/her mother was not. Mishima, mistaking himself for Saint Sebastian, and even Pasolini, allowing himself to be executed by a hoodlum on an Italian beach, carry to the limit the slavish moment of male eroticism appended to a deathful veneration of the Phallus. And all those having a soul—men or women—rush to contemplate it as if they saw themselves. Mortification settles in, in fact, when artistic sublimation—a liberating way out in the face of the unbearable weight of the ideal—proves of little avail as opposed to the morbid delights of the entire body changed into a murdered penis. Social conditions (persecutions, ideological and financial constraints of all kinds, and so forth) and the greater or lesser strengths of enigmatic talents are in this case essential factors that stem the morbid unfurling of drives or, on the contrary, hasten it; yet this should not hide a fundamental given: Eros is essentially manic. Its infatuation with power, idealized as it might be, hence its joying in the Phallus, has its reverse side—depression, the destructive relish that takes pleasure in dissolving the Ego and even the living being itself. Probably more archaic than sexual jouissance, such a pleasure, with its strong morbid component, is, according to Freud's biological myth, anchored in the prehistory of matter that propagated by means of scission before having acquired an organ with erotic and procreative functions. Whatever the biological truth, fantasy, for its part, grounds soulosexuality's propensity for the morbid (and also all depression) in the area around the hole (Gustav Aschenbach calls it the "abyss")—the adored and the abhorred maternal sex. . .

Thus stretched out over a gamut that runs from sex to heaven, from horse to bird, demoniacal Eros is another fundamental means—the other modality—of culture and tranquillity for man and society. Other as opposed to mystical purification that confronted man with maternal abjection—with a mother less sovereign than desirable as much as she was threatening, major appeal for the centripetal, autonomic tendencies of the future speaking being.[11] The man of abomination, the man of catharsis, was confronted within fear and shame by a pre-object that was fascinating and repulsive. To free himself from it he marked out boundaries, interdicts, and taboos on his own body and within language, which is the true territory of his habitat. By contrast, erotic, manic, or idealistic man does not touch the mother. The taboo has been imposed once and for all: language and law are already there. What he has to do now is maintain them, thus fulfilling the desire of a sovereign, untouchable mother; or transgress them, while remaining faithful to her own ascendancy in the atopia of erotic, homosexual possession. The latter, although made marginal by the metaphysical, educational ideal, will nevertheless torment bodies as a joying, shameful, nocturnal lining, and the language of the philosophical educator as well. Fountainhead of pleasure, and fountainhead of rhetoric. One will never sufficiently contemplate the exquisite motion by which the rhetorician surreptitiously transgresses language, less in order to impose an outrageous, scandalous, and base style—as would have done the poetic or prosaic stylistician who also dares touch the mother—than to include that subtle disturbance within the most respectfully submissive, well-mannered, harmonious communication. In love with language, the rhetorician is finicky about details, a fetishist of readymade, beautiful forms, and he draws them from the fountainhead of his soulosexuality, which is thus only revealed, in order to fashion a crown to a real or imaginary Diotima. The prototype for the troubadours' Lady, that supreme addressee of an eros that has been transmuted into an apologetic verbal creation, shares with a woman only the repressive power of the phallic mother. The one who joys only in being filled with her own father—the divine.

That the libido, hence Eros, be male does not absolutely prevent the woman from placing her loves in it. Through a more or less painful identification with man. With more letdowns on the sadomasochistic manic side, also with more difficulties in keeping faith in the vision of supreme wisdom gotten from an idealized mother. She will tend to replace the ideal mother with a superego-prompting father; such a change will provide her with fewer jubilatory visions but more overburdened and disciplined efforts through accession to knowledge.

And the Other Sex in Itself

If, on the other hand, there were a female libido, could one imagine an erotics of the purely feminine?

To the extent that she has a loving soul, a woman is drawn into the same dialectic involving confrontation with the Phallus, with the whole accompaniment of ideal images and domination-submission tests that it implies. Would she wish to break free from it, she would encounter the dynamics of Phaedrus in the mirage of androgyny or love for another woman. Whatever the organ, confrontation with power remains. Nevertheless, androgynous paradise and, in another way, lesbian loves comprise the delightful arena of a neutralized, filtered libido, devoid of the erotic cutting edge of masculine sexuality. Light touches, caresses, barely distinct images fading one into the other, growing dim or veiled without bright flashes into the mellowness of a dissolution, a liquefaction, a merger. . . It evokes the loving dialogue of the pregnant mother with the fruit, barely distinct from her, that she shelters in her womb. Or the light rumble of soft skins that are iridescent not from desire but from that opening-closing, blossoming-wilting, an in-between hardly established that suddenly collapses in the same warmth, that slumbers or wakens within the embrace of the baby and its nourishing mother. Skin; mouth; empty, excited, or filled opening of the lips—they coat such emanations, drain their tension, and, beyond any aggressive breakthrough, float, cradle, drug. Relaxation of consciousness, daydream, language that is neither dialectical nor rhetorical, but peace or eclipse: nirvana, intoxication, and silence.

When such a paradise is not a sidelight of phallic eroticism, its parenthesis and its rest, when it aspires to set itself up as absolute of a mutual relationship, the nonrelationship that it is bursts into view. Two paths are then open. Either they take up again, yet more fiercely, the erotic mania along with the havoc of the "master-slave" game. Or else, and often as a consequence, death breaks into the peace one thought one had taken in. A grinding death in that belly that had previously been so protective, coaxing, and neutralizing. Death from being merely a some one [on]: lost identity, lethal dissolution of psychosis, anguish on account of lost boundaries, suicidal call of the deep.

Insoluble, dramatic up to the very moment of the drama's dying out, our loves leave us only with the so-called perverse solution—shifting from the abject to the sublime, savoring the gamut of sufferings and delights, supreme guarantee against boredom. . .

A Holy Madness:
She and He

"Man does not know what love is, or hate, and both of these in his eyes are vanity"

(Ecclesiastes 9:2)

Unrepresentable Law

Be it passionate or even base, like the bond between man and woman, or sacred like the one linking man to God and vice versa, biblical love is most often signified by the root 'ahav, "to accept," "to adopt," "to recognize" (and can be exchanged with "to reject," "to disown," "to repudiate," for hatred). On the axis of absorption and rejection, love assimilates, tames, shelters, thus including a maternal or even uterine connotation; but it also allies itself with, recognizes, legalizes—with a paternal connotation. Thus riham, out of rehem, uterus, marks a sense of family; hafez means pleasure of, pleasure for; razah, to feel comfortable with, to accept; terms like hashak, expressing a personal attachment, or hanan, suggesting concrete favor rather than affection, and finally hased, meaning loyalty but also true love, alliance (Gen. 20:13, 47:39), would have a stronger intellectual connotation.

The early texts of the Bible contain only two, very elliptical references to God's love for man.

"David consoled his wife Bathsheba. He went to her and slept with her. She conceived and gave birth to a son, whom she named Solomon. Yahweh loved him and made this known through the prophet Nathan who named him Jedidiah [beloved of Yahweh] in accordance with the word of Yahweh" (2 Sam. 12:24—25). It is thus through ("avour") Nathan that Yahweh lets David know that he loves the child; God has not himself spoken of love, he expresses himself here through a go-between; a second name is then given to this child of illegitimate love, indicating that it is recognized by God. But the name Jedidiah will not appear in the text again.

"Blessed be Yahweh your God who has granted you his favor . . ." asserts the queen of Sheba, seeing that Yahweh loves Israel (1 Kings 10:9). It will be noted that it is a foreigner who speaks and that she expresses herself indirectly, by means of riddles.

The immediate love of God for his people, a love that demands neither merit nor justification but is based on preference and choice, directly establishes the loved one (who is also loving) as a subject in the strong sense of the term. Its correlative is nonrepresentability: it is through Nathan, but without words, that it becomes known that Solomon, son of David, is loved. The name of the child, Jedidiah, is not repeated but appears to be inscribed there as if to appease and call attention to the desire and murder that ground Solomon's conception—the queen of Sheba does speak in riddles . . . That nonrepresentable love, attributable as it might be to a legislating god, is nonetheless accessible to someone who, like David, utters a loving discourse that is only gesture and voice—sound, cry, music, afloat on primal repression, incantation of primary narcissism. Plato's "chora" in Timaeus is perhaps more adequate to evoke a significance of that sort, one that is prior to the sign.

The theme of divine love is fully developed in Deuteronomy (4:37, 7:8, 7:13, 10:15, 10:18, 23:6). On the threshold of exile Ezekiel takes up again the affirmation of God's love for his people (Ezek. 34:11—16): "For the Lord Yahweh says this: I am going to look after my flock myself and keep all of it in view." In like manner, Jeremiah: "I remember the affection of your youth, the love of your bridal days . . . Israel was sacred to Yahweh" (Jer. 2:2-3).

But the most noted statement of biblical love is set forth as a law of love, as a duty of the faithful toward God and their brother: "You shall love Yahweh your God with all your heart, with all your soul, with all your strength" (Deut. 6:5), and: ". . . you must love your neighbor as yourself" (Lev. 19:18). Yet that love-law often causes one to forget the complex dynamic of biblical love that the Song of Songs displays and elaborates.

History, Memory, Phallus

There are differences of opinion as to the possible date of composition for the Song of Songs. For some, the text that frequently refers to Solomon (3:7, 9, 11; 8:11, 12) would have been composed by Solomon himself, son of David. The earliest date was set around 915-913 B.C. This opinion prevails among nineteenth-century Jewish and Christian critics.[1] Others believe the text is more recent but contains allusions to archaic periods.[2] Those who base their arguments on linguistic analyses generally deem that the text can be dated back only to around the third century B.C.[3] Among modern scholars, Chaim Rabin returns to the hypothesis of the Solomonic origin of the text, educing it mainly from Indian religious influence (archeologically attested, according to some) on Hebrew civilization up to the second millennium B.C. Such Indian influence would be evidenced in the Song of Songs by the woman's being the main subject of enunciation, by the frequent celebration of nature's renewal, and finally by the listless quality of the woman lover, which, beyond a certain amount of male aggressivity, constitutes the dominant tone of amorous feelings—a coloration that is particularly frequent in Tamil poetry, according to this writer.[4] He assumes that the text might well have been written by someone having traveled through Southern Arabia as far as India during the golden age of Jewish trade with the Orient, which also corresponds to Solomon's time and to that of Tamil poetry. Let us point out that Adam Clarke had already established correspondences between the Song of Songs and the Gita-Govinda.[5] These interpretations note a similarity between an Indian divinity (Krishna, for instance), both sensuous and mystical, and the woman lover in the Song of Songs; but they forget to note that the enunciation of the Song of Songs is very specifically individualized, assumed by autonomous, free subjects who, as such, appear for the first time in the world's amatory literature.

The first Jewish interpretations, like Christian ones later on, are allegorical. Rabbis read into the love of the Song of Songs the relationship between Yahweh and the chosen people. That is the midrashic interpretation as well as that of medieval commentators like Saadia, Rashi, and Ibn Ezra. On the basis of linguistic givens, it is thought that the Song of Songs' Targum might be dated from the fifth century A.D. up to the ninth at the latest.[6] We find in the Targum the famous gloss by the great scholar Saadia (892-942) according to which the Song of Songs is a chest whose keys have been lost. Subsequent Christian interpretation sees in it the Church's yearning for God, if not the forecasting of the Virgin Mary's love or of the mutual love between Christ and the Church. A few moralists were shocked by the idea of a woman, a shepherdess to boot, making advances to a king and thought the psychology lacking in verisimilitude or alien to Western ways. Bossuet, in 1693, observed the correspondence between the Jewish nuptial week and the divisions of the Song of Songs; there followed a theory proposing the Song of Songs as transposition of nuptial songs, and this includes Ernest Renan's investigation as well as more ethnological research comparing the Song of Songs with Syrian nuptial customs. One has been able to prove relationships between the Song of Songs and pagan fertility cults observed in Mesopotamia. It has been interpreted as a worship of the God Tammuz-Adonis rather than the God of Israel.[7]

Jewish mysticism, which is now well understood thanks to the work of G. Scholem,[8] interprets the Song of Songs in the light of what R. Patai has called the Hebrew Goddess. Exegeses of this sort have their point of departure in the demonstration that Yahweh was originally represented with a female companion. Later, when it became forbidden to represent God, the woman was demoted to the position of guardian, represented by two female cherubs. After the destruction of the first temple, the idea takes hold that God alone possesses both male and female characteristics, and henceforth the cherubs manifest only divine attributes. For the Talmud, the male cherub represents God, the female cherub the people of Israel. Finally, the Cabala elaborated the mystical sephirothic theory and viewed King and Maronite as two divine entities.[9]

Let us note lastly that "feminism," as if stemming from a form of Hindu tradition in Song of Song studies, deciphers in it an instance of support for its "depatriarchalizing" interpretation of Judaism.[10]

Seeing love represented in the Song of Songs as the powerful antidote for death has led some scholars to find connections between its text and the orgiastic celebrations in Babylonian and Greek worship, for which, among others, Ugaritic texts bear witness. The obsessive presence of myrrh and of spices commonly used in such mortuary and orgiastic banquets is presented as evidence, as well as certain linguistic data. Let us recall that the Greek herma, and the Ugaritic and Hebrew yad, "hand," are used to designate phallus and tombstone. Likewise, in Hebrew, "memory" and "phallus" seem bound to the same root, *dkr, *zkr.[11] How can one not pay attention to such interpretations when reading in the Song of Songs that "love is strong as death"?

Metaphor of Metaphors

The phrase Shir ha-Shririm is a superlative that at once excludes the amorous incantation from any other discourse, song, and other things sacred. The title, however, does not reveal the allegorical scope of the dramatic incantation it contains. That will be done in the Lamentations of Jeremiah, which, in Hebrew, carries the name of the first word of the text: "How," eykah ("Oh, how lonely she sits, the city once thronged with people, as if suddenly widowed"). Still that adverb, axis of allegories, symbols, figurative sense, is just as suited, if not more so, to a song of love as to a lament. Unless, seeing that they are brought together in the Five Scrolls and barely separated from the story of Ruth the Moabitess, which perhaps insures their happy continuity, we interpret love and lament as invocations issued out of the same reserve of incompleteness, failing, call for meaning. Love as unacknowledged lament? Lament as unsuspected love?

Greek theater and poetry on the one hand, Mesopotamian fertility rituals on the other, most probably feed this song whose tone is so often pagan and yet finds its natural place in the Bible. Rabbis understood this, about the year 100, at Yabneh, when they finally agreed, not without reservations, to include the amorous dialogue within the bosom of sacred writings. "In the beginning, Proverbs, the Song of Songs, and Ecclesiastes were suppressed; because they were considered as mere parables that did not belong in the Holy Writ [religious authorities] rose up to suppress them; [they remained so] until the coming of Hezekiah's men who interpreted them."[12] Rabbi Akiba, for his part, ardently defended, probably with some irony, the right of the challenged text to be included: "God help us! There never was a man in Israel who questioned the sacred nature of the Song of Songs; for the whole world is unworthy of the day when the Song of Songs was given to Israel. If the entire scriptures are holy, the Song of Songs is holier than the rest."[13]

Before looking into the allegorical rabbinic interpretation that justified the insertion of the Song of Songs into the Bible, let us emphasize some rhetorical aspects of the text.

He Exists: He Hastes Away

The hymn of love at once confesses its source, its object, and its addressee—Solomon the king, at the same time author and loved one, is also the one to whom the text is addressed. "The Song of Songs, which is Solomon's / Let him kiss me with the kisses of his mouth. Your love is more delightful than wine. / Delicate is the fragrance of your perfume, your name is an oil poured out, and that is why the maidens love you."

A king threefold: sovereign, poet, lover, the addressee of amorous passion is there, posited from the start without any hesitation. He exists and he loves, for it is out of him that the—his?—words of love are issued. The loved one also reveals his presence by speaking personally and in a manner very much like that of the Shulamite lover, picking up her phrases and turns of speech and applying them to her. It is true that the presence of the loved one is fleeting, it is eventually no more than an expectation, and at the end of the song (which is said to have been displaced), the lover goes as far as to adopt the loved one's wandering, his perpetual flight, by suggesting it to him ("Haste away, my Beloved. Be like a gazelle, . . ."), as if he were not in himself and from the beginning of the text an unceasing rush . . . Nevertheless, and through the very flight that is assumed by both protagonists—lovers who do not merge but are in love with the other's absence—no uncertainty affects the existence of the one who is loved and loves: "Let him kiss me with the kisses of his mouth."

Finally, the elusiveness of the loved one is revealed by the presence of the shepherd. Some verses lend themselves to ambiguous interpretation: who is the lover—a king or a shepherd? Renan thought he could detect the presence of the shepherd, beloved of the maiden, whom he notes we were already able to glimpse in chapter one, verse seven, "who now (chapter five) becomes an absolute certainty." Things, however, are not that explicitly obvious, and the generally allegorical nature of the text allows indeed for all sorts of surmises, except, perhaps, that of the shepherd, precisely, as far as chapter five is concerned. Renan pursues, "This a capital point and the key to the poem. If one has erred so long with respect to the organization of the poem, it is because one has not noticed the important distinction made at that point; the distinction entails that Solomon is not the loved object and, what is more, his absence is the necessary condition that allows the loved object to be enjoyed."[14] Perhaps such a reading has the advantage of emphasizing the biblical tension between a present (incarnate) loved one, who would be the shepherd, and another nonrepresentable one, holder of authority and taboo, forever out of reach although he conditions the lover's existence, and who is God (here, Solomon). There is, however, a problem in rationalizing those two aspects of divinity in such a way, in dissociating them as drastically as Renan would wish. Indeed, such a distinction may seem as foreign to the allegorical text of the Song of Songs—which is designed to put one off the track and thus favor connotative readings, individual and collective interpretations—as it is to the very spirit of the Bible. The latter does keep God invisible; nevertheless it does emphasize his presence in all ritual as well as daily aspects of life, without presenting any desirable, corporeal reality (as the shepherd would be for the shepherdess) that might be compared to or placed in competition with his authority. Renan has perhaps given us a lay reading, and too much so, because it is finally a humanistic one, and too much so, insensitive as it is to the absolute tension of love for the Other. Supreme authority, be it royal or divine, can be loved as flesh while remaining essentially inaccessible; the intensity of love comes precisely from that combination of received jouissance and taboo, from a basic separation that nevertheless unites—that is what love issued from the Bible signifies for us, most particularly in its later form as celebrated in the Song of Songs.

Indeed, as soon as the evocation of the amorous experience begins we step into a world of undecidable meaning—the world of allegories. "Your love is more delightful than wine; / delicate is the fragrance of your perfume, / your name is an oil poured out, / and that is why the maidens love you." The "name" that is evoked almost from the very beginning induces intoxication; its precision and uniqueness trigger, it would seem, an overflowing of meaning, a flow of significations and sensations comparable to that produced by caresses, perfumes, and oils. The sensitive and the significant, the body and the name, are thus not only placed on the same level but fused in the same logic of undecidable infinitization, semantic polyvalence brewed by the state of love—seat of imagination, source of allegory.

The Figure of Speech Is Amatory

As in Greek poetry, as with Theocritus or Vergil, the biblical love in the Song of Songs is stated in metaphorical discourse. The figure of speech is amatory: as condensation and displacement of semantic features it points to an uncertainty, not concerning the object of love (Solomon's existence is more than certain, absolute, even if he flees—he is the author, just as he is the shepherd) but an uncertainty concerning the bond, the attitude of the loving subject toward the other. It is the very enunciatory pact, in its amatory particularity and on account of the intensity of affect required on the part of the two lovers (who are the two speakers), that disturbs the normal, univocal exchange of information. While signed by Solomon, it is nevertheless spoken with equal if not dominant strength by his beloved, a woman, the Shulamite; consequently, the pact does not cease unveiling its dramatic duality, which is internalized by each of the lovers and perhaps even by each of their words. In order to become, for the other, more evocative of the experience that is germane to the loving subject—for such is the objective of amorous dialogue—each bit of information is loaded with semantic polyvalence and thus becomes undecidable connotation. One can trace the path of that metaphorical discourse down to Baudelaire, and its nonetheless polyvalent ellipticalness with Mallarmé. Let me point out, nevertheless, that with the very dawn of lyrical poetry—(and the Song of Songs, which reminds one of Greek tradition, condenses its rhetoric magnificently)—the transfer of meaning (metapherein = to transport) sums up the transference of the subject to the place of the other.

Infinity and Repetition

Commentators have also noted the aleatory order of the verse: the last one could well be situated in the body of the text, and moreover any other change of order would not disturb the value of the whole. Whatever the historical or textual reasons for it, that compositional disorder in fact emphasizes, and especially to the extent that it does not affect one's understanding of the amorous message, that the lyrical meaning is contained in each of the minimal elements of the text, which thus condense, in microcosmic fashion, the totality of the message. A metaphor, and perhaps even a simple invocation, a tonality of speech, are endowed with the semantic power of the whole.[15] The intensity of an element (word, melodic or choreographic sequence, etc.) is at once present, imposed, presupposed. As if, because of the simple amorous enunciatory act, the totality of signification (itself undecidable, allusive, blurred, precisely containing no more than the principle of the mutual aspiration of the speakers for each other) worked its way into signs and infrasigns. The lyrical whole is not logical gradation or structure founded on this or that variant of the laws of repetition: it is a renewal, itself open, infinite, indefinite—in salvos, gusts, reiteration of a violence that is as semantic as it is emotive—of the intensity of the amatory pact. That neglect concerning structure involves only the large discursive units (sequence of verses, for instance). Syllables, rhythms, alliterations are rigorously ordered within each verse, and, besides, duplication is compulsory. Thus, to the description of the beloved by Solomon (4:1), "How beautiful you are, my love, how beautiful you are! / Your eyes, behind your veil, are doves" there corresponds the description of the loved one by the Shulamite (5:1): "My beloved is fresh and ruddy, / to be known among ten thousand. . . . / His eyes are doves at a pool of water." One also finds in 7:4 a resumption of 4:5 ("Your two breasts are two fawns, / twins of a gazelle"), as well as numerous identical comparisons of neck with tower, teeth with flock of sheep, evocations of Lebanon, Carmel, and so forth.

Such persistence of repetition at the level of small units of discourse and of coded comparisons or metaphors, along with its relaxation at the higher level of logical organization of the whole—where it nevertheless continues to be present in muted fashion, producing not an ordered composition but aleatory resumptions—suggest the impact of the death drive in the amorous invocation. The beloved states it straightforwardly: "For love is strong as Death, jealousy relentless as Sheol. / The flash of it is a flash of fire, / a flame of Yahweh himself" (8:6).

A Dialogue

Finally, let me point to the dramatic aspect of a text that is spoken by two soloists and a chorus that, split in two, also sings duets: "Return, return, O maid of Shulam, / return, return, that we may gaze on you! / —Why do you gaze on the maid of Shulam / dancing as though between two rows of dancers?" (7:1). Borrowed from Greek drama but also from Mesopotamian rituals, those aspects inscribe amorous discourse in the dynamics of stage performance. The characters in other biblical sequences (think of David, Isaac or Abraham, or even Ruth, who figures in the Five Scrolls, which also contains the Song of Songs) are the agents and functions of a narrative. No matter what psychological dimension the modern reader gives them, they are actually logical markers within the unfolding of the divine word addressed to the chosen people. On the other hand, with the dialogue between the Shulamite and Solomon, we are witness to the staging of a dialectic: the protagonist constitutes himself as such, that is, as lover, as he speaks to the other, or as he describes himself for the other. A technique of Greek drama, as well as deep logic of the act of communication, such theatricalization of speech and its subjects is accomplished with the greatest intensity and verisimilitude where an amorous situation is involved. Nevertheless, the dialogue of the Song of Songs is neither tragical nor philosophical; while it radically opposes the sexes it still ties their real and symbolic communities together. The amorous dialogue is tension and jouissance, repetition and infinity; not as communication but as incantation. Song dialogue. Invocation.

Whether the speaking subject, insofar as he is a loving subject, is in a permanent state of flight with regard to his addressee; whether he calls him, precedes him, answers him, follows him, without ever uniting with him except in the synthesis of the choruses that break up into the two parts of a duet—such a dynamic reveals, at the very heart of monotheism, at least two motions. The first amounts to the following: through love, I posit myself as subject for the speech of the one who subdues me—the Master. The subjection is amorous, it supposes a reciprocity, even a priority for the sovereign's love (Solomon is, as I said, the presumed author of the song, the one without whom the song would not exist). At the same time, and this is the second motion, in amorous dialogue I open up to the other, I welcome him in my loving swoon, or else I absorb him in my exaltation, I identify with him. With those two motions, the premises of ecstasy (of one's going out of oneself) and of incarnation, insofar as it is the ideal becoming body, are set within the amorous incantation of the Song of Songs. But still more immediately, the space of a psychic innerness is sketched out here, inseparable from amorous space. Such innerness remains of course theatrical; it harbors a polyvalent theatricality, if only through its vocal, gestural, and visual fabric, as much as through its verbal one. And yet, on account of the amorous yearning that unifies and totalizes, beyond the irredeemable separation underlined through the topos of flight, love is already the receptacle of inner life. "I am sick with love," the Shulamite sings (5:8), foreshadowing the coming psychological meanderings starting with the mystics and extending to the romantics.

A Song—A Body

Because of its corporeal and sexual thematics ("My Beloved thrust his hand / through the hole in the door; / I trembled to the core of my being"—5:4), indissolubly linked with the dominant theme of absence, yearning to merge, and idealization of the lovers, sensuality in the Song of Songs leads directly to the problematics of incarnation. The loved one is not there, but I experience his body; in a state of amorous incantation I unite with him, sensually and ideally. The allegorical rabbinic interpretation that sees God himself in the loved one actually favors the "incarnational" potentiality of the Song of Songs: how can it indeed be avoided, if I love God, if the loved one is, beyond Solomon's body, God himself? As intersection of corporeal passion and idealization, love is undisputably the privileged experience for the blossoming of metaphor (abstract for concrete, concrete for abstract) as well as incarnation (the spirit becoming flesh, the word-flesh). Unless incarnation is a metaphor that has slipped into the real and has been taken for reality? A hallucination that is assumed to be real on account of the violence of amorous passion, which is in fact the ordinary manifestation of an alienation that confuses the fields of representation (real—imaginary—symbolic)?

Sex and God

It still remains to be determined through what explicit justification and according to what unconscious logic the Song of Songs could find its place in the Bible. How did a literally erotic text become sacred? One can first imagine that, very logically and as if overwhelmed by the amorous metaphoricalness, the rabbis carried the obvious figurative meaning of the amorous text one step further; they idealized it by raising it another notch, and this, of course, was quite a notch since the sublime, regal loved one turns into God having an amorous dialogue with his beloved, the nation of Israel. And yet the erotic meaning of the text could escape no one.[16] Then if the text was inserted among the Scriptures was it perhaps because it filled a gap in the biblical array? While the neighboring nations (Hittites as well as Semites) carried out sexual rites in sacred grove or temple without for that matter comparing themselves to lovers of their gods, the Jews alone had no sexual rites. As ultimate homage to the fertility of the mother-goddess, such rites were necessarily dismissed from a religion of the Father. By introducing the theme of sensuous love the Song of Songs does not for that matter represent a concession to feminine fertility. If it recognizes desire, if it describes its displays and wanderings in voluble and voluptuous fashion, it does so in order to subject them to the Lover's regal authority. As for the Shulamite, she wished indeed to introduce him "into my mother's house" (8:2); she would have preferred him to be brother, familial, and thus loved without constraint. And yet he flees, defying not only the maternal hearth where the power of the mother goddess or of the wife is huddled, but the very sexual merging itself: "On my bed at night, I sought him / whom my heart loves. / I sought but did not find him" (3:1, taken up again in 5:6). Isn't such a defiance, precisely a tension, what brings to view, gives voice to, turns into gesture, into poetry, the strength of desire to which any reader, ancient or modern, is inevitably sensitive, a desire that acts as a powerful motor during the entire course of the sexual, familial adventures of the chosen people?

Considering the erotic component that is disseminated throughout the entire biblical text, as in comparison to the absolute presence of its God, as demanding as he is loving, the Song of Songs is not a foreign element in the Bible—it merely dots the "i's." Desire and God have always been there, our task is now to put them under scrutiny, as such, together, in the folds of individual psychic experience. The word "love" consecrates their being brought together: sensuous and deferred love; body and power; passion and ideal.

The Legitimate Couple

It is probably of prime importance that we are dealing with conjugal love, in order for the possible influences of religious or sexual mentality among neighboring nations to be integrated into the body of biblical writing. Gerschon D. Cohen is doubtless right when he emphasizes this: the love of the couple sanctified by Law is the pillar of Jewish society, and depicting the lovers in the likeness of a devoted married couple, maintained by the husband's jealousy but ensuring security to the wife is undoubtedly a feature of folk manners that legitimates and only thus sanctifies love.[17] "My sister, my promised bride" (3:10) seems to point to the virginity of the beloved rather than a literally incestuous relationship; it will also be noted, concerning that virginity, that at no point is sexual intercourse carried out. Conjugal, exclusive, sensuous, jealous—love in the Song of Songs is indeed all of that at the same time, with in addition the unnamable of carnal union. It should be noted that these particulars set it radically apart from Platonic love (partaking neither of its psychodrama nor its abstract ideality), as well as from the pathetic and enthusiastic mystique of orgiastic love germane to pagan worship, whose illusion of fullness it does not share. At equal remove from both, sealed by law as much as based on a distance, a flight, even an impossibility, love in the Song of Songs turns over a completely new leaf in the experience of western subjectivity. Its enigmatic appeal, its lyrical charm, arise probably in large part out of the wonder found in its psychosocial particularity: its being a legitimation of the impossible, an impossibility set up as amatory law. One would have to look into the sociology and history of the Jewish people for the reasons that allow such an experience of conjugal life—unless one also allows, reversing the order, that it is the presence of a paternal, single, stern, and loving divinity that has finally shaped such a conjugal experience. Unique of its kind, it is the spoken word of a law inscribed in desire, which, beyond foreign influences, is marvelously gathered within this Song of innovating love. Neither philosophical quest nor mystical enthusiasm, biblical love celebrates in fact and for that very reason the shift of religion (which is finally a celebration of the secret of reproduction, the secret of pleasure, of life and of death) to esthetics and morality.

After having seen what kind of love is called forth in the Song of Songs and through what general rhetorical process this takes place, we are better able to understand the meaning of its insertion into the Bible. No other nation, even if dedicated to sacred orgiastic worship, has imagined its relation to God as that of the loving woman to the Husband. Love in the Song of Songs appears to be simultaneously in the framework both of conjugality and of a fulfillment always set in the future (sustained in short by a very feminine, very human, very hysterical "that is never it"?), if not seen as impossible (recognition of amorous alteration as unavoidably missing the other who was barely touched and immediately lost, as on the brink of psychotic hallucination?). One is thus faced with a true dialectical synthesis of amorous experience, including its universally disturbing, pathetic, enthusiastic, or melancholy aspect on the one hand, and its singularly Judaic feature on the other—legislating, unifying, subsuming burning sensuousness toward the One. The One is first heard: the text's emphasis on the ear will be apparent—"I hear my Beloved. / See how he comes . . . / See where he stands / behind our wall" (2:8)—even before the beloved is visible. But also, and very broadly, the Unique is imagined, seen, sensed—witness all the visual, tactile, and olfactory descriptions of the lovers' corporeal qualities—in opposition to the postulate of God's irrepresentableness. God is seen and heard by chosen ones, by lovers, rather feminine lovers; but never merging, never definitively offered for an incarnation that would be accomplished once and for all.

The Song of Songs is thus a crossroads text where one will find the particulars of Jewish religious mentality as well as pagan esthetic influences and the premonitory signs of incarnate religion. One can understand the ancient orthodox rabbis' reluctance to stamp it with their approval. But one cannot help rendering fascinated homage to those men who, beyond what we think were their prejudices, took it into the sacred scriptures, thus realizing one of those exceptional syntheses of the ancient world which do not cease to fill us with wonder. And which, at any rate, had probably deferred the possible splitting off of a new branch of monotheism in the guise of a religion of love, as would happen later with Christianity.

For, thanks to the immediate sexual tenor of the Song of Songs, to which are added the rabbis' allegorical interpretations that assign the erotic meaning to God, the Bible is far from denying any human sexual feature to the Jewish God. But by keeping love under the husband's rule, protecting it from mystical effusiveness by setting up the flight at the center of amorous yearning, the Song of Songs gives Judaism the unique trait of being the most erotic of abstractions, the most ideal of sensualities.

It has been demonstrated that an allegorical exegesis of the Song of Songs cannot be found prior to the destruction of the Second Temple.[18] But doesn't the fact that the book had been placed in the library of the Dead Sea sect prove that it had been studied from a religious standpoint before that?

It is possible to interpret the action of Rabbi Akiba and all those who supported the acceptance of the Song of Songs as sacred text, on the condition that it be given an allegorical reading, not as censoring its amorous or lyrical erotic value but quite the opposite—as an acknowledgement of such implications for the symbolic exegesis that subtends them in order to spiritualize them. Is not symbolic exegesis thereby a simple recognition of the rhetorical infinity—of the metaphorical proliferation—present at the foundations of amorous discourse?

A Wife Speaks

Finally, the Song of Songs is a subtle transcending of Greek or Mesopotamian eroticism and initiatory philosophism through the assertion of woman—the amorous wife. She, the wife, for the first time ever, begins to speak before her king, husband, or God; to submit to him, granted. But as an amorous loved one. It is she who speaks and sets herself up as equal, in her legal, named, unguilty love, to the other's sovereignty. The amorous Shulamite is the first woman to be sovereign before her loved one. Through such hymn to the love of the married couple, Judaism asserts itself as a first liberation of women. By virtue of being subjects: loving and speaking. The Shulamite, by her lyrical, dancing, theatrical language, by an adventure that conjugates a submission to legality and the violence of passion, is the prototype of the modern individual. Without being queen, she is sovereign through her love and the discourse that causes it to be. Limpid, intense, divided, quick, upright, suffering, hoping, the wife—a woman—is the first common individual who, on account of her love, becomes the first Subject in the modern sense of the term. Divided. Sick and yet sovereign. "I am black but lovely, daughters of Jerusalem, / like the tents of Kedar, / like the pavilions of Salmah. . . . / He has taken me to his banquet hall, / and the banner he raises over me is love. / Feed me with raisin cakes, / restore me with apples, / for I am sick with love. / His left arm is under my head, / his right embraces me" (1:5 and 2:4)

And an entire nation saw itself as the Shulamite, chosen by God. This pinnacle of religious sentiment is also its immediate passing over to a freedom that is regulated with erotic passion and rhetorical invention as had never been seen before.
