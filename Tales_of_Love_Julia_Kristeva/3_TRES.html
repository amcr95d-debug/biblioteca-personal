III

Narcissus: The New Insanity

"... the strangeness of his infatuation ..."

(Ovid, Metamorphoses, III, 350)

Only at the beginning of the Christian era did the fable of Narcissus enter the domain of literature. We owe the first complete variant to Ovid (43 B.C.—A.D. 16) who inserted it into the third chapter of his Metamorphoses, which he began in A.D. 2 and completed in A.D. 8 on the eve of his exile to the dour shore of the Black Sea. Conon, Philostratus, and Pausanias, too, each in his own way, tackled the tragic tale of the youth enamored of his own image; but these late renderings do not allow us to reconstitute the original version of the myth.[1] Let us therefore restrict ourselves to Ovid.

This is the outline of the myth. Born at Thespiae, in Beotia, son of the river Cephissus (we shall later encounter the water that held his image) and the nymph Liriope (leirion, the lily, will eventually be metamorphosed, as the myth tells it, into that other flower of moist areas, the funereal narcissus), Narcissus is a youth whose beauty is as dazzling as he is scornful. Spurning youngsters of both sexes alike, Narcissus encounters a prefiguration of his doubling in a watery reflection in the person of the nymph Echo. In love with him but spurned, Echo, who can only repeat the words of others (Juno willed it so in order to punish her for having overly protected the adulterous loves of her father, Saturn), ends up by wasting away, "her body dries and shrivels" and "then she is voice only, for the bones are turned to stone." Finally Narcissus' deluded lovers ask of Nemesis, the goddess of Rhamnus, that he may "love one day, so, himself, and not win over the creature whom he loves." The punishment is carried out when, bent over a spring to quench his thirst during a hunt, the youth is seized with thirst of a different sort: "As he tried to quench his thirst . . . he saw an image in the pool, and fell in love with that unbodied hope, and found a substance in what was only shadow."

We are here confronted with what we can but call the vertigo of a love with no object other than a mirage. Ovid marvels, fascinated and terrified, at the sight of a twin aspect of the lure that will nevertheless continue to nourish the West's psychological and intellectual life for centuries to come. On the one hand there is rapture at the sight of a nonobject, simple product of the eyes' mistake; on the other, there is the power of the image. "What you seek is nowhere. . . . The vision is only shadow, only reflection, lacking any substance. It comes with you, it stays with you, it goes away with you, if you can go away."

One then witnesses an erotic scene between Narcissus and his double, all woven with impossible embraces, missed kisses, deluded contacts. With the eye, the mouth is the main organ of amorous longing, like the skin that is frustrated by "a thin film of water" that keeps them apart.

At last the moment of understanding is at hand. After many frustrations Narcissus gathers that he is actually in a world of "signs": "You nod and beckon when I do; your lips, it seems, answer when I am talking though what you say I cannot hear." The exertion for deciphering leads him to knowledge, to self-knowledge: "He is myself! I feel it, I know my image now."

We have reached the crux of the drama: "What shall I do? . . . What I want is with me, my riches make me poor. If I could only escape from my own body!"

The tragedy reaches a higher level when Narcissus, at the moment when his tears disturb the pool, realizes not only that the loved image is his own, but furthermore that it can disappear—as if he had thought that, for want of touching, he could nevertheless be satisfied with contemplation alone ("let me keep looking at you always"), which has henceforth also become impossible. In desperation he "beat his bare breast with hands as pale as marble"; and so Narcissus dies at the edge of his image, and Ovid adds, "Even in Hell he found a pool to gaze in, watching his image in the Stygian waters." When mourners, whose lamentations Echo repeats, prepare the funereal pile and seek his body, "they found nothing." Through a strange resurrection, the narcissus flower has taken his place.

There has been much emphasis placed on the morbid, narcotic, chthonian meaning of this legend, as of the flower that bears its name. The humid, subterranean torpor of narcissistic space links the fable to the vegetative intoxication of Dionysus; the theme of sight points in the same direction (Narcissus dies after he has seen himself, Pentheus dies for having seen the mysteries of Dionysus), as does even more explicitly the character's genealogy, integrated by Ovid into the Dionysian cycle (Poussin remembered this in his Birth of Bacchus, which pairs the two myths and the two heroes).

But it is perhaps more interesting today to stress the originality of the narcissistic figure and the very particular place it occupies, in the history of Western subjectivity on the one hand, and on the other, taking its morbidity into account, in the scrutiny of the critical symptoms of that subjectivity.

Plotinus, or the Theory of Reflection and Shadow—an Internality

The reflection of which Narcissus became enamored and which led him to his death became the fundamental topos of a thought that parted with ancient philosophy to nourish speculative thinking, including the patristics of the early centuries of the Christian era. The body of hermetic writings as well as the gnostics viewed the perceptible world as the result of a fault one might call narcissistic, since archetypal man is deeply fond of his own reflection, which is nevertheless merely the result of a fall.[2] With Plotinus (A.D. 205-270), to the contrary, the primary reflection that created the cosmos is a necessary process, and it is only the reflection of that reflection in perishable substances that leads us away from the ideal and consequently deserves to be condemned.

This Plotinian double motion is worth examining.

On the one hand, as in the comogonies of his day, the perceptible world is for Plotinus the result of a reflection in a mirror; as if the pernicious effect that is today pejoratively called "narcissistic" were, logically and quite normally, the necessary creator of the world. In short, the soul produces a reflection when it encounters inert matter, just as a body is reflected in a shining surface.[3]

On the other hand and nevertheless, it would be a serious error to take for solid reality what is merely a reflection—as Narcissus does. Narcissism is thus condemned, but that condemnation has no bearing on the origin of the reflection process; according to Plotinus, the error would simply set in at the moment when the individual being grants reality to such images instead of examining his own intimacy.

"Let him who is able to do so advance and heed the inner beauty of this sanctuary, penetrating it intimately: let him close his eyes to the spectacle of terrestrial things, without throwing a backward glance on the bodies whose graces formerly charmed us. If he still sees corporeal beauties, he must no longer rush at them, but, knowing that they are only images, traces, and adumbrations of a superior principle, he will flee from them, to approach that Beauty of whom they are merely the reflections. Whoever would let himself be misled by the pursuit of those vain shadows, mistaking them for realities, would grasp only an image as fugitive as the fluctuating form reflected by the waters, and would resemble that senseless man who, wishing to grasp that image himself, according to the fable, disappeared, carried away by the current. Likewise he who would wish to embrace corporeal beauties, and not release them, would plunge, not his body, but his soul into the gloomy abysses, so repugnant to intelligence; he would be condemned to total blindness; and on this earth, as well as in hell, he would see naught but mendacious shades."[4]

The reference to Narcissus is generally accepted by all commentators. Such a recall of the narcissistic error endows Plotinus' remarks with a meaning that goes beyond that of orthodox Platonism (the Symposium is discussed in Plotinus' treatise Of Beauty) and carries out a more psychological, because more speculative, unfolding of the soul's activities.

Another mythical reference to the mirror, in Plotinus, seems to proceed in the same direction—a condemnation of imageladen dispersion; what is recommended is the reunification of the soul within the ever-present unity of intellect. "Human souls rush down here because they have gazed at their images as in the mirror of Dionysus. Nevertheless, they have not severed links with their principle, intelligence."[5] Let us recall that, according to one version of the myth, Dionysus as a child allows himself to be seduced by Hera by means of a mirror, before undergoing the ordeal of the Titans, who cut him up into pieces that are then put together again by Athena and Zeus, or Demeter, or Apollo . . .[6] The exquisite details of this myth, which was taken up again by Plotinus, thus indeed converge, it seems to me, on the theme of unification through the multiplicity of unessential reflections, which one more directly encounters in Plotinus' handling of the myth of Narcissus.

In this instance the error lies, therefore, in failing to see that the reflection is of none but the self; Narcissus after all is guilty of being unaware of himself as source of the reflection.[7] Let us accept that charge, which indicts Narcissus as being guilty of not knowing himself: he who loves a reflection without knowing that it is his own does not, in fact, know who he is. Let us note, however, that beyond that error, the reflection—of Narcissus—allows Plotinus to hypostatize the activity of reflection up to the complete establishment of consciousness of self, facing which the shadow (the narcissistic one, the one who ignores that its source is the very Self) is a supreme error. Intrigued by Narcissus' reflection, Plotinus' reflection is thus anything but narcissistic. Narcissan if you wish, but violently antinarcissistic, he condemns the veneration of images, beginning with his own. He objects to his disciple Amelius, who asked that he allow a portrait of himself to be drawn, "Is it not enough for me to have to carry around this image in which nature enclosed us? Must I besides transmit to posterity the image of this image as if it were worthy of attention?"[8]

Nevertheless, if the shadow and fetishist images are condemned, sight and reflection—Platonic instruments they might be, but how dramatized, humanized, and eroticized by Narcissus—become with Plotinus logical elements of the elaboration, beyond narcissistic madness, of that western consciousness of self which might have nothing to do with an Ego in love with itself, but whose autoerotic jouissance is nonetheless striking. Let us listen to Plotinus.

The eye having henceforth become "inner" no longer sees "bright objects" but the "beauty of the good soul," provided the following precept is followed:

Withdraw within yourself and look. If you do not yet therein discover beauty, do as the sculptor, who cuts off, polishes, purifies until he has adorned his statue with all the marks of beauty. Remove from your own soul therefore, all that is superfluous, straighten out all that is crooked, cleanse what is obscure and make it resplendent, and do not cease sculpting your own statue until the divine resplendence of virtue shines forth. . . . Have you become thus? Do you see this? Do you purely dwell within yourself, without any obstacle to unity, does nothing foreign anymore, by its submixture, alter the simplicity of your interior essence? . . . you shall then have become light itself, . . . Then must you observe carefully, for yours will be the only eye that is able to perceive supreme Beauty. . . . For the eye will first have to be rendered analogous and similar to the object it is to contemplate. Never would the eye have seen the sun unless first it had assumed its form."[9]

Here one witnesses a masterful synthesis between the Platonic quest for ideal beauty and the autoeroticism of one's own image, which inevitably suggests Narcissus. As if, within the Plotinian plan, narcissistic autoeroticism allowed him first to rehabilitate the activity of the narcissistic process—to be distinguished from its stop: the shadow-image mistaken for an essential reality. By that activity, Platonic dialogism is transformed, with Plotinus, into a monologue that must indeed be called speculative: it leads the ideal inside a Self that, only thus, in the concatenation of reflections, establishes itself as an internality. To the narcissistic shadow, a snare and a downfall, it substitutes autoerotic reflection, which leads ideal Unity inside a Self that is illuminated by it. Narcissus has been transcended, and beauty becomes embodied in the inner space. Would Narcissus then be a suffering body, which, like that of his renowned contemporary, Christ, leads (but afterwards, with others) the idea to become embodied in self-awareness, to become subjective? The suffering of Narcissus nevertheless leaves its mark in the apotheosis of that noetic Whole now become internality of consciousness; as if it assumed the ill-being of the youth before his unreachable reflection, the Plotinian Self, with its individuality and totality united and repelling each other, is aware of having changed: "He has become other, he no longer is himself."[10]

Ulysses unto the Father

It will be no surprise to note that the antithesis of, or rather the taking over from, Narcissus on the journey of his transformation into speculative internality is accomplished through the agency of another mythical figure—Ulysses, who is also on a quest, but not of his own image nor of an inner body. Courageously or cleverly fleeing the watery element and the sirens, neither does he yield to his body, which he knows to be merely a reflection of his soul. Ulysses' soul, that anti-Narcissus, leaves on a quest for the "fatherland," for "it is there that dwells our Father,"[11] in order to discover beyond the body that light of which it is only the reflection, and finally to reach the intellect that reflects primordial light. It has been observed that Paul said nothing else (Heb. 11:13). There thus seems to take shape, with Plotinus, a true hall of mirrors reflecting the luminous Unity, where multiplied reflections are spread out, going from the idea to the body and vice versa. That sparkling actually realizes the complete passage of sight into form, of Narcissus into speculation, of autoeroticism into righteous soul, "We must close the eyes of the body, to open another vision. . . . But how shall we train this inner vision?"[12] And also: ". . . as we do not perceive it, beauty leaves us insensible; but as soon as it penetrates into the soul, it moves us. Now form alone can penetrate into the soul through the eyes."[13]

Love—the Sight of the Invisible

Such internal unity, established by inner sight and whose mythical genesis from Narcissus to Ulysses we have just glimpsed, proceeds through love and the exclusion of the impure. Love is, in short, the soul's sight for invisible things:[14] contemplation triggers emotion, "but it is possible to experience feelings (and the soul does in fact experience them) even for invisible beauties; any soul, so to speak, does so, but especially the most loving."[15] In the final analysis it is love that constitutes the inner unity of the soul: as in the Symposium, but in explicitly autoerotic fashion, the soul is established through loving itself in the ideal. "What are your ecstasies, or your enthusiasms? Whence come your desires to unite yourselves to your real selves, and to refresh yourselves by retirement from your bodies? Such indeed are the experiences of those who love genuinely." It is felt "when one discovers in oneself, or contemplates in others, the greatness of character, the justice of the heart, the pure temperance, etc."[16] At the same time and conversely, the ugly soul is not an internality, it does not have its own space, it is no longer able to see within this unifying circularity because the latter lifts one toward beauty. "It no longer contemplates anything it should, it is no longer allowed to remain solitary, because it is constantly attracted toward external, inferior and gloomy regions. . . . ugliness has been derived from something foreign."[17]

What is defined as a love is the luminous radiation, the gleaming reflection of the One, which the soul watches and loves.[18] The One, however, exerts no effort in order to love or be loved, it is constantly at rest, and only created beings, through a praiseworthy life, can rise up to it and join with it. There is thus nothing in that loving reverberation of the Plotinian One that might recall the generosity of Christian Agape. Nevertheless, the phrase "God is love" can indeed be found in Plotinus, meaning a self-sufficient love that radiates in itself and for itself—a felicitous, dazzling return of Narcissus. The One "is simultaneously the loved one and love; He is love of himself; for He is beautiful only by and in Himself."[19] That autou eros that I see as sublime hypostasis of narcissistic love was to constitute the decisive step in the assumption of inner space, the introspective space of the western psyche. God is Narcissus, and if the narcissistic illusion is for Myself a sin, my ideal is nonetheless Narcissan.

Eye-Image-Woman

Let us imagine the sense-world with every being remaining as it is, without confusion, and yet all together in the unity of the whole . . . as in a transparent sphere external to the spectator, in which one can truly contemplate everything. Preserve the form of this sphere, and without diminishing the size of it, suppress mass, extent, and material conception. Then invoke the divinity that created this sphere of which you have made yourself an image, and pray that he come to you. The Divinity that is simultaneously single and manifold will come, bringing His own world, in the company of the other inferior divinities that exist within Him; each of these is all the others; they all form a single divinity.[20]

Thus is spelled out a divinity that is One and unifying through the dynamics of sight, which is, in fact, delegated to light itself. Neither eye nor image, neither subject nor object, neither understanding nor object of thought; rather a light contemplating itself, the autovision of light.[21] Understanding itself, but also the soul that is established through being enlightened by it, sharing the rounded, soothed softness of a contemplative sphere:

One may be assisted in forming a conception of it by imagining a variegated living sphere, or a composite being made up of many animated and brilliant faces. Or again, imagine pure souls, pure and without fault, and then universal intelligence seated on a summit, and illuminating the whole intelligible region. One might thus contemplate it well, but from the outside, as one sees another; but that is not enough; one has to become Intelligence, and then choose oneself as object of contemplation.[22]

Such an internalizing metaphor of intelligence impelled by love of the good appears distinctly more feminine when compared to Plato's winged Eros.[23] Do not the feminine and the Narcissan, with Plotinus, merge in that image of his early biography, as recalled by Porphyry, which shows the child, already eight years old, still sucking his nurse's breast?[24] The internality illuminated by One good that comes out of it is at any rate gaze and eye, just as the good itself, a soothing haven for narcissistic dichotomies, ecstatic resorption of inside/outside, same/other splittings: "Furthermore, how could he exist? It is as though he rested upon himself, contemplated himself. With him, what corresponds to existence is this contemplation."[25]

It is far from certain that woman be more narcissistic than man, as Freud maintained. But for a woman to be able to redirect the insatiable craving for a fine distinctive image toward her inner bosom or, in more psychological terms, her inner solitude, in the exquisite pain of contemplation, daydreaming, or even hallucination—that amounts to a true resolution of narcissism that is not at all erotic (in the Greek sense of the word) but is, quietly or fanatically, wholly amorous. Dissipation of the glance within itself, fusion and permutation of the one and the other, neither seeing nor seen, neither subject nor object; love in the feminine, against which mystical experiences stumble, coils up around the mother-child's tight embrace, the blurr of images prior to the "mirror stage." A swallowing up of the imagination by the real, the emergence of the imagination under the aegis of the symbolic, the beginning and absolute of the ideal—this feminine facet of love is perhaps the most subtle sublimation of the secret, psychotic ground of hysteria . . .

If it be true that in Narcissus' universe there is no other, one might nevertheless think of the spring as his partner. A symbol of the maternal body, is it not after a fashion penetrated by the youth who thrusts his image into it? It still needs to be said that such a possession, and one must call it wholly imaginary, holds only nothingness in store for the other, particularly the other sex. Simply a medium, the spring merely engulfs and ruins the one who ventures into it. In this autarkic love, which leads to union with a divinity that is itself autarkic, there is no other but nothingness. The Narcissan Plotinian divinity is love, but it is a love of self and in itself. The one who constitutes himself through it creates himself in and for himself. Not for the Other. To be or to vanish, to integrate the other in bedazzlement . . . Augustine (354-430) on the road to Tagaste or in his garden, enlightened by revelation, is a reader of Plotinus.

Alone with Him Who Is Alone

The soul having become, so to speak, a pure gaze toward the One, toward understanding and the good, divests itself of the body and, in the dynamics of this amorous ascent, creates the space of western internality. The untranslatable Plotinian phrase MONOS PROS MONON, which is rendered [in English as "the one to the one" is interpreted in French] as seul à seul, "alone with someone" [or, in the present context], "alone with him who is alone." Although it is already found with a slightly different meaning in rituals originating in Egypt as well as with neo-Pythagorians, with Plotinus it sums up the existence of a solitude that is opened up, for it is directed to an ascent toward the One.[26] Out of the same (MONOS) Plotinus creates another same (MONON, the neuter form). He creates a split but harmonized unity that is symbolized by hands clasped in prayer. Before becoming an invocation, a request, or an entreaty, this new subjective posture is, according to Plotinus, effective in its display alone, in its distinctive topography, simply showing the relation of self to self through the intermediary of the One. The subject thus takes itself for the object in an amorous splitting that is nevertheless superseded by the mediation of the ascent toward the good. "One must still ascend to the Good to which every soul aspires . . . until, having relinquished everything that is foreign to God, alone with Him who is alone, one beholds Him in all His simplicity and purity, Him from whom all depends, to whom all aspires, from whom everything draws its existence, life, and thought."[27] Let me note finally that The Enneads close with an apology of solitude oriented toward the One, as by an assumption of narcissism: "[the soul] then does not aspire to anything different from itself, but penetrates into itself. As soon, however, as it is within itself alone it necessarily resides in Him . . . Detached from all things here below, scornful of all earthly pleasures, the soul has taken flight toward Him alone."[28] As a pause within this apotheosis of contemplative solitude, let us read a passage that is not far from the conclusion of the Enneads, keeping in mind Narcissus' tragic adventure. The dreary chthonian flower has not been forgotten: it has been, so to speak, assumed, displaced, subsumed within the experience that is no longer narcissistic but inner.

We withdraw into ourselves, and there is no part of ourselves that is not in contact with Him. Then the soul can see Him and see itself, so far as it is possible to have such contemplations. One sees oneself shining brilliantly, filled with intelligible light; or rather, one becomes pure light, a subtle and weightless being. One becomes divinity, or rather one is divinity, a shining, amorous light. Until one falls back under such weight, and the flower withers.[29]

Without Object—a Melancholy Being

It might seem excessive to grant Narcissus such a crucial part in the history of western subjectivity, hypostatizing the function of reflection and, on the basis of its narcissistic failure, setting in motion the internalization of reflection in order to transform Platonic ideality into speculative internality. If it be true that Plotinus' texts authorize us to do so, other mythical, philosophical, or historical trends lead equally and powerfully in that direction. Narcissus, however, appeals to us, and he is essential as a source of western subjectivism—and not only so because of his explicit or allusive presence in The Enneads. The banality of his person (there is nothing heroic about the youth from Thespiae) as well as the insanity of his adventure (Ovid speaks of a novitas furoris, a new insanity) turn him into a borderline case indeed, but also a common one. Neither Dionysus nor Christ but tragic and immortal through floral metamorphosis, that lover of himself is strangely close to us in his everyday childishness. And yet he makes us uncomfortable, giving off a subtle uneasiness, a cold, sticky discomfort. As if at the start of a new era, the Christian era that led us to assume our humanity through the imposing suffering of Christ, insinuated in parallel fashion, not on the sacrificial heights of Calvary but in the dank, swampy, wastelands of human experience, that internality, that Psyche turned into psychicism, had a price—a new insanity. Human, all too human.

In short, there is infantilism and perversion on the brim of western internality. The pejorative meaning of such terms—from the standpoint of ideated Unity—in no way rules out their constituent, although displaced, enhanced, "sublimated" presence at the foundations of the psyche. Finally let us not forget what the twists and turns of narcissistic adventures have perhaps caused us to lose sight of love; it makes up the framework of the Narcissan adventure, its cause and its aim. Eros, amor confess to having no other object than their own image, a reflection of their own body, an idealized portion that serves for the whole. For eroticism to be autoeroticism, in the watery element of an archaic forest, amounts to a demystification, a lowering of the sublimity that characterizes the amorous state. On the other hand, if the Narcissan adventure is perceived by Ovid as insane, it is not so in the sense of a sexual frenzy, as experienced by Dionysus or the bacchantes. Here, insanity comes from the absence of object, which is, in the final analysis, the sexual object. What is the object for? It serves to give sexual existence to anguish. Narcissus does not reach that point. He lives in another dimension. Not secured to an object, his anguish returns, and when he realizes through that rebound that the other in the spring is merely himself, he has put together a psychic space—he has become subject. Subject of what? Subject of the reflection and at the same time subject of death. Narcissus is not located in the objectal or sexual dimension. He does not love youths of either sex, he loves neither men nor women. He Loves, he loves Himself—active and passive, subject and object. Actually, Narcissus is not completely without object. The object of Narcissus is psychic space; it is representation itself, fantasy. But he does not know it, and he dies. If he knew it he would be an intellectual, a creator of speculative fictions, an artist, writer, psychologist, psychoanalyst. He would be Plotinus or Freud.

Nineteenth-century German psychiatry has retained the perverse aspect of Narcissus' myth: the love of the subject for his own body. That is indeed how P. Nacke puts it to use in 1899. And yet, not only is perversion an undefinable notion on account of its numerous paradoxes; not only does it seem, when you come right down to it, necessarily and universally germane to the neoteinic person's sexuality; but furthermore, and this is what the fable narrated by Ovid has the advantage of highlighting, it is far from certain that the narcissistic "perversion" is borne by sexual desire. There is nothing here to remind one of the erotic mania of Phaedrus or Alcibiades. Would the Narcissan "Amor" which initiates the speculative space of psychic, ideal, and contemplative internality, be borne by another force? Which one? Melancholy? Death drive? A term like "negativity" or "death drive" might indeed be better suited to describe that uneasy splitting, that kaleidoscopic and eventually muddled identity—in short, that death of the youth smitten with his image. A death that nothing justifies, unless it had been . . . always already there. Perverse Narcissus or psychotic Narcissus? Novitasque furoris, Ovid writes, and we are ready to believe him. It is a new insanity, soothed and worked-through against the background of a wholeness ideal that postmythical thought ended up by imposing on a henceforth Christian West.

Must one insist on the price (probably very much due to Plotinus' own story, but which does not, for all that, lose its symptom value) that is paid for this luminous, reflective closure of psychic, autoerotic space under the constituent eye of the One? "Plotinus was subject to chronic digestive disorders," Porphyry disclosed at the very outset of his Life of Plotinus, "nevertheless he never was willing to take any remedies. . . . He never bathed, contenting himself with daily massage. . . . But when at the time of the plague . . . the man who rubbed him died of it, he gave up the massage. This interruption in his habit brought on him a chronic quinsy" (p. 6). And at the time of breathing his last, the man who lived while ignoring his body in order to build, out of his suffering, the psychic space that we cannot be sure we have departed from as yet, avers, "I am trying to unite what is divine in us to what is divine in the universe."[30] A melancholy, pathetic individual, Plotinus did not seek an object to halt his anguish. He lashed himself down to the archetype or rather to the source of objecthood—image, reflection, representation, speculation. By totalizing them, by unifying them within the inner space of the Self, "alone with him who is alone," causing Platonism to topple over into subjectivity. By deferring or waiting for incarnation. Its task would be simply to rehabilitate bodies, inevitably suffering, and images, nevertheless inferior. The Christly passion and the arts of all Churches have come to root into the history of created beings, of sons, that psychic space of love that the Narcissan myth and the neo-Platonic logos had actually just completed.

Confronting this new narcissistic fury, very different and nevertheless related thought systems were contending for ideological domination of the ancient world: Christianity, gnosticism, and neo-Platonism. They offered divergent solutions, but the problem to which thinkers had to respond was identical: it involved the emotional disarray of a society whose center no longer was the city (polis), but which was becoming a civilized universe (oikoumene) that, while cosmopolitan, gave human beings over to what H.-Ch. Puech calls "ineffable solitude."[31] Platonism, struggling against gnosticism,[32] was thus not unaware of Christianity. But in autonomous fashion it accomplished the culmination of Hellenistic thought by effecting the metamorphosis of myth into logos, of fiction into philosophy. It was an epistemological trend toward internalization in which what is represented (mythical images) topples over into the discourse of the representing Sage. Needing no Savior, Messenger, or Intercessor, not postulating the necessity of a real salvation, the Plotinian universe, while remaining hierarchical, effected a presence as well as a permanent implication of the superior within the inferior and vice versa. Such a mystique of immanence within a metaphysics of transcendence[33] relied essentially, as has been noted here, on the dynamics of reflection in both the concrete and abstract meanings of the term. How can one avoid deciphering a Narcissan trend inscribed in the very dialectic of the idea that generates the internality of a new man in Plotinian reflections such as the following: the One "is completely inclined toward Himself and is immanent in Himself";[34] "What is outside Himself is also Himself, for He is the comprehension of all things, and their measure. Rather, He is the internal, being the most intimate depth of all things";[35] and finally, "The wise man is imbued with reason, and in himself possesses what he manifests to others; he contemplates himself; he not only tends toward unity and isolates himself from external objects, but finds all things within himself."[36] The tragic, death-bringing solitude of Narcissus has henceforth become, in the ideational dialectic of daring separations and reciprocal reflections, an apology of being "alone with him who is alone," the MONOS PROS MONON, on which will be founded a man very different from the political and erotic animal of the ancient world. Leaving politics to its laws, Narcissus has become a Sage and he opens up the city to speculation. The soul ceases being a goddess in order to reflect itself as a psyche, as an internality proper to each individual solitude.

Let us point out that if Narcissus' myth does indeed appear to provide one of the most spectacular elements of that reflection theory, the latter may also be found elsewhere and probably makes up the general web that sustains other mythical representations. In order to conciliate the infinite and the limit the thinking of the period often conjured up the theme of the leaning (parekupsen) man (Poimandres) or woman (Sophia). Like a "woman leaning out the window" or a "goddess leaning out the window," the mythical being gazes at himself below as if in the surface of the water and longs to meet that image. Creation and fall at the same time, that gnostic image represents another use of the reflection theme. Finally, the apocryphal acts of the apostles (Acts of John, 26-29; Acts of Andrew, 5-6) evoke knowledge through the mirror and set it against face-to-face knowledge, the immediate vision of the self by the self being considered superior to the recognition of a simple illusory reflection.[37]

A Loss of/into the Other—Ecstasy

Finally, how can one avoid seeing in the Narcissus myth as well as in the speculative doctrines of the time (gnosticism, neo-Platonism, Christianity) an impossible, tragic, painful attempt to tackle a problem that the ancient world had not solved—otherness, to eterotes? What difference is there, with Plotinus, between the human soul that has emerged from the cycle of incarnations and divine souls? Is there a difference in strength and function, but not in quality?[38] The problem is not stated, or at any rate not in such terms. If, however, for gnostics, the Creator's other can only belong to the realm of evil, the Plotinian other does not, no more than it is numerically or qualitatively different: it is undifferentiated motion toward nonbeing. To eterotes would in short only be desire for nothingness. But if everything that is not the One is endowed with such otherness and thus such a yearning for nothingness, the otherness disappears when we merge with the One. The blending-love with the One is elimination of otherness. The soul's merging with the One is described by Plotinus in visual terms: "seeing oneself whole" (Enneads V, 5, 10) rather than "being whole." The soul then loses is specificity, it is no longer completely itself, it is outside itself, in extasis. It has pushed aside otherness in order to reach, beyond the grasp of otherness, the steadiness of rest. Beyond the fear that such a motion arouses, the soul devotes itself to it for it is destined for such union—as our eyes await the sunrise. Now it is precisely the distinctive feature of the loving nous (as opposed to the thinking nous) that it effects the merging that is loss of otherness.[39] One may well wonder at the inherent contradictions in Plotinus' theory of otherness. How can one indeed reconcile the following propositions: the One is transcendent, otherness is the first product of the One as a separating motion from himself, and the soul becomes united with the One in mysticism? Doesn't the imbroglio take place because the sun is taken as a model for an incorporeal universe? Otherwise one would have to abandon the idea of the other as tendency toward nothingness, viewing the other as a simple finite being—and that is a step Plotinus did not take.

Actually, everything becomes coherent again if one remembers that the sun is intended not to represent the laws that bear on physical bodies but to provide a basis for the theory of reflection. The other, that is, the eye, sees only because it reflects the light of a single source. The loving soul must therefore give up its otherness and give in to the sameness of the single light where it loses itself as other, in other words, for Plotinus, as nonbeing. Narcissus' error would lie in his having reversed the perspective: he mistook his eye for the solar source, for the One, and believed there could be an other for that other. Plotinus knew that the soul is always already an other, but it can emerge from its solitude, its nothingness, its possible downfall, by means of an amorous return to the Single Source. After having been barely sketched out, the soul's otherness is thus reintegrated into the mystical, initiatory path of nondualistic neoplatonism.

As to the mythical Narcissus, he is a modern character much closer to us. He breaks with the ancient world because he turns sight into origin and seeks the other opposite himself, as product of his own sight. He then discovers that the reflection is no other but represents himself, that the other is the presentation of the self. Thus, in his own way, Narcissus discovers in sorrow and death the alienation that is the constituent of his own image. Deprived of the One, he has no salvation; otherness has opened up within himself. He no longer has the thinking nous of the ancient world that could have enabled him to approach the other as plurality, as a multiplicity of objects or parts. He no longer has the Plotinian loving nous that could have led him to escape his otherness through a merging with the One. If he is alone with him who is alone, his otherness is not completed within totality, it does not become internality. It remains open, gaping, mortal, because deprived of a One.

Is it then by chance that the images of psychological or esthetic Narcissi accompany the crises of salvation religions, and are a compelling presence in our contemporary world, shaken by the death of the One God?

Our Faith: The Seeming

Freud in the Realm of Narcissus

What has been going on during the two thousand years Narcissus has been with us?

At the beginning of the Christian era, when he showed up as an antidote to Eros, he was also the link, an unknown, underhanded one, between the ancient, mythical world, and the new world of the incarnation. Indeed, Narcissus was no longer a god of love whose power is gauged according to his effects on others; he was himself in love and, immediately, in love with an illusion. Narcissus, or love of man as impossible love. Narcissus or passionate love. Neoplatonism's bid for power and, as we shall see later, that of Christianity, particularly with Thomas Aquinas, involved taking up again the reflexive, specular, speculative, and intimist Narcissian dynamics, while at the same time considering the narcissistic event—his love—as a mistake in viewpoint, so to speak. If Narcissus had known that he was not the source of the illusion, but that he himself was already a reflection of essential Unity, he could have loved himself with impunity, hence loved his image, within the orbit of essential, divine unity beyond which one cannot go; for that is the auspicious space of specularity, and of amorous speculation as well. Condemned, only in part, by Plotinus, basically rehabilitated in its introspective construction, narcissism was openly restored by incarnate religion.

"You must love your neighbor as yourself" (Mark 12:31) is what the Bible had already said. "If anyone wants to boast, let him boast about the Lord" (1 Cor. 1:31). Henceforth, love of self is an error only to the extent that one forgets one is the reflection of the Other (the Lord). Knowing it does not erase the illusion, it rehabilitates it by inserting it into the dialectic of the ascent toward the Other. Is not the religion of salvation, from this point of view, salvatory precisely and inasmuch as it creates a space of love that takes into account the illusion, the seeming, and the impossible settled at the very heart of supreme reality—at the heart of the relationship with the Loved One, and therefore at the heart of simple reality, which is defined only through belonging to the One?

On Death's Way

When Freud took the word narcissism back from the realm of psychiatry, Narcissus again and more than ever caused discomfort; Narcissus had become a perverse symptom. The tremendous introspective elaboration, of which Plotinus or Thomas Aquinas only represent eminent summits, had become dismantled, and a sickly, illusory longing on the part of a Self deprived of an Other is all that remains of the speculative journey aiming at the creation of a subjective internality. Throughout his discussions with Jung, Freud in his turn and surreptitiously rehabilitated narcissism. Not only in the beginning was there self-love, he said substantially, but the Ego Ideal that emerges out of any love relationship is taken over from primary narcissism, only thus reconciled with parental and social demands. What is more, as if he were (unconsciously?) faithful to the Plotinian or Thomist thought process, Freud inscribed narcissism—which for him is basically and absolutely libidinal—within the dependency of a "new psychic action," which turns out to be an archaic instance of symbolic fatherhood as magnet for primary identifications and requirement for the Ego Ideal. Having thus recognized the original, libidinal foundation of narcissistic seeming, Freud, in the same stroke, strengthened his conception of the libido and the Ego by defining them as dependent, beyond autoeroticism, on what Lacan calls the Other. In so doing, and while strongly emphasizing the dynamic value of transferential or love relationship, he already set a limit to its power. Narcissus is both prime mover and barrier for love.

With Beyond the Pleasure Principle, greater light was thrown on the narcissistic libido—and dramatically so: ego drives also include death drives. Narcissus in love hides the suicidal Narcissus; the most urgent of all drives is death drive. Left to itself, without the assistance of projection upon the other, the Ego takes itself for a preferential target of aggression and murder.

With Freud, rehabilitation of narcissism did not therefore lead to a promise of salvation but to the discovery of death's work. A personal fantasy? The echo of a tragic era? The fact remains that the conclusion of the twentieth century, which began with the fulfillment of Freud's work and two world wars, hands down to us an unbearable amorous space. Freud's humanistic culture made him heir to Christian spirituality and symbolism, and he attempted to take over where the "religion of salvation" left off, even borrowing from it his "talking cure." In an ultimate spurt of compromise with and distanciation from the spiritualist and psychiatric legacy of his time (from Nacke to Jung), Freud relied on Narcissus, first in order to rehabilitate him by including him in his libidinal apparatus—Eros is initially narcissistic; then to emphasize, in a very biblical fashion this time and worsening the unease already present in On Narcissism: An Introduction, that love is no more than a chancy statis of hatred. Thus, in the Papers on Metapsychology, he specified,

Love is derived from the capacity of the ego to satisfy some of its instinctual impulses auto-erotically by obtaining organ-pleasure. It is originally narcissistic, then passes over on to objects, which have been incorporated into the extended ego, and expresses the motor efforts of the ego towards these objects as sources of pleasure.[1]

But,

Hate, as a relation to objects, is older than love. It derives from the narcissistic ego's primordial repudiation of the external world with its outpouring of stimuli. . . . it always remains in an intimate relation with the self-preservative instincts; so that sexual and ego-instincts can readily develop an antithesis which repeats that of love and hate.[2]

If it is true that any autonomy of the Ego is defined by opposition to its other, its object, and opposition implies hatred—in that case the Ego, drastically, is an Ego of hatred. Let us make this clear: if love takes over from narcissism in ulterior sexual drives, it is underlain, borne, and determined by hatred. Freud stops on the brink of Sade. But his timorousness is only apparent, for where Sadian love is set forth triumphantly, with and against the Revolution or the Pope, Freudian love—transference—holds to a wager lucidly laid beyond hatred and death: it is the amorous transference that produces the dynamic effects of the cure. Freudian dualism finds its strongest expression in this impossible harmonization of amorous space, in this fractured space of lovehate. Love is a necessary seeming, which is to be restored, aroused, promoted endlessly. This, in order to analyze it, in other words to dissolve it down to its framework, its carrier wave—which is hatred. The new world is one of lovehate. Those who face up to it are neither believers, nor idolatrous, nor followers, nor faithful, nor disappointed. Alone with him who is alone, monos pro monon, Narcissus knows one cannot go beyond narcissism, but without fretting over it he builds temporary, gossamer, limpid loves. If he goes through passions or twilights, he is neither dramaticoromantic, nor feverishly pornographic, nor unhappily disappointed. Disenchanted but not depressed, Narcissus no longer takes himself, since Freud, for a sin or a sublime value, but rather for that infinitely distant boundary marker on the basis of which an immediately symbolic sensuality attempts to take shape. Like an image simultaneously composed and decomposed on videotape, love is only for the time being and forever. End of love as religion? Reintegration, banalization of esthetic love?

The Fake Is Necessary

Narcissus kills himself because he realizes that he loves a fake. The moral condemnation that concludes the Ovidian myth thus reveals the concomitance of narcissism and the fake. That aspect, the only one explicitly taken up and condemned by medieval Christianity, has deep roots on which Freud's reexamination of the problem finally enables one to shed light. If, as we have seen, one is not born a Narcissus (one is born autoerotic), and one becomes Narcissus only following the impact of a paternal identification subsequently producing the Ego Ideal, where does the fake come from? The fake would arise because one only rarely succeeds in identifying fully with that ideal; either it does not hold, or it is demolished, or else Narcissus, with his mother's help, believes he does not need it because he already is it (an ideal for his mother). Then, instead of having to create what will enable him to equal his ideal—a work, or an idealized object to love—Narcissus will fabricate an ersatz.[3] To do so he will cathect, instead of the paternal Phallus, such-and-such pre-Oedipal preobject (gaze, orality, anality, etc.). Anal eroticism of narcissistic fakeness goes hand in hand with the fetishization of one's own image (to the extent that the latter is a fake ideal) and unveils the moment of erotic passivation facing the Father, to whom Narcissus is fixated. That moment is probably necessary for any idealization, provided it is introjected in the Oedipal structure through displacement of incorporate elements; with Narcissus, however, it remains a stumbling block. If this suggests homosexual potentialities in Narcissus, it should not obscure the consequences of such an oral-anal fixation for his use of language. Precisely, he will oralize it, but he will also endow it with the entire secret and intense weight of anal modeling in order to cleanse it of abstract ideality and load it with the jubilatory latencies of an archaic, maternal tongue. Echolalic, vocalizing, lilting, gestural, muscular, rhythmical. The narcissistic seeming thus necessarily entails a modification of language. There is enough there to establish premises for all of art.

"Authentic" or "fabricated," art in any case includes its narcissistic moment, its necessary share of seeming, of fakeness if you will, with which it challenges the universe of established values, pokes fun at them, and appeals to us by means of a bonus of ease and pleasure. It manages to have us love it . . .

Narcissus as Counterfeiter: Man and Woman

The Middle Ages take up again the theme of Narcissus in order to condemn, essentially, what it contributes to the baneful human bent toward the seeming falsehood, and even fraud, which accompany self-love, at the expense of love for true divine values.[4] Thus, for the troubadours, "The example of Narcissus is a warning against both pride and the follies of love"[5] while in Ovide moralisé (1316; "Ovid Moralized") Narcissus stands out as emblematic of worldly conceits, seeming, and pride. In Jean de Meung's Romance of the Rose the fountain of Narcissus is a site of Death, facade, and false knowledge, to which are contracted the running waters of truth.

Let me emphasize Dante's handling of the medieval interpretation of Narcissus, one that is as elliptical as it is masterful. The straightforward evocation of "the mirror of Narcissus" occurs in canto XXX, verse 128. It has been accurately noted that the context for this canto is that of forgers, counterfeiters, and falsifiers.[6] Involved indeed are Master Adam, who counterfeited the Florentine currency, and "false Sinon, the Greek from Troy" who is said to have induced the Trojans to let into their city the wooden horse conceived by Ulysses. To undermine the currency—symbol not only of political and also moral value—is to become a loathsome being: Master Adam's physical deformity is proof as well as punishment. He suffers from dropsy, a disease characterized by the retention of fluids in parts of the body, which swell disproportionately while others become atrophied ("The heavy dropsy, disproportioning him thus with humors badly absorbed, left his head too small for his belly, and made him hold his lips apart as the hectic does who, for thirst, has one turned up, the other down," v. 52-57). Now it is precisely this Adam, holding his stagnant well within himself, who utters Narcissus' name—the one and only time it is mentioned in the Divine Comedy. The allusion seems clear: not only is monetary fraud simply seen as one of the revelations of Adam's original sin (note the homonymy between the counterfeiter and the first man), but also and at the same time (Adam's) original sin is shown to be related to Narcissus' lapse. Indeed, just as Narcissus becomes enamored of a sham, Master Adam alters the true currency, which symbolizes a law and an ethics that transcend him; by thus wishing to usurp the Prince's place and God's as well he harms the flow of goods in the city and of fluids in his own body. Lacking ethics (Dante mischievously plays on the two meanings of etico—ethical and hectic, in this instance a drying out of the brain), he cannot find a spring to quench his thirst: water stagnates in his belly while his mouth thirsts. At the same time he has lost his landmarks; he cannot see, his eyes have been walled up "and the foul water . . . makes a hedge before your eyes," (v. 122-23).

Sinon, a character invented by Vergil, who posed as a deserter from the Greek camp in order to deceive the Trojans, fares no better: he has a raging fever and emits foul-smelling fumes. He, too, is thirsting (for truth? deprived of true, running water?), he is described by Master Adam as eager to "lick the mirror of Narcissus" (v. 128). The two falsifiers thus come back at each other with images of thirst and blindness, which indeed appear to be metamorphoses of Narcissus' myth; they could also be seen as variants that condense original sin with that of narcissism and strengthen the condemnation of both. "But because fraud is peculiar to man it displeases God more" (Inferno, canto XI, v. 25).

Nevertheless, to my knowledge, commentators do not seem to have noticed the presence of a third forger (the first, in fact, in the order of appearance in canto XXX): it is a woman, "wicked Myrrha," "who was devoted to her father beyond the bonds of lawful love. She came thus to sin with him, changing herself into another's form" (v. 38-41). The appearance of daughter-father incest at the outset of the canto touching on narcissistic falsehood causes perhaps one of its sexual meanings to be unveiled, one that the forgers of false values or mischievous betrayals do not clarify. In short, to borrow a false form is to acquire the advantage of evading the law, which is in the first place that of the forbidden father. Daughter-father incest, like the sin of Narcissus, is an infringement of upright love. The creature-daughter muddles up creation's hierarchy by sinning with her creator; Narcissus ignores that hierarchy by loving a false creation, his own image, which is not a soul created by God. Wicked Myrrha may remind one of gnostic Sophia, who wants to know her Father, the Infinite, and coincide with him; attempting that impossibility would be her undoing if God did not stop her, thanks to Horos, the "limit." It will be noted that if Myrrha, like Sinon and Master Adam for their part, offends God through "incontinence, malice, and mad bestiality" (canto XI, v. 82), it is the daughter-father incest that bears the burden of embodying the sexual aspect of this infernal sin that causes a catastrophe in circulation, exchange, and reflection. A defect in thinking ("Fraud which hurts man's conscience," canto XI, v. 52), in one's own image, in the identity of the body and its parts, original fraud is also, perhaps above all, an unsettling of incest prohibition. Moreover, we know how powerfully Dante sublimates the son-mother incest by endowing Beatrice with the attributes of spiritual Motherhood, furthermore declaring the Virgin mother to be "her son's daughter." There is apparently nothing similar in the daughter-father relationship, for with the father we most obviously reach the ultimate point of the specular and speculative structure, the stability and unicity of which (as may be seen in Plotinus and Thomas Aquinas) were necessarily to guarantee the very existence of psychic as well as speculative internality.

In other respects, the falseness of this villainous Myrrha is also probably due to this, which can be deciphered in the narrative Ovid devotes to her: the true passional object of her love and her hatred as well appears to be her own mother. "O mother, mother, happy in your husband!" Myrrha exclaims in her nurse's presence, and she misleads her father by coming to him disguised as another young woman at the very moment her own mother celebrated the yearly festival for Ceres. Would the daughter's incestuous love for the father be "false," besides, because it concealed a yet more intense and shameful passion for the mother herself? Transformed into a tree whose "tears are of great worth," myrrh, Myrrha leaves us with a fascinating image of childbirth—a tree that gives birth to Adonis, the seducer of Venus. The son, Adonis, thus avenges himself for the passion instilled in his mother, but in fact he fulfills, as his mother's surrogate, Myrrha's true love for the mother goddess.[7]

A Solar Anti-Narcissus

Nevertheless, there may be an error—the poet's error—that is "the opposite error from that which kindled love between the man and the spring" (Paradise, canto III, v. 17-18). If it is "opposite," this in no way implies that it is not an "error."[8] The error lies in having visions—seeing forms, substances, sights where there is nothing but the transparence of the sun: "So I saw several faces ready to speak" (v. 16). As if he were warned by the narcissistic error that mistakes reflections for an essential reality, the poet immediately passes judgment on himself and turns his eyes away. Now this turning away, this disavowal of the images, this rushing toward dazzling light is condemned by Beatrice; she considers it a "childish thought," and points out that what the poet believed were delusions are "true substances whom God gratifies with "the light of the Truth" in order to guide them (v. 25-31). These visions, therefore, these shadows the poet thought were deceitful, are a reality. The poetic error, as can be seen, is the opposite of Narcissus', who thought that the shadow was a reality. Nevertheless, it is also symmetrical, for in both instances what is involved is not seeing the other as he is and having the mind surge back upon itself—and this also entails the disappearance of the image's specific reality. Hence, without a strong object, the poetic cast of mind would amount to turning objects into the subject's creation—to mistaking reality for a ghost created by the author. Dante's quest in his Paradise consists in rising to the sight of true light. Nonetheless, within this straight ascent, Dante the poet is snatched up by the light's becoming-substance, by thickenings of the transparency, by its seemings that are less visible than "a pearl on a white forehead" (v. 13). A judging mind, he rejects them as "narcissistic." In love with Beatrice he is led, nevertheless, to rehabilitate them. Here we are witnessing a subtle analysis of the poetic condition: fascinated by images on the one hand, in quest of truth on the other. Or perhaps at the same time? Indeed, the simultaneity of the two motions is what insures a true way out of the narcissistic delusion; one must not become blinded by images for the sake of an immediate vision of truth, but one should recognize them as such, as reflections of a spiritual adventure constituted by rising thoughts—an adventure that exceeds them.

The Anti-Narcissus is eventually located beyond Narcissus and not on the near side of him. Faithful to Plotinian and Thomistic tradition, Dante subsumes the "seemings" as necessary landmarks within the journey to Paradise. Luminous bedazzlement remains the supreme vision ("As the sun hides itself with too much light . . ."; Paradise, canto V, v. 133); the lightning that causes reason and sight to falter is precisely that ultimate love "which turns the sun and the other stars" (canto XXXIII, v. 145). But on that path, "I, a mortal, feel this lopsidedness in me" (mi sento in questa disagguaglianza, canto XV, v. 83), and it will be noted that only the feminine—maternal—presence of Beatrice allows him, in canto III, to overcome the error that is the opposite of Narcissus' and to invest the seemings of bodies, the seeming bodies in the quest for truth . . . In a lopsided, limping fashion.

Is the poet Narcissus' defeat? Or rather, his relief: a rehabilitation of the seeming, provided it is located, displaced, in the bosom of the love between the poet and his mistress, a spiritual mother who guides him toward the other?

In love with himself without knowing it, a victim of his sight that appears to him as creating the world, the poet tends to mistake the world for a show—arbitrary, false, liable to be manipulated by himself. Out of uprightness, however, he turns away from it and, to punish himself, closes his eyes. Beatrice alone allows him to accept an Other as the source of his visions, which are thus the realities of the Other and not "poetic" or "childish" delusions.

Narcissus as Child

"You know the answer, you hold the key, / Love's tender secret, share it with me"[9] Cherubino sings desperately, melancholically, irresistibly in Mozart's Le Nozze di Figaro, and his nostalgia, at the same time deeply fulfilled and forever inconsolable, probably is the best expression of what Narcissus would have said had he been able to sing. Had he known how to give himself the means, as an exemplary Christian, to embody eroticism within music.[10] His slender, inflaming, passion-taut song is not an invocation to another; he nourishes himself with his own elevation, his "levitation." Coiled up around its sighs, the heart doesn't know if it wants an other; it sings of itself, it sings. If it were love, there should have been an object. But the countess, simplified figure of the good (or is it the bad?) mother, is not an entity truly apart from what is indeed the narcissistic passion of the naive youngster, unaware of differences (child/mother, day/night, man/woman). Nevertheless, all that matters little to Mozart, who was able to find a voice that could make sublime passion, enamored of its own velvet lining, surge out of narcissistic rage. As a myth, Narcissus is tragical. As a song, he is Cherubino. Revealed religion and feudal society, some aspects of which I shall take up later (see Four and the first section of Six), have not only removed into, if not an enticing person, at least a delightful one. Angelical.

The pleasure principle thus inserts itself into the religion of salvation.

Delightful Narcissus, Symbolist or "Watery Prowler"

The more or less underhanded claim of narcissistic experience as necessary foundation for art, as creation of semblances and yet as unique access road to truth, torn between pleasure and death, is a constant one throughout the centuries. But credit is due to Paul Valéry for having claimed without a complex the "delights" of the "inexhaustible I" (Narcissus Speaks, Fragments of Narcissus, Narcissus' Cantata). There is no trace left of the slightest shame or condemnation: scription, which knows and wants itself to be sign and symbol, claims Narcissus' divided image and draws its inspiration from that exquisite separation within the speaking being that "slips in between the two of us the steel that cuts a fruit" (Fragments). Narcissus is thus henceforth, for contemporaries, "a sweet fragrance with a mellow heart" (Narcissus Speaks). The tragedy of the youngster enamored of his image appears as the very fount of self-knowledge: "Up to this delightful moment I was unknown to myself / And I did not know how to treasure and reach myself!" And the poet, who confesses to be less perfect than "the short-lived immortal," discovers an inexhaustible "treasure of helplessness and pride," which bears out his own position of admitting of no other essence than that of the Self. "I am interested only in my sole essence; . . . Everything else is mere absence." A hymn to the death of God. This praise of Narcissus becomes the psychological result of the speculative position: "I am alone. I am myself. I am true . . . I hate you" (Cantata). And also: "To my scorn of the gods, could anything be changed? I love what I am. I am the one who loves" (ibid.). Such implicit polemic with Descartes's Cogito ergo sum is actually and consequently a hypostasis of jouissance through truth, a glorification of incantation beyond knowledge. An apology of the shimmering of signs set on the instability of water: scription challenges the One and the closed space, it is a cry of necessarily short-lived freedom, which is nevertheless called upon . . . "Speech," "Fragments," "Cantata": through Valéry Narcissus survives, rehabilitated, as a poet.

Wilier, more elliptical, more ironical, also more fascinated by woman, Mallarmé was perhaps the first of our modern writers to transform Narcissus into his "virgin, undying, and beautiful" space of "today." If he conjures him up explicitly only in The Gods of Antiquity, where he makes out only "the silence or deafness of deep sleep," it is possible to read The White Water Lily as the heterosexual version or inversion of Narcissus. At the outset one encounters the poet, with "his eyes focused inward on the complete oblivion of going," who ends up nevertheless in "a few clumps of reeds," "a damp, impenetrable refuge," "the mysterious conclusion of (his) journey." Mallarmé's smile, an enigma that links the irony of a turn-of-the-century professor with metaphysical disillusionment faced with the emptiness of symbols—this smile is without doubt what changes Narcissus' sad chthonian flower into a White Water Lily, a white beacon, colorless light of the powerlessness in reaching the other. The smile is the confession of a fear that has barely been overcome: "I smile at the beginning of a slavery relieved by a feminine possibility." The "watery prowler" enjoys the delights of solitude together: "Separated, we are together: I interfere in some of her embarrassed privacy, in this abeyance over the water where my dream causes her, undecided, to linger." "To sum up with one gaze the virgin absence scattered over this solitude . . ." Are there two of them? Who can tell? There remains the certainty of "a happiness that will not come to pass," with the water lilies "wrapping up a nothing within their hollow whiteness." Let the "rape of (the) ideal flower" be accomplished: indeed. But that has nothing to do with Valéry's praise, mentioned above, of the unique essence-body. The body, the sex, that "imaginary trophy" Mallarmé implies, "swells with nothing else but exquisite vacancy of the self." This does not at all deter him, on the contrary, it encourages him to "love," "pursue," "any lady who stops sometimes and for a long time, as on the edge of a spring to be crossed or of some ornamental pond." Even though Narcissus is in love, the white water lily that disguises him reveals nevertheless the emptiness of his space. Mortal there, ironical here, the impossible merging does not deserve death. "Victoriously fled from beautiful suicide . . ." Like all our contemporaries, Mallarmé inherited the narcissan psychic space. But that space is henceforth empty. "Nothing will have taken place but the place . . ."

Closer to Valéry, to whom he dedicated his Traité du Narcisse, André Gide takes hold of the myth and extracts from it an essay in symbolist esotericism. His Narcissus, who still seems to owe nothing to Dante aside from recalling a same tradition, "dreams of Paradise." The fantasy of an androgyne before sexualization, Narcissus is now the "Adam of perfect spaces"; he "is solemn and religious." A necessary being, for he expresses the inevitability of expression, he is nevertheless guilty to the extent that he favors what is expressed, the form, the symbol, over the idea that is being expressed. The poet would have the ability to see, through narcissan appearances, the essential and stable, harmonious and true forms, and for that very reason he would be the holder of paradise. Scandalous but absolute. Devoted to the surface of the gaze also to the truth. "The poet is the one who looks. And what does he see?—Paradise." The Work, a fatal, numerical, "paradisiacal and crystalline" Form, is not simply an appearance since it is a symbol. "Has it been understood that I call symbol—everything that seems?"

Surely one will have understood that Gide is the anti-Mallarmé par excellence. To the narcissan vacancy of the latter there is substituted, beyond the "empty skies" that are nevertheless conjured up, the full completeness of symbolic meaning. To Mallarmé's hollowing out, Gide's contemplation. To the shattering of the self, of the body, and to the laughter with the former, the autoeroticism of the latter.

Two paths are open to the psychic space of today's Narcissus, Narcissus the artist—the "watery prowler"—or the religion of the Self. The first is more ironically than mythically narcissan: decentered as it is, it opens up the space of thought to the labyrinthian and muddy canals of an undecidable sailing, of game-playing with fleeting meanings and appearances, with images . . . The other is psychologically or speculatively more narcissistic: dependent on a lay religiosity, it has God descending within Narcissus and imposes the Self as the pedestal of a new religion—generalized symbolism. All appearances "mean," therefore the Self is sacred. Esoteric art is the culmination of Christianity, which had removed Narcissus' guilt. If we still have a religion today it is an esthetic one, for Narcissism shields itself most intensely in the fleeting displays of fictional meaning.
