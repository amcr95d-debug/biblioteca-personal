IV

God Is Love

The law of love, issued from the Bible, suggested the following to Christians: "You shall love Yahweh your God with all your heart, with all your soul, with all your strength" (Deut. 6:5), as well as, "You must love your neighbor as yourself" (Lev. 19:18). On this basis a true revolution took place, doubtless dependent on the waning Hellenistic world, but above all on a new, unprecedented, scandalous, insane attitude, which transformed Greek Eros and biblical Ahav into Agape—Christian love.[1]

A Disinterested Gift

Paul is responsible for the most precise and most specifically new expression of this unprecedented attitude that the synoptic Gospels certainly include but do not make clear. Indeed, the word agape (which in Latin is rendered as caritas—a term that has been watered down far too much in Christian tradition, both theological and popularizing, hence prompting us, today, to return to the Greek word so as to emphasize its novelty) appears only twice in the Gospels (Matt. 24:12, and Luke 11:42), and with a meaning close to the one it has in the Old Testament—ordered love, deserved love.[2] Now, at the same time, as early as the Gospels, Christian love is definitely a disinterested gift. Far from needing to deserve it or to fear its withdrawal by God, the Christian is assured of being loved, independently of his merits. Could that love also—especially?—be a love for those who are unworthy?[3] Such a concept of theocentric love, as opposed to human deserved love as it is to an eros aiming at happiness, is indeed in the making in the Old Testament;[4] the fact remains that in the Book of Psalms, "the way of the wicked is doomed" (1:6) and there is no place for them in the Law of the Everlasting.

Paul, to whom we thus owe the new definition of God as God of love,[5] puts his definition together by means of a triple motion that has been brought to the fore by A. Nygren.[6] Paul begins by downplaying somewhat the love of man for God, and he emphasizes the Gospels' theocentrism: instead of urging man to love God, he postulates that it is God who loves, and disinterestedly so. Next, he reveals—and this is doubtless the strongest point of his thought on love—that the disinterested gift the Father's love consists of is, all things considered, the sacrifice of the Son—the agape is the agape of the Cross (Rom. 5:6—10). Finally, it is love for one's fellow men, including not only one's kin and the righteous but enemies and sinners, who are to be loved as we love ourselves, which is to crown this scandalous renunciation of happiness; nevertheless, it proves to be the most direct way toward allegiance to the Father—not as Law but as Noun, Word-and-Love. A nominal immersion. Christian baptism.

Let us take a closer look at the dynamics of Paul's thought. Is it still, for us, scandal or insanity, as Christ's Passion was, according to Paul, in the eyes of Jews and Greeks?[7] Do we still remain Jews and Greeks? Is there a post-Christian actuality? The dilemma of love and its invisible presence, which overcomes us, a presence that is inexpressible and throbbing on account of its weakness, leads, in the final analysis, to such questions.

In the love relationship we are dealing with, the stress is thus placed on its source, God, and not man, who loves his creator. This displacement will even entail a change in wording: for Paul, finally, it will no longer be (on the man's part) a matter of agape but of pistis—faith. God is the first to love; as center, source, and gift, his love comes to us without our having to deserve it—it falls, strictly speaking, from heaven and imposes itself with the requirement of faith.[8] Thus defining God through his love leads one, as a first step, to modify the position of the Law and, without forsaking it, to think out its "accomplishment" or its "fullness," which would be love: "Love . . . is the answer to every one of the commandments" (Rom. 13:10). It logically leads to an identification of God with love,[9] even if it is with John that the phrase "God is love" finds its rhetorical development.[10] The essential moment of this theocentrism is the inversion of Eros' dynamics, which rose toward the desired object or supreme Wisdom. Agape, to the contrary, inasmuch as it is identified with God, comes down; it is gift, welcome, and favor. Agape comes closer to Jewish love, for it is indeed true that the biblical, paternal God is the one who chose his faithful.[11] I shall return to the deep meaning that positing such a gift-love might have for the speaking subject, as it is originally set in motion by an Other who turns his suffering into an offer and a welcome. Let me emphasize here its indebtedness to biblical love but also the difference that separates the two: agape is disinterested, less a choice than benevolent generosity, a fatherhood that isn't stern but familial and enlightening. Love toward the ungodly is the best demonstration of the primacy without initial reciprocity of divine love. "A man is happy if God considers him happy, irrespective of good deeds" (Rom. 4:6).

Nevertheless, the inaugural gift and the one for which there is presumably no reward consists, what is more, in the sacrifice of a body—and that is the other distinctive feature of Christian love, one that is, strictly speaking, incomprehensible within the context of ancient religiosity. It is the body of Christ, of the son. Love is accomplished by means of a death that is temporary, to be sure, and yet scandalous, insane, inadmissible. Such a love does not aim at eternity but resurrection, shouldering in its trajectory the low point of the annihilation of the loved one. Is this masochistic madness, sacrilege, the end of the divine in the sense of the untouchable? The end of strictly forbidden things and for that very reason the end of the Almighty?[12]

Death Is an Immersion

The gift of love through sacrifice is the reversal of sin: "But the gift itself considerably outweighed the fall. If it is certain that through one man's fall so many died, it is even more certain that divine grace, coming through the one man, Jesus Christ, came to so many as an abundant free gift" (Rom. 5:15). To the Law that causes sins to be plentiful ("When law came, it was to multiply the opportunities of falling," Rom. 5:20) there is thus substituted the plethora (pleonasmos) of grace through gift-love.[13] Authority woven of Law and sin is replaced with abundance, wealth, and generosity of grace: "Sin will no longer dominate your life, since you are living by grace and not by law" (Rom. 6:14). Plethora, immersion (baptisis, baptisma) (Rom. 6:3), abundance: love first asserts itself, in its polemic with sin and Law, as an exchange, a settling, a merging, a reconciliation (Rom. 5:10—11).[14] Such an identification-reconciliation with the supreme ideal leads the faithful to a grandiose hypostasis and at the same time deprives them of their "former selves," which is the lustful body.[15]

The movement combining increase and deprivation is indeed very far removed from the classical concept of sacrifice.[16] Not only is the sacrifice temporary (the body of Christ will be resurrected in its integrity) but in addition, and beginning with the immersion of the faithful in Christ, what the faithful allows to die is only a lustful body, the erotic body, in order to recover, through resurrection, the body in its integrity but completely invested in the ideal. If there be loss, it is both total (it is not part of the body, or even objects useful to the body, but the entire body that has been done away with) and null and void: a simple step in the agreement ("reconciliation") with the God-Father; an internal antithesis within the triadic motion that, starting with a love set down since the beginning of time, will take over the negation and culminate in the synthesis of resurrection.

Homology/Analogy

The Christly passion and, by homology, any passion that ends up in death, is thus only evidence of love and not a sacrifice stemming out of the law of social contract. Sacrifice is an offering that, out of a substance, creates Meaning for the Other and, consequently, for the social group that is dependent on it. Conversely, passionate love is a gift that assumes total suffering and loss, not in order to make of it a metaphorical assumption toward the Other but to allow a Meaning, always already there, anterior and coming from above, to manifest itself to the members of the community that share it. If sacrifice is a metaphor (obliteration of a concrete substance so that an abstract meaning might come to the sacrificers), passionate love is the experience of becoming homologous, a baptism. Paul comes out with the notion when he speaks of the immersion of the faithful in Christ, in his death as in his life: "If in union with Christ we have imitated (homoiomati) his death, we shall also imitate him in his resurrection" (Rom. 6:5).[17] God analogous—or more precisely homologous—to man has to be mortal for a time, in order for mortal man to become analogous to God. The bond that any religion establishes between the divinity and its believers becomes here an identification (Einfühlung with Freud; see One, "Freud and Love," above), and because of that very fact its name is love. Not sacrifice.

There is no idealizing identification without the murder of the loved object, which, in this homo-logical situation, is also a killing of oneself: that is what the agape of the cross has come to lay down. This internalization of murder is its consummation, its consumption, too. Is there a difference from masochism? Jubilatory suffering inflicted on one's own body by a supreme and cherished authority probably is the trait they have in common. The fact remains that agape-passion traverses that economy with the certainty that it can go beyond it. That it can remain within analogy, that is to say in a logical, nominal identification with the Other, without necessarily having to carry out in the reality of the body itself the obsessive return of destructive pleasure.

Narrative vs. Fantasy: A Crystallization

Circumscribed within the unique experience of the Son with a capital S, this killing of my own body in order for Me to be equal to the Father is by the same stroke removed from fantasy, which belongs to me alone and grabs me in my immeasurable pleasures and losses. On the contrary, the passion of the cross, raised to the level of universal narrative, is the vanishing point of fantasy: its unloading into a universality that, on the one hand, forbids me to take myself for Christ (it short-circuits fantasy), and on the other, introducing into the preconscious the idealizing necessity of one's own death, at once favors my leap into the Name of the Father. A subtle machinery for idealization rather than repression stricto sensu, agape-passion turns into the erotic unleashing of the death drive only when a narrator—an I—takes it upon himself to recompose the Writing of the Universal Subject and join his own name to the Name of the Father. Like the mystics, like Sade or Artaud calling themselves Christ in a rage as loving as it is destructive.

For the believer, on the contrary, the killing of his own body as a condition for ideal identification is, so to speak, suspended by the evangelical narrative: it is at the same time crystallized, brought to the fore and immediately fastened to the experience of Christ and of him alone. Inhabited by Christ, "adopted" by the Father, the believer puts to death only his sinful body, on the path that leads him to agape.[18] Putting one's body to death is thus an episode of adoption—a theme that is essential for the understanding of agape, for it highlights a paternity that is not of the flesh but of the name: "The spirit you received . . . is the spirit of adoption, and it makes us cry out, 'Abba, Father!'"[19] The son, and the believer who identifies with him, will be welcomed by the Father, made homologous to him (this is a second-degree identification) beyond the sacrifice of the body and as very condition for that sacrifice. The Father who loves me welcomes me as pure spirit, as Name, and not as body. The expression, "Abba, Father," reminds us of the words of Jesus on the Mount of Olives, shortly before his arrest, and later during his last moments on the cross.[20] This painful adoption for the Son whose human disarray, even though it lasted only an instant, suggests that he would have preferred sparing himself the passion turns out to be, because it makes the Son homologous to the Father, an election. Consequently, it is a powerful parry against any suffering previous or inferior to death.

After saying this, what can we add? With God on our side who can be against us? Since God did not spare his own Son, but gave him up to benefit us all, we may be certain after such a gift that he will not refuse anything he can give. Could anyone accuse those that God has chosen? When God acquits, could anyone condemn? Could Christ Jesus? No! He not only died for us—he rose from the dead, and there at God's right hand stands and pleads for us. / Nothing therefore can come between us and the love of Christ, even if we are troubled or worried, or being persecuted, or lacking food or clothes, or being threatened or even attacked. As scripture promised: For your sake we are being massacred daily and reckoned as sheep for the slaughter. / these are the trials through which we triumph, by the power of him who loved us. (Rom. 8:31-37)

My Body and His Name

A triumphant "we," freed of anguish and defying death, rises from this adoption-love and sets up the believer as subject of the Other—of a signifier purified by passion. Never without it, but beyond it. Who could ever forget, indeed, that the Name of the adoptive Father is nourished by the corporeal passion of the Son, that pure love is channeled through the nullification of the entire body, which is by the same token destined for resurrection and yet put to death for the time being? Christianity is a nominalism only if it affirms an invigorating love through one's acquiescence to being put to death. Such an accepted death is the high point of negative narcissism carried to its limit (accepted death), and that doubtless explains why such an activity of narcissistic satisfaction (negative as it may be) could lead to imaginary salvation. The obliteration of the body and of the Body's image are nevertheless hypostatized in Christ, and this leads to the abolition of the Self (of the body-Self) and at the same time to its renewal within the Subject who is the Adopted Son through the Name of the Father: "I live now not with my own life but with the life of Christ who lives in me. The life I now live in this body I live in faith: faith in the Son of God who loved me and who sacrificed himself for my sake" (Gal. 2:20).

Christian love is an idea that changes my body into a worshiped name. The killing of the body is the path through which the body-Self has access to the Name of the Other who loves me and makes of me a Subject who is immersed (baptized) in the Name of the Other. A triumph of idealization through a sublimatory elaboration of suffering and of the destruction of the body proper, agape marks, for that very reason, the end of sacrifice. Or rather, it neutralizes it by means of subjective internalization: by a working-through in the Gospel's narrative.

That, in short, is how what Nietzsche called "that ghastly paradox of a 'God on the cross'"[21] turns out to be, on a deeper level, a sublimatory overtaking of the masochistic position inasmuch as it conditions idealization.

The Neighbor as Oneself

The "we" that takes the place of this immersed Self in order better to position itself ideally, a Christian we, finally elaborates itself through the third step of agape: love of one's neighbor. Dependent on the logic we have just skimmed over, the love of one's neighbor contains an additional element that completes the ideal relief of narcissism. "The whole of Law is summarized in a single command: Love your neighbor as yourself" (Gal. 5:14).[22]

The prescription to love one's neighbor as oneself is not specific to Paul's writings: it is found in Matthew (5:43) and Luke (10:27), and its first expression goes back, as I have pointed out, to Leviticus (19:18). It is true, moreover, that the advice has nothing to do with egoism in the common sense of the term, and Paul emphasizes disinterested agape (1 Cor. 13:5: "It is never rude or selfish"). Nevertheless, the Judaic message has already been modified: not only do neighbors include foreigners as well as sinners, but the "oneself" carries a more personal, more individualistic resonance, so to speak, in the context of a Christly experience of an incomparable and yet exemplary Self, a focus of attraction, identification, and glorification. This "personalist" aspect is precisely what will be elaborated by theologians, particularly, although differently, by Augustine, Bernard of Clairvaux, and—masterfully in his apology of the "proper"—Thomas Aquinas. The Self becomes a center of reference (the others will be loved as the believer loves himself) and consequently removing guilt from the love of self does not constitute narcissism or sinful egoism but can assert, at least under certain circumstances, that it is worthy of God's love. Furthermore, the love of Self is the prototype of any other love, to the extent that theologians have wondered which of the two is primary.

The description of Christian agape is now complete. By glorifying a self placed within an adoptive Father after having shed the body-Self covering, agape builds psychic space as the complex space of a subject. The absorption of narcissism within the image of a Oneself stretched out to include neighbors, foreigners, and sinners will have been the final touch brought to this structure where the dynamics of internal life may henceforth be played out: construction-deconstruction, life-death . . .

Paul and John

The Pauline revolution drew from John's letters both its most striking formulaic expression and an ambiguity that attests to its fragility and contamination by other conceptions. Striking turns of phrase indeed, which carry one step further the identification, already implicit with Paul, of God with love by making it explicit: "My dear people, let us love one another since love comes from God and everyone who loves is begotten by God and knows God." (1 John 4:7); "This is the love I mean: not our love for God, but God's love for us when he sent his Son to be the sacrifice that takes our sins away" (1 John 4:10); "We are to love, then, because he loved us first" (1 John 4:19). The theocentric concept of agape and the primacy of God's love for man are here stated with the greatest of emphasis. This theocentrism assumes, as Nygren stresses, cosmic dimensions, for John tells us that love existed before the creation of the world: "You loved me before the foundation of the world" (John 17-24). Prior to the existence of any object whatsoever (of any "world"), agape, as love of the Father for the Son, is thus other than love of an object: an internal turn within the subject, its dynamic, its consummation, its very potentiality. Nevertheless, there is added to this quasimystical love a Johannine love that is closer to merit love and motivated love for one's brothers: "Because the Father himself loves you for loving me and believing that I came from God" (John 16:27). Now this slippage remains, or so it seems, dominated by the idea that brotherly love is not a love for fallen strangers in the world but for beings that belong always already to the Name of the Father. "I pray for them; I am not praying for the world but for those you have given me, because they belong to you: all I have is yours and all you have is mine, and in them I am glorified" (John 17:9-10). Johannine love sketches out the space of a relationship between I and You, Son and Father, an exclusive, absolute one in which They—third parties—are only intermediaries. Probably never has the giving love of the Father, shining on the idealization and ideal becoming of the Son, been asserted with greater clarity. Refusal of the world seals that apotheosis of ideality, as John's Letter states: "You must not love this passing world or anything that is in the world. The love of the Father cannot be in any man who loves the world, because nothing the world has to offer—the sensual body, the lustful eye, pride in possessions—could ever come from the Father but only from the world" (1 John 2:15-16). Possibly what is outlined here is a sublime Platonic Eros, a celestial Eros lacking his burden of carnal lust, exclusively opening up idealizing perspectives for an important trend in Christendom. This is not to suggest that the passion of Christ is absent from John's writings. But in opposition to Paul, who was originally impious, the stress John places on departing from the world appears to save him the painful, flesh-imbedded, and exorbitant violence of the Pauline agape, which was mainly conceived as a passion of the cross.

Orality Dedicated to the Father

Identified with the Eucharist or not, but always establishing a communion by means of food, agape became in a later Christianity synonymous with the communal feast among Christians. An orality not satiated as desire but symbolically appeased: the devouring and assimilating of the other transformed into fullness, reconciliation with excess—that is what the amorous meaning of a banquet reveals (Plato's, for instance). Turning love into an identification with the ideal father and having that identification based on an absorption, an oral assimilation of his body, introduces into Christianity a relief of oral sadism directed at the archaic maternal body. The mother will not eat you, do not eat her; look for the sign of the father within her, do not let it scare you, but liken yourself to that crossroads that is both body and name, desire and meaning. You will become sensible, that is, enamored of yourselves, Him, others. Christianity, by thus inserting a Third Party between the Self and its destructive hunger, by setting up a distance between that same self and its nurse, offers to destructive avidity—a Word. Language. Individual speech arises only under those conditions, and it is on account of speech that voracity is assuaged and shifts into satisfying identification of the same with the other, put to death but exalted as equal to the Third Party in the loving bond. Henceforth love will be a discourse that takes death-dealing hunger into account, builds upon it but lines it and, by displacing it into a symbol, goes beyond it. Christian love: a more-than-hunger in the Father's bosom . . . Sublimation of homosexuality, abduction of the mother's nourishing attributes, and subordination of imagination to the sadistic protection of the One. You may let him be killed, but I eat him, we become reconciled. Loving idealization is a symbolic oralization beyond sadomasochism . . .

An Early Renaissance: The Amorous Twelfth Century

The twelfth century was indeed a singular age; courtly love flourished along with at least three great amatory doctrines. The one by Abelard (Introductio ad Theologiam, 1121; Commentary on the Epistle to the Romans, 1136-40) advocated sacrificial love, including humiliation and contempt for oneself provided the loved one joys and glories in it. That of Guillaume de Saint-Thierry (De Contemplando Deo and De Natura et Dignitate Amoris, 1118-35), while remaining faithful to the Abbot of Clairvaux, proclaimed its own concept of natural love: it makes us equal to God and enables us to know ourselves through the knowledge of our own greatness. Above all there was Bernard of Clairvaux (1091-1153), because of the central position this soldier of God held in his century, equally so on the roads leading to Jerusalem as on the paths of a renewed faith that lighted up his age (De Gradibus Humilitatis, 1125; De Diligendo Deo, 1127; In Canticorum Sermones, 1135-53); his doctrine, in line with the intransigency of an early crusader, imposed upon Europe the idea of man as an amatory subject.

Before examining a few aspects of Bernard's thought, one cannot help wondering over the peculiar contemporaneousness of courtly literature with this gathering of mystic writings. Are they related, is their presence a simple coincidence within the very large framework of Christian culture, are there limited influences, or does one represent a reversal of the other? Etienne Gilson argued against the attempt made by some to establish a connection, or even an analogy, between the religious and the worldly aspects of love in the twelfth century.[1] To summarize his argument, the main objection he raised was that, within the framework of religion, and even more so of Christian mysticism, love was all beatitude, in opposition to the pure love of courtliness, which in its very purity and above all else is renunciation and evil. For the mystic, God having loved us first, our love can only possess this very God who is its supreme object and thus attain bliss—provided, of course, that it be what it must be: this happy, fulfilling possession constitutes its purity. On the other hand, courtly love, disinterested and pure as it may be, is predicated on a renunciation: the loved Lady doesn't necessarily love her bard, and the troubadour, for his part, has no desire to possess her, and he assesses the value of his love by the degree of his frustration. To such a big difference one might add this: courtly lyrics extol love between created beings, human love, even if as time went by the Lady assumed more and more the traits of the Virgin Mary, while mystic love is directed at an extraordinary, ideal, immeasurable object, and only by inference can it involve the proper self or one's neighbor.

Nevertheless, this rational refutation leaves one somewhat uneasy; the more so as the reader of courtly poetry, which enigmatically opened out and wilted within the span of a century in the south and southwest of France, allows himself to be carried away by the ardor not of the statement but of its courtly utterance. In Marcabrun as in Jaufre Rudel and Guiraut de Bornelh, one is dazzled by the incantation's intensity; courtliness is a song of joy, of joi, before being a message to a stately, more or less approachable Lady. The convergence between a lyrical jouissance that is internal to the very incantation and the theme of the joi on the one hand, and the experience of love on the other, was discovered by Paul Zumthor.[2] It suggests, moreover, if one goes back to the context of amatory mysticism in the twelfth century, that all objects are not impossible for the troubadour, and that all beatitude is not forbidden to him. The troubadour is at least sure of possessing the Word, within which, precisely, he carves out his being as lover. Identification with the song-Word and the Creator, a joy of incantation-creation, is the only perceptible evidence—but is there objectively any other?—for beatitude in general and courtly jouissance in particular. It is at any rate evidence of a sense of fullness, if one wishes to relate the finality of love in troubadours and amorous Carthusian monks.

On the other hand, if we now consider religious doctrine itself, the sense of beatitude and assured possession of the object does not prevent degrees of love having been set; these suggest variations in the fullness of feeling, or even imperfections. Gilson noted it but as he did not find the same degrees of love progression with mystics and with troubadours, he concluded that the analogy was not an essential one. And yet, beyond formal differences, the common conception of a love with progressive stages leads one to think that, for the lay person as well as the mystic, love is a violent wrenching of lovers one toward the other, an alienation as dramatic as it is jubilatory. With Bernard, for instance, love, without being an affliction, as it is for a troubadour, is anchored in affect and desire and, far from erasing the lack that defines pure courtly love, introduces it into the very heart of love's violence for the purpose of overcoming it. For a commentator who devoted eighteen years of his life to the Song of Songs, as Bernard of Clairvaux did, love is also an affliction—and this whatever the fully and normally theological reasons for that conflict-spawning dichotomy, reasons that are bound to the corporeal nature of man and to his fall.

The difference between religious and worldly doctrines of love in the twelfth century is therefore perhaps not as drastic as one might have been led to believe by an interpretation concerned with preserving the theological orthodoxy of mystic writers, on the one hand, and the strict secularity of courtly behavior on the other. Beyond the question of influences, what seems more interesting is the social and theological climate of that age, which must have allowed a matching genesis, if not a reciprocal contamination of the two discourses. Beyond the affirmation, Ego cogito . . ., which Descartes handed down to us in a straight line originating with Thomas Aquinas and whose fruits and miseries our age has gathered, such a climate reveals another one of man's practices at the very heart of that first renascent or precolonial expansion that the crusades constituted for the West. Saints and troubadours seem to proclaim, Ego affectus est, thus glorifying what, in the light of Reason, will appear to be base irrationality. They impart willpower to their love, enlighten it with reason, tinge it with wisdom, in order to raise it to the dignity of a divine essence. And man, uncertain, passionate, ill, or happy identifies with that affect. For God is love . . .

What Is an Affect?

Bernard's language is not as rigorous as the one handed down to us by Thomas; with him, the same word may cover broad, and sometimes important, differences in meaning. I might add that his thought, which is of course essentially Christian and consequently leads him to understand "love" as meaning God (since conversely God is love), is nevertheless indebted to the rhetors and moralists of the Ancient world. Cultured people in the twelfth century would refer to Cicero and his De Amicitia to find that friendship is the benevolentia that wishes the good of its object outside any considerations of usefulness—it is a virtue, it is based on the similitude (resemblance) between partners and does not come into being without reciprocity. They learned their amatory code in Ovid; the twelfth century has been rightly called aetas Ovidiana.[3] Ovid convinced them that love is natural, in the sense of being innate, and the only thing there is to learn about it is not how to arouse it but how to purify and strengthen it. Finally, they also and especially read the Scholastics, who considered love to be an affectus cordis; as the contemporary meaning of the word "affect" suggests, and contrary to twelfth-century theology, they saw it as the exclusive domain of feeling and sensitivity.

While love is, with Bernard, one of the four affects (along with fear, sadness, and joy), his notion of affect carries with it a sometimes ambiguous complexity that will enable us to approach the density and sometimes the conflicts his experience and conception of love lead him to. First, we must note that affect (affectus) is located in the soul while being considered one of its attributes. Moreover, distinctions are made between affectus animae, affectus mentis, and affectus cordis. As with the other writers of his age, the affect, with Bernard, is dependent on the senses as much as on will since the speaking being, to the extent that it is a spiritual being, is driven by one single logic.

As bond between man and externality, God, and things, the affect is a notion closely related to desire. The difference between the two may reside in the following: desire, as we shall see, emphasizes the lack, whereas affect, while acknowledging the latter, gives greater importance to the movement toward the other and to mutual attraction. Aelred, abbot of Rievaulx and disciple of Bernard, gave the following definition: "Affect is a spontaneous affinity with someone, hence it emanates from the heart itself."[4]

Moreover, Bernard often used the word affectiones with the meaning of affect, while giving it a more specific signification: it refers to the various feelings, made up of affects, that the soul has for God (thus: timor, spes, obedientia, honor, amor). "Note that among the worst ills by which men are overcome the Apostle includes the absence of affection (reference to Romans 1:31)," Bernard wrote in Sermon 50 on the Song of Songs. He singled out three types of affection: one issues from the flesh, another controls reason, and finally a third brings in reason. "Concerning the first, the Apostle says that it neither is nor can be subjected to God's Law," Bernard wrote in the same Sermon (50:4), and we will often encounter in his writings such a statement about a first degree of affect or love that is stubbornly resistant to divine law.

Passivity-Passion

Before returning to this point, which is one of the most interesting and most debated ones in his doctrine, let me note that the affectus, as the name suggests, is for the twelfth-century thinker basically passive. An outside agent is needed for the soul, thus set in motion, to show an affect in response. The origin of affects is external to man: Ipse dat occasionem, Ipse creat affectionem.[5]

All in all it is difficult to find a contemporary equivalent for Bernard's notion of affect; on the one hand, it seems to suggest a very primitive component, one that is very rudimentary and essential to the bond between subject and Other; on the other, it is at once posited not only as acquired, as the result of an external action, but also as intrinsically spiritual. Pathos, if one keeps its passive aspect in mind; drive, if one thinks of its primitive nature, unamenable to the law, a component of desires; love, if one considers the final stage of purified affect. One is able to appreciate the complexity involved, which some call a contradiction, by the following definition, among others: "Affects, simply called, are found in us naturally, it seems as though they emanate from our own being, what completes them comes from grace; it is indeed quite certain that grace regulates only what creation has given us, so that virtues are nothing but regulated affects."[6] At least at certain stages of his reasoning Bernard considers the four affects as liable to produce effects that are not orderly but muddled and vile—pathetic, drive-related? "There is no human soul without those four affects (love, joy, fear, sadness); but with some they are a matter of shame, for others one of glory. Indeed, if they are purified and well ordered, they are the glory of the soul under a crown of virtues; if they are disorderly, they are its embarrassment, its humiliation, and its disgrace."[7]

Nevertheless, such a possibility that the affect might be shielded from regulating will is put aside in other definitions, which assume the immanence of affect within spirituality. "Learn from me, for I am gentle and humble in heart," Bernard thus quoted Matthew (11:29), and commented: "He said 'in heart'; in the affection of the heart, which signifies the will."[8] On account of incarnation, and by bearing witness to his love for us in his flesh, Christ becomes the prime motive for our renouncing the concupiscence of the flesh, the vile affect, deprived of will, which invades us because of nature and the fall, and for choosing the contemplation of His passion: ". . . indeed, what can there be for him [man] that is so sweet in the flesh when he finds such sweetness in the passion of Christ?" Yet such an argument, absolute as it is, did not seem sufficiently certain to Bernard, who was well aware of the powers of concupiscence: "But such fondness may be mistaken," he went on, "if it is not accompanied by prudence, and it is not easy to be on one's guard against the poison that happens to be mixed with honey."[9] Reason comes to the aid of affection for the Christly flesh in order to equip the believer with prudence on the path toward pure love.

Area of Dissimilarity: The Bovine Body

Thus for the Christian, a human being imprisoned in his flesh, the affect of the flesh is already significant; even though it might be the zero degree of our love for the Other, the affect is already dwelled in by Christ. One notes the very tight bond between flesh and spirit that is instituted, for Bernard, by the notion of affect, thereby translating the exquisite dialectics of the Trinity which is outside the scope of this work. The point is to avoid removing the flesh from a spirituality that would thus become too ethereal, without forgetting, nevertheless, the presence of the spirit in a flesh that composes an area of fundamental dissimilarity between man and God.

Borrowed from Augustine, as Gilson has established,[10] the notion of regio dissimilitudinis signifies with Bernard "the area of sin and difformity of the lost dissimilarity," and this implies, on a deeper, ontological plane, a true loss of being. "The noble creature that was crafted in the area of dissimilarity but in the likeness of God did not understand that it was honored and allowed itself to fall from similarity to dissimilarity."[11] But Bernard's frequently coming back to the exile of man in a sentient, animal creation in no way suggests some sort of dualism of thought or a suspicious kinship with Michael Baius or Luther in the matter of an incurably sinful nature, unamenable to grace.[12] In fact it testifies to Bernard's simply anthropological (and not only theological) concern with the dynamics of amatory passion as a corporeal as much as spiritual passion. Nevertheless, this carnal affect liable to disgrace if not well ordered seems astonishingly contemporary because of the immanence of the signifier; indeed it suggests the great distance separating Christianity from the Platonistic or neo-Platonistic universe, both of which eventually renounce the body. "I think this is the principal reason why the invisible God willed to be seen in the flesh and to converse with men as a man. He wanted to recapture the affections of carnal men who were unable to love in any other way, by first drawing them to the salutary love of his own humanity."[13]

Does Bernard's concept of the body convey the influence of Ovid and Cicero and, in the background, Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics, which proclaimed the natural appetites (epithumia) that govern, according to him, friendship and love? Or else did Bernard's very own experience as a chronically ill person remind him that the body, on account of stomachaches and frequent vomiting, is the unsurmountable presence of a remnant of death? Or more simply and still more courageously, was it the composite, heterogeneous, and subterraneously conflicting nature of incarnational Christianity that led Bernard to pay attention to the dissimilarity, if not the duality, that characterizes human experience and of which love is the dramatic expression? The fact remains that the loving thought of this warrior was destined to subsume a body that he does not hesitate to liken to a cow. "There are two places for the reasonable soul, the inferior one, which it rules, and the superior one, where it rests, and it is God . . . Our body finds itself located between the spirit it must serve, and the desires of the flesh or the powers of darkness, which wage war on the soul, as a cow might be between the peasant and the thief."[14]

The more the mystic goes beyond such a bovine body, the more he assigns to it its place of animal remnant, the more the "cow" imposes itself upon the affect and love, even though they are administered, dictated, implanted by the grace of the Other. "Those who were reared in the purple claw at the rubbish heaps."[15] "Be ashamed, my soul, for having exchanged divine resemblance against that of beasts, for wallowing in the mud, having come from heaven. Look at me, says the body, and you shall feel shame."[16] If there is a sin, it is not that of carnal affect, even if it is most natural, but that of the "warped" soul,[17] which seized it at the very source of its being . . .

Desire: Violent and Pure

Another notion, complementary to the affect, nevertheless comes and ties itself to the dramatic notion of love in Bernard: desire. Profusely developed in On the Song of Songs but already fully present in the treatise On Loving God, the notion sums up what Augustine himself understood by the same word: rerum absentium concupiscentia ("concupiscence for absent things").[18] Bernard first considers psychological desire as being germane to any man who longs for what he does not have. And while placing desire for God on a different plane, he sees this mystical desire become rooted in the initial human desire, which is when all is said and done a "voraciousness."[19]

The desire of corporeal, insatiable senses, however, is ordered by reason, which warns them and sets them on another path. "It is no less folly to think a rational soul will be satisfied rather than merely puffed up by any kind of material goods."[20]—"He makes you desire, he is what you desire";[21] "He offers the opportunity, . . . and consummates desire."[22]

Such a quest for God as desirable or lacking object, a quest nevertheless inspired by him, underlies all the commentaries on the Song of Songs. Lacking but fulfilling, absent but giving satisfaction: such is this extraordinary desired object that leaves the paths of psychological human experience where Bernard, along with the ungodly, had begun to understand it. The desire for God finally proves to be fulfilling if and only if the lover (the believer) recognizes that God desired us first to the extent of creating us in his image. That is why God is defined as also desiring, and desiring first: "The wedding feast is ready. . . . God the Father awaits us, he desires us not only on account of his infinite love—as his only son who is in the Father's bosom tells us: My Father loves you—but for himself, as the Prophet says, I shall do this not for you but for myself."[23]

Consider this mirrorlike motion: my desire will be fulfilled through Him, for He has fulfilled his own by creating me in his image. The specularity of Christian love and the sublimating resumption of narcissism that it produces are common: they will show up again in Thomas Aquinas. A loved ideality that is meant for love is brought within reach of human experience by referring to the immediate objects that assuage the desire of individuals. We meet again with a kind of "primary narcissism," surmountable only through the positing of an Other who is supposed to have existed before us and who is not unaware, on his part, of that longing for self-gratification and total gratification. Let me recall, in this connection, a hardly orthodox but highly significant expression of jubilatory identification of the lover with his God, in a prayer that has been attributed to Augustine: "O Lord, if I were God and you Augustine, I would rather that you be God and I Augustine."[24]

Completeness and Deprivation

Thanks to the originary specular affection for that Other, only thus may desire for an absent being—an avid, total, impossible desire—be guided by will and wisdom and become a desire that is pure. Pure, with Bernard, means fulfilled by merging with the Loved One—God. "Let your actions, your zeal, your desire be like lilies in their ingenuousness and their scent."[25]

But let us not forget that completeness in beatitude, both presupposed and reached in a final stage, also includes the whole of human experience with deprivation as preliminary to identification with the ideal Object: "The desire that urges her not only to arise but to hurry is a violent one. . . . The Bridegroom does not therefore awaken her against her wishes as he begins by giving her a desire to do so, inspiring her with an impatience to behave in a saintly fashion."[26] There is no better way to stress the dissimilarity, the heterogeneity between the lover and the friend, the bride and the bridegroom, man and his God, than to emphasize the desire that causes the one to lose composure and faint, cry, moan, and fret in an attempt to join the other: "When a soul of this sort has often sighed, or rather prayed without interruption and suffered because of desiring the divine presence so much. . . ."[27] Such a suffering produced by the lack of the other is the indispensable lining of beatific satisfaction, assumed and accepted. Suffering would thus condition jouissance, while jouissance would be the spur of a new suffering quest.[28]

Is this the masochistic dialectic of jouissance under the command of an ideal as loving as it is basically stern? Overtaken in turn by bliss—that merging identification with the ideal, ad unum—it nevertheless partakes of an exceptional impulse toward stability and limitation of desire. Without repression but assuming even the most paroxysmal states of passion in order to express them. Such harmony is exceptional and remains confined to the twelfth century and a few chosen ascetics, in spite of the later popularity and the belated insipid vulgarization of Christian love. What an admirable success, however, one that unleashes desire at the same time as it frustrates and soothes it by giving it an object in the tempo of its intemperance—an infinite Object. I is like that Infinite. To love comes in here in the place of to be and as: copula and comparison, existence and image, truth and deception. A drifting together of the symbolic, the real, and the imaginary.

The Stages of Love: Primacy of Carnal Love

Bernard seemed to distinguish a natural love, determined by the lover's human condition, and a sublime love, indebted to God. The ideal primacy of the second is dependent upon the precedence of the Creator as well as upon the precedence of his love for us; this is obvious and indisputable for a Christian. Nevertheless, turning his attention to the human experience where, as a precocious Renaissance man, he wished to root human experience, Bernard defined carnal love as primary, de facto if not de jure. "This is carnal love by which a man loves himself above all for his own sake. He is only aware of himself; as Saint Paul says: 'What was animal came first, then what was spiritual' (1 Cor. 15:46). Love is not imposed by a precept; it is planted in nature. Who is there who hates his own flesh? (Eph. 5:29)."[29]

Such a de facto if not de jure precedence of carnal love gave rise, as one might guess, to various interpretations and disagreements. Some, like Gilson, tend to minimize its importance and blame it on a clumsy turn of phrase; they give greater importance to what is indeed implied everywhere—the logical precedence of divine love (of God and for God). Others, such as P. Rousselot,[30] emphasize the necessary duality between man and God that such a primacy of carnal love presupposes. Paroxysmal expression of the regio dissimilitudinis, as one might call it, this duality conditions another psychic particularity that may be observed in the amatory state, that is, the extent to which man must force himself in order to go beyond himself, to be emptied of himself, in his love for the other.

A reading that is sensitive to Bernard's originality must note not only his conformity with the Christian canon but even more so his particular contribution to classic doctrine. This interpretation seems essential, leaving aside the ambiguity of Bernard's wording, simply because access to supreme love is a process involving tearing oneself away from earlier carnal love. Upon that carnal state of love follows what one might call a love of need: "Man, therefore, loves God, but for his own advantage and not yet for God's sake."[31] And yet the trials of life lead to a third stage where one joys in the sweetness of the Other: man thus comes "to love God not for his own advantage but for the sake of God."[32] Finally the fourth stage is the one in which man "no longer even loves himself except for God"; by means of this apparent renunciation he exorbitantly enlarges love of self to the dimensions of God, as proclaimed in the Psalms: "My flesh and my heart faileth: but God is the strength of my heart, and my portion for ever" (73:26).[33]

A state of harmony, of deification (see On Loving God, 10:28, "It is deifying to go through such an experience"), of the body's transformation into "a spiritual and immortal body, calm and pleasant, subject to the spirit in everything" (On Loving God, 10:29), such an apotheosis of love is in the final analysis a violent transformation of carnal love into a jubilatory state of Self idealization through successful identification with the Other.

Several texts emphasize the painful nature of the wrenching that leads to jouissance in the Other: ". . . those who have purified the cesspool of their soul must not conclude that they have therefore been themselves wholly purified; on the contrary, it is then that they experience the need to purify themselves often, not only with water but with fire."[34]

It will be noted, moreover, that the point is not to love but to love oneself and even more so to love oneself in God.[35] A state of jouissance for souls that have freed themselves of the body,[36] love in this fourth stage does not actually renounce the body but alters and displaces it to join with it elsewhere: "It desires to have its body back, a glorified body."[37]

Seizure: Evidence of Dualism

The term seizure points to this displacement of souls outward from their bodies, the body's potential nevertheless acting as a permanent source of violent and contradictory stimulation. "The strength of this love seized their souls so entirely that, despising the pain, they were able to expose their bodies to exterior torments. No doubt, the feeling of intense pain could only upset their calm; it could not overcome them."[38] This subtle account of a doubling, of which amatory ecstasy is a critical realization, continues up to the description of beatitude. Here it is the permanence of desire that indicates the insistent presence of the flesh—uncontrollable affect, yet sublimated. "Here is fullness without disgust; here is insatiable curiosity without restlessness; here is that eternal, inexplicable desire knowing no want."[39]

Does this mean that forcing carnal love to rise up to this fourth ecstatic stage implies that the carnal love one tears oneself away from is basically evil? Those who first try to minimize Bernard's dualism of sorts show that such is not the case. Indeed, Bernard distinguishes between amor that acts (urget) out of necessity and a negatively connoted cupidity that carries away (trahit).[40] Bernard, however, often gives to love the attributes of cupidity and concupiscence: "Since we are carnal and born of concupiscence of the flesh, our cupidity or love must begin with the flesh."[41] I should point out that this distortion of a love that, in principle, can only be noble because created by God is due to the fall, it is therefore secondary and not, as Bernard would have it, necessary and previous.[42]

Valid as all those arguments doubtless may be within the framework of Catholic theology as well as in the whole of Bernard's writings, that does not erase the ambiguity of his thought. One may claim that the ambiguity is due to awkwardness, that the necessity of a previous carnal love, which Bernard postulates, is only a moral one (caused by sin) and is not essential; that would be eminently accurate provided one gave a Thomistic reading of Bernard's text. But Bernard remains within ambiguity, torn between Self and Other, flesh and spirit, lust and harmony. The entire scope of his love as strain, progression by stages, transition, is indeed inscribed within a dialectics rather than a dualism that would be unaware of the precedence of the Christian One. Nonetheless, the presence of carnal heterogeneity is such that Bernard even goes so far as identifying any love (whether of kin, friends, enemies, or God) with one of the five senses; he put sight above all others, for it is "the one of our senses that bears the greatest resemblance to God's love."[43] The carnal implanting of love is thus posited with the greatest ingenuousness and eloquence.

With respect to this implanting, the freedom of love resides indeed, according to Bernard, first and foremost in the freedom of the will that draws it away from carnal desire and organizes it according to grace and to reason directing the senses.[44] Free will and grace allow us to rise above ourselves: "For it would be better for us not to be rather than always be ours."[45]

Holy Violence: Heresy or Freedom

But the freedom of love, as love and not as reason, also lies in the relative independence of flesh, affects, and desires. They are God's Signifiers as much as they are heterogeneous to his Meaning, they stand up to will and reason, and that resistance makes them the mainspring of love as well as free will. Freedom to love, freedom not to consent. Bernard emphasizes that in love as in religious experience in general, an ascent is as necessary as a descent: "He alone has risen to heaven who first descended from heaven."[46] Love thus appears as an area of freedom because it accepts the protagonists' dissimilarity and even their conflict, in the same way as it aims for the identification of the one with the image of the Other—of man as an animal centered in the precedence of his needs with an ideality that is nevertheless deified because it is assumed to be accessible.

Bernard opposed Abelard, who hypostatized the duality of lover and loved one and consequently demanded the humiliating submission of the one to the glorifying perfection of the other. With Bernard, to the contrary, deification is possible through harmonious identification with the Loved One, accessible beyond violence. He thus heralds Aquinas. But in opposition to the rigorous speculative abstraction of the latter, Bernard takes into account the reality of the human condition as constituted by sin. An astute psychologist more than he is a philosopher, Bernard does not dodge the deep tendency toward egoism and narcissism but heads straight for the theological difficulty that might be outlined as follows: taking our depravity into account, we must reach the city of God. Indeed, governed by the higher inclination toward the good and toward God, our fallen nature yet remains rebellious. Without that resistance we would not have needed the holy violence that constitutes love in order to reach the ideal. Without it Christianity would be a philosophy of the good, eventually a rationalism, not this passion of the body wrenching itself which is called love. The words of Paul could not fail to touch those twelfth-century theologians who were lovers more passionate than rational: "I shall destroy the wisdom of the wise and bring to nothing all the learning of the learned. . . . For God's foolishness is wiser than human wisdom."[47] And Bernard went even further: "What a violent, consuming, impetuous love this is! It thinks only of itself, disregards everything else, despises everything, is satisfied with itself! It confuses stations, disregards manners, knows no bounds. Proprieties, reason, decency, prudence, judgment are defeated and reduced to slavery."[48]

It thus seems that Bernard's doctrine stands equally removed from two others. It is as distant from Fenelon and Baius's heresy, which stresses "evil nature" so that love might destroy it and "pure love" be reached only at the cost of desire and personal happiness, as it is from the Pelagians' heresy, which considers that the natural bent of man is already to love God, and this reduces grace to being a complement of a fortunate nature.[49] Love in Bernard's style appears, in short, to be like a hyphen constituting the specificity of man as nature-and-meaning, body-and-ideality, sin-and-divine-grace. Let us recall one of the many expressions of that ambivalence: "We also love our spirit in carnal fashion when we break it through prayer, with tears, sighs, and moans. We love our flesh with a spiritual love when, after we have subjected it to the spirit, we exercise it spiritually for the good and watch with judgment over its conservation."[50]

Maintaining the heterogeneity of these limits, together with subordinating them to the essential precedence of divine ideality—this is what makes of Cistercian mystique, more than any other doctrine, the adequate and powerful means to define man's being as lover. Neither sin nor wisdom, neither nature nor knowledge. But love. No philosophy has equaled the philosophical success that could give satisfaction to drive-impelled narcissism while raising it above its own realm to give it an extension that could reach the other, the others—a divine extension, to be sure, and a social one in passing.

Morality and Bliss

For Kant, desire for happiness comes under the heading of Rousseauistic sensitivity, and if we must fulfill our duties toward ourselves and our neighbors in order to gain happiness in future life, this will just be as if it were ordered by God. For in the final analysis man settles his obligations through the autonomy of his will. Bernard posited the immanence of God's love in our nature, carnal and greedy as it may be; this is lacking with Kant and causes the loss of what one readily calls the "unconscious" part of love, at the same time as the loss of amorous happiness as the essential definition of the human event. Moreover, Kant dissociates our bliss, for which God has created us according to the philosopher, from the glory that God gave himself in so doing; Kant believes that the opposite would assume too much egoism on God's part. Now, precisely, this authorization to be ideally egoistic, to mirror oneself in a God who also loves and joys—this ensures with mystics the harmony of desire and possession, which is loving happiness.

Even Spinoza, the great philosopher of bliss (see section titled "Spinoza's Joy" in "Ratio Diligendi" below), who places identity between intellectual love of the self and love of God, diminishes the conflictual and jubilatory richness of love as it is found in Bernard. For Spinoza's identification of self-love with love of God, and even love of God for himself, is immediate and thus tends to obliterate the two rims of ecstatic contradiction, several ranges of which we have skimmed through; as a consequence, free will and merit on the one hand, sin on the other, find themselves excluded . . .[51] The ethic that is firmly grounded in understanding neutralizes the amatory hell that thunders with Bernard . . .

Nevertheless, and beyond the theological and philosophical implications that Bernard's writings may have had and still have, do they throw any light on the lay experience of love—the daily, human experience, the humdrum and the singular one? What do we care, what did the ordinary people of his time care about his ravings over the stages of amatory perfection, when we are overcome by the joys and the pains of love?

The often ambiguous, some call it clumsy, emphasis on the precedence, indeed the resistance of carnal affect in the experience of love does of course first point to the share of pleasure and desire that is included in any amorous exaltation that is basically idealizing. Bernard seems to say, do not get love, pleasure, and desire mixed up; if you desire you are not necessarily in love, for loving implies a certain wrenching of the self for the sake of ideal identification with the loved one. But in a more underhanded, nocturnal fashion, such stress on the flesh that withstands amatory idealization and disinterestedness opens love toward that which eludes consciousness, knowledge, and will: toward the region that today we are quite ready to call the unconscious. Love is therefore the allowed expression, allowed because included within the good, of the uncontrollable force of the unconscious. Thus to define man's being is an act that, beyond the theoretical and philosophical field involving subjectivity, enriches the real-life experience of love, in the same way as do language, rhetoric, and literature—as terrains of an accepted madness.

Furthermore, when boredom sets in at the sight of Bernard climbing the terraces that lead him to the pure love of God, let us not forget that within our own loves, in addition to and beyond desire and pleasure, we are caught within an alchemy of idealization, of whose twists and turns we are secularly ignorant. If we have difficulty loving, it is because we have difficulty idealizing—difficulty investing our narcissism in an other who is considered to have immeasurable value, thus guaranteeing our own potentiality for immoderation. And on the contrary, when we succeed in loving, is it not because someone, man or woman or child, or a word, a flower . . . was able to withstand our unflagging power of distrust, hatred, and fear of delegating ourselves to an ideal otherness? Finally, are our own stages of affection for our loved ones so different from the ascent toward pure love that the twelfth-century Cistercian devoted to his ideal object, his God? . . .

Before turning this Bernardine page, which, save for those few who have chosen to live outside of time, is only a museum page, let us once more admire the exceptional balance between a voracious self and a tyrannical ideal, an unsatisfied desire and a nevertheless assured possession. Such a taut peace, painful harmony, narcissism of the body-Self, infinitely swollen only to be emptied for the benefit of a violent identification with a sublime alter ego—that is love. A radical means of treating the affliction as a symptom or as madness, by placing love-sickness at the heart of the human being? Ego does not yet know how to be because he thinks . . . "I" is because I love. An I that, moreover, cannot be defined, seduces in the grand manner, but does not point to itself as basic identity, split as it is between the Other and the affect. An I that is passion. Ego affectus est. "I sense that my words please you" (Bernard of Clairvaux).

Ratio Diligendi, or the Triumph of One's Own.
Thomas Aquinas: Natural Love and Love of Self

One loves a thing insofar as it is one's own good.

(Summa Theologica, II, ii, 26, 3)

But everything which already has being naturally loves its being and with all its strength preserves it.

(De veritate, 21,2)

"I Love in Order to Be"

Beyond the specious arguments in which theology had to become involved when pondering over God's love, there are at least two aspects of its reflections that concern the contemporary reader. Such a reader will, on the one hand, decipher a powerful attempt to come to grips with love of self as both the necessary and limiting basis of all love.[1] He will, on the other hand, come upon the conviction that it is possible to set up, instead of the disappointment that any amatory quest unfailingly provokes, an absolute ideal Object, beyond which bliss is guaranteed along with desire.

The speaking subject is a loving subject—that is what those ontologies of love appear to postulate. We do know that medieval man cannot write without loving and that his love is best realized within the stirrings of signs that make up the amatory text—as literary historians have demonstrated when sounding the legacy of the troubadours; even so, we have difficulty crossing the barrier of Descartes's Ego cogito ergo sum in order to conceive, beyond the latter's luminous lucidity, another subject that might define its being as amounting to the mixture of affect, desire, and meaning—love. I am to the extent that I am loved, therefore I love in order to be: that, for the medieval thinker, would be an implicit definition of the subject's being, if he could express a subject's thought. Indeed, it is doubtless a Cartesian conditioning that prompts us to dream of such a pre-Cartesian loving subject. Nevertheless, beyond this theoretical ghost, does one not actually see a subject emerge out of Aquinas's works, a subject that is knowing only to the extent that it is loving? It is a complex dialectic of passivity and activity, effect and cause, subjection and freedom, fall and grace . . . But it is also an exquisite blend of affect, desire, and meaning, spliced together in such a manner as to mark out, while bonding them, an inside and an outside, a flesh and a spirit, a "one's own" and a "being good" ... To say that such elaborations soothe and take over from secondary narcissism (perhaps thrusting their effects as far as primary narcissism) is a blunt statement that could shock the believer and bother the analyst as he finds himself involved in a discourse to which he is opposed. It remains nevertheless true that what has been defined as the nonanthropological, nonpsychological aspects of that love could be understood only thanks to recent developments in psychoanalysis; by incorporating psychotic experience and fitting it in at the basis of psychicism they have allowed us to reach its inhuman intelligibility, so human nevertheless, and also, taking things the other way around, permitted us to consider post-theological, secular means of sublimating our sickness of being, which is a sickness of love.

Already with Augustine, if it is true that sin comes out of self-love, the good way to love is still to "love oneself" "for and because of God," or "within God."[2]

To Love Oneself or the Other: In Praise of One's Own

The privilege granted to self-love goes back, in philosophy, to Aristotle.[3] The primacy of self-love that permeates so many theological discussions finds with Thomas Aquinas (1227-74) a dialectical solution whose skillful compromise we shall appreciate here.

Referring to Augustine in his Commentary on Sentences (1254-56), an early work, Thomas in fact subscribes to the postulate of self-love's precedence, a love that would open a natural path for individual experience to have access to ontological goodness.

Thomas was doubtless well acquainted with Bernard's positions; leaning on Augustine and Aristotle, he devotes distinctions 27, 28, and 29 of the third book of Commentary to the primacy of self-love. After having refuted the possibility of loving God with concupiscence ("God is the good of man" must not be understood, Thomas warns, as if it were a useful good, but as a whole of which man partakes, "of" indicating an objective genitive), after having brushed aside the temptation to love the friend for the sake of the merces, the reward (for that would be concupiscence and not friendship), Thomas, in article 5 of distinction 29, clarifies the hierarchy between the three kinds of love. Our own proper good is found, according to the Angelic Doctor, in God as to its cause, in ourselves as to its effect, in our neighbor as in a similitude. Consequently the greatest good is God, but the first access to him comes from our immediate relation to ourselves; moreover, the similarity between others and ourselves permits us to have access to them. Self-love, in this logical concatenation to which Thomas subjects it, enjoys a historical or genetic primacy. But God remains the absolute proper, the best of myself, more myself than me, the absolute Self. Ecstasy would then be defined in a precise manner: the Self is its point of departure, but leaving the Self is its condition. "Love thrusts man out of himself and establishes him within the one he loves: with love, the one who loves always neglects himself in some way or another," an objector puts forward, quoting Dionysius the Areopagite (Divine Names, 4:13). Thomas proves that it is the opposite (sed contra) by defining ultimate love as a conciliation between ontological good and the genetic primacy of the self. If it is true, he goes on, that in love the loved one as such comes before (potius est) the lover, the fact remains nonetheless that each person can, out of self-love, come before any other loved one, provided he loves himself more than any other, and that is what happens if one is for oneself one's favorite loved one. There is nothing egoistic here, as far as Thomas is concerned, for that moment will allow one to settle within oneself, in other words to be within the ontological good that is accessible in the first place as one's own—to be good. Indeed, how could one convey such goodness to others unless it be firmly established in oneself? This praise of one's own proper good, indeed of one's Own as Good, is expressed as follows: "It is true that the good that I have may be recognized more perfectly in another than in myself. And yet it is found more perfectly in myself as my own. The other's good is never mine, save by similitude, while the good that is in God is also mine, according to cause."[4]

Settling Within Oneself

One should also read carefully, on the topic of self-love, the very specific interpretation that Aquinas gives of the well-known sicut te ipsum ("You must love your neighbor as yourself") when he elaborates on the love of the angels. First he postulates that even if love is uniting and consequently, as Dionysius made clear, it could not simply be directed to the self, nevertheless to be One is more than to be united. This means that love of others originates in love of self, and this not only because love of self might be the model but because in loving itself the subject affirms its ontological solidity (being good).[5] Consequently, loving as oneself will be analyzed on the basis of notions of proximity (being with, getting together, meeting again, convenire cum) and similarity (particularly, love of the species). All in all, sicut te ipsum does not mean "as much as yourself" but "similarly as yourself." The identifying purpose of this point will be appreciated, as it specifies the place of the Self as a center that is projected on others.

It is thus love of one's own self as one's own good that determines and directs other consecutive loves. This love of "one's own self," however, should be conceived only as participation in ontological good, which obviously refers to God but is also to be interpreted as preservation of the species. The created being loves, as it partakes of the species: it is preserved through love, and because it is "of God."

The notion of self-love thus holds a pivotal place in Thomistic ontology and causes the esse suum and the bonum proprium to flow into one another.[6] If I am, I partake of being; if I have being, I can only love it as my own, and by preserving it I preserve myself. Now it is because being is good that everyone remains in it.[7] Love of self expresses the desire for preservation in being and in goodness; it is therefore the convertibility agent for being and goodness. Within it, and through the valorization of "one's own," the perception of being as goodness is carried out. Love of self is what establishes one's own as a unity as well as one's own good—a lovable unity. Such a being, generally speaking, and the (good) being of self are in the final analysis basically desirable.

The Desirable Being

Any being is desirable before being knowable or while it is knowable. Better: it is being itself that the feature of desirability suits best—appetabilis. Consequently, everyone naturally desires to preserve his being; he flees what tends to destroy it and resists that to the fullest extent. Thus, being itself, as desirable, is the good.[8] My being is no less so: I perceive and desire myself as a perfection of my own. I am, which means I am good, therefore I love myself—that is how one might sum up the praise of one's own, which includes what in contemporary terms would be called narcissism in an ontology of love.

The immediate experience of one's own as presence of the good in the self is thus the only one that, in the self, allows for exchange as reciprocity, friendship, or even love. Esse is an esse bonum, hence appetabilis. The Self, in its ontological immanence, is equally so: good and desirable. The being as good—which presumes nothing concerning the true being—at once leads to the own being through the expedient of desire, which naturally "appropriates" what is good. If there is (a) good, I can perceive it and know it only as good for oneself. I am therefore a part of that good, and that assertion is synonymous with my natural appetite for the good. As such, I love myself. Beyond its psychological value for justifying and removing guilt from controlled narcissism, love of self thus presents itself, with Aquinas, as the logical go-between that internalizes the good at the same time as it ontologizes the Self as one's own always partaking of the good. A desiring subjectivity is in the process of being established, and nevertheless, conversely, the ontological immanence of each being to itself is at the heart of this theology.

Charity and One's Own

One might, however, legitimately wonder if love of self, second to love of God but first in the human experience of having access to the good, is not surpassed by charity, that peculiar blend of friendship and the supernatural. The Treatise on Charity (in Secunda Secundae of the Summa Theologica) confirms nevertheless the inescapable, because natural, presence of self-love in charity as well. Article 4 of question 25, "Whether a man ought to love himself out of charity?," restates that love of self is the "form" and the "root," the formal cause, of any love. What the precedence of self-love is dependent on, according to Aquinas, is not only the goodness of the Self, which we had noticed above, but what he states in spatial terms—the proximity of self to self. One loves oneself because one is better (as one gathered from one's own experience of taking part in the good) and because one is closer to oneself than anyone else. The objection nevertheless remains even in that spatial definition of self-love: should not the self's preference for self disappear in the life of glory since we are there closer to God than to anyone? "On the contrary" Aquinas answers, "Nature is not done away to be perfected by glory. Now the order of charity given above is derived from nature: since all things naturally love themselves more than others. Therefore this order of charity will endure in heaven."[9] The primacy of self-love thus persists within charity, including charity for those "who are better than we are."[10]

Thus, without access to one's own good there can be no access to God. Without self-love, no love for God can be perceived or thought, and consequently no gift of love to others.

One is tempted to understand such reasonings as psychological, and a reading that is heedful of the preconscious or even the unconscious finds it legitimate to do so. Nevertheless, in attempting to remain faithful to the Thomistic perspective, one cannot overemphasize the expansion of the Self that loves itself, and thus justifies itself, to the dimensions of being; love of self allows Aquinas to locate being at the heart of the Self. Conversely, the analyst will decipher, according to his wont, the reverse of the coin: if I love myself, it is of course as part of the Whole, but it is especially because I consider myself to be a good as worthwhile as the Whole good. The similarity between the part and the whole, the individual and the universal, based on the good, founds, for Aquinas, the absolute value of singular individuality. The tremendous respect for the singular that he thus evidences rests upon the acknowledgment of an imposing aspiration for being (part of) the Whole. Acknowledgment of our preservation by a mega-appetite? Of our megalomania?

Natural Love Is a Judgment

What meaning does Aquinas give to the phrase "natural love"? In the Summa Theologica (Prima Pars, question 60) he clarifies, in connection with the love of the angels, what might be applicable to man, under the heading of a dilectio naturalis. While such natural love involved, with Bernard, a number of ambiguities (acknowledgment of concupiscent flesh, sin, or a base nature yet already imbued with divine presence), the equivocation persists with Aquinas, but the effort to be more precise is not comparable. For him, "natural" first means what is related to the primitive affect previous to will. "Natural" is also the opposite of theological love, that is, charity. But as Roger de Weiss points out, "natural" also refers to what is opposed to bestiality. The desire to preserve oneself forever (perpetuo manere) is termed "natural." Finally, "natural" is the attribute of will itself, will considered as natural: "All naturally want bliss." Human beings, although intellectual, cannot escape that natural "bent"—thus they experience a natural love.

One might say, in short, that natural love is defined by its object, the good, and its end, bliss. It is included among the acts of will as their principle and cause.[11]

That sort of naturalness in love allows one to note, as may be done in connection with other aspects of Aquinas' doctrine, the extent to which love and knowledge are intermingled. Even when "sensitive appetite" and "intelligential appetite" are maintained as different powers.[12] Aquinas carries to its apex the identification of love with knowledge already affirmed by Aristotle, Cicero, and more plainly yet by William, abbot of St. Thierry.[13] The logic of the argument that compares love and knowledge is enough to have the latter absorb and subsume the former. Aquinas thus suggests that if the intellectual being is capable of reflexiveness in knowledge, he is also capable of reflexive intentionality in love of self.[14]

To say that self-love is natural implies in the final analysis that it is a component of will itself as guided by reason and wisdom.[15] The heterogeneity of carnal desire as opposed to the will for Meaning, and of course knowledge (a heterogeneity that is in evidence in Bernard's work), becomes obliterated here. With Aquinas, amatory desire is always already willful, because it is appropriated by one's own good, of which God constitutes the absolute presence. If concupiscence remains one of the categories within which he operates, it is only an accident of the love substance; even concupiscence no longer has anything sensuous about it, it is simply a change or, more precisely, a partialization of the loved object (one truly loves when one loves the object of friendship for itself, one loves with concupiscence when one loves it for the proper good of the one loved in friendship).[16]

Amatory Reason

While desire and love thus always turn out to be shaped by intelligence (if only on account of the inescapable presence of will), reason, in turn, is underlaid with love—it is a ratio diligendi. As such, it has God for object but if and only if he is my own good. Nevertheless, for God to be my own good, or for my own good to be a ratio diligendi, I must love myself. Otherwise, assuming that I do not love myself, I do not love God. That is how the ratio diligendi is natural: it proceeds from the (natural) universality of self-love as indispensable access to the pre-existent supreme good. As can be seen, the thesis stating the naturalness of self-love is a consequence of the thesis, induced from the theology of creation, positing the being as good.

Creationary naturality implies the immanence of spirit in nature and subordinates all logical precedence (affective, for instance) to will and knowledge. Love of self is naturally directed toward the good, for it is part of the latter; it will necessarily be natural and virtuous. On the contrary, sin and the love of sinners for themselves is not a love, it is not "natural." For natural love is by definition synonymous with virtuous love.[17]

The assumption of self-love through one's own good is thus accomplished for the benefit of virtue: any activity not underlaid with designs on the good is therefore eliminated from the field of love and is termed unnatural, sinful.

Amatory Interest: A Convenientia

Love of self binds man to himself, but in addition it provides him with "something more" (Dionysius), that is, being one entity—a unit. And only following such achieved unity can union with the other be realized in friendship or in love.[18] Let us examine more closely this amatory union.

In what may be called, following Roger de Weiss, a "mechanistic" theory of love, Aquinas compares the attraction of friendship or love to what happens when a motor pulls toward itself an object, which is thus subjected to its action; the object is at that moment caused to incline towards the motor, then it moves, and finally it comes to rest. Thus, "in the movements of the appetite, the good has an attractive force, evil a repelling force. It is therefore the good that, in the first place, causes an inclination in the appetite for connaturality with the good. That is what concerns love, as passion, to which corresponds hatred, which is its opposite."[19]

Nevertheless this seeming mechanism is at once corrected by the notion of convenientia—a complicity bond between the motor and the moved one, the lover and the loved one. Man, as intelligent being, does not merely allow himself to be moved by the object, he judges whether the singular object suits the universal notion of goodness. In short, suitability is a judgment of suitability, a loving knowledge not only of self but of the loved one. Love is thus defined, one sees it once more, not subjectively (there is, properly speaking, no subjectivism in this ontology) but as a relationship nevertheless, one that precedes all affect and implies preliminary knowledge, a judgment, an intent.[20]

The relationality implied by the convenientia theory is expressed, as Roger de Weiss already analyzed, in the thesis of the two types of transcendentals: those depending on being in itself and those depending on being for another. Aliquid, bonum, and verum belong, as one knows, in the second category. The soul is that exceptional being that presupposes an aliquid different from the others, "such that it agrees with (convenire cum) every being"; hence it accomplishes the relationality par excellence constituted by the amatory function of agreeing with an other. "Such a being is the soul, which, as is said in [Aristotle's] The Soul, 'in some way is all things.' The soul, however, has both knowing and appetitive powers. Good expresses the convenientia of being to the appetite power. . . . True expresses the convenientia of being to the knowing power. . . ."[21]

Identification or Singularity

The Thomistic commentary on "Love your neighbor as you love yourself" must be placed within that framework. The following is taken from the Commentary on Sentences (III, 29:2.1): "Because love unites, as it were, the one who loves with the loved one, the former behaves toward the latter as toward himself, or toward that which concerns his own perfection." The motive of love for the other must therefore be that good that I aim at for myself. That is what leads me no longer to emphasize the ontological differences that separate me from others, but to go beyond even convenientia for the sake of identification and similarity.[22]

The triumph of Self in this total identification with the other is the hallmark of idealized and omnipresent narcissism. Beyond judgment and the amatory act of unification, it is nevertheless the other's unity that stands out, in love, as its compelling, supreme end. In that sense, the term "as," "sicut," does not point to equality but, as I already noted, similarity, and it prompts one to love the other as another singularity, a specific number, another oneself.[23]

Nevertheless, the mystical tradition of Dionysius, for instance, and probably one strand of Aquinas' own thought, compels the latter to consider love of one's neighbor not only as unity but as union. This last term implies that violence is done to the same so that, at the same time as he preserves himself, he disappears in the other. Love as wound is far from being an absent theme with Aquinas.[24] Loss of one's own, ecstasy, rapture, seething:[25] the theologian's metaphors tend to sum up that difficulty in his doctrine, which, on the whole, does not necessarily prepare one to consider the antagonism between same and other since they are ontologically presumed to be similar within the good.

A more specifically Thomistic approach, however, brilliantly brought to light by Roger de Weiss, explains amatory union on the basis of the demands of the knowing subject. By giving greater importance to the similarity and proximity of lovers in God, amatory union is not a dramatic union of substances but a merging of lives that were already God's: "But love encourages the union of things, as much as possible. For that reason, divine love causes man, to the extent that it is possible, no longer to live his life but that of God."[26]

In the final analysis, the Thomistic logic of love amounts to positing that because there is Unity and love for that Unity (of Self), there is also Union of the two (the loved one being identified with oneself).[27]

The Thomistic argumentation based on unity may be interpreted as a Platonistic approach, which sets in a causal relationship that which is a matter of formal hierarchy,[28] while it wrongly refers to Dionysius. As to the psychological aspect that concerns us here, let me say that the dynamics of Thomistic thought completes the sublimation of narcissism within an ontology of the good.[29]

A Soothed Love for the Other, Preparing the Thought of the Other

Whenever ontology ceases to be an ontology of the good and relegates the latter to the sole realm of morality, narcissism cannot be dealt with. The ratio that, thus deprived, is no longer amoris but cogitans can but condemn or forget the amor sui, fundamental as it yet is. Poverty and greatness of the knowing subject who repressed his advent as a loving subject. With Aquinas, the knowledge of the self loving itself has not reached this state of repression but it is an idealization, perhaps a sublimation for some of his followers—a masterful one at that. Altering the same as well as the other in the light of their union tempers the unity of each and disrupts everyone's own identity, even though it had initially and jealously been posited. This altering of "one's own" melted into the other at the heart of amatory union effects something like a reduction of the splitting that takes place within the loving being. Besides, it is given the highly sublimatory designation of "diffusion," "diffusibility."

It is generous, transitive, mingling the borders of substances without erasing differences in formal unities. Once more, the presence of a good that is "appetabilis" successfully performs that miraculous operation by lessening the differences between lovers. Up to a point, the other is merely an intermediary for the diffusion of the Other good in myself, of myself into the Other as good. An absorption of the other in the judgment of the good, such amatory philosophy brings down the violence of mystical dualism. Its unifying kindness prepares the reign of the subject that has been unified by his own judgment, no longer affective but knowing. The latter will tend to brush aside the appetite that preys on it in the esse bonum and maintain only his understanding of the esse verum.

The assumption of narcissism will have been the agent of that revolution, which, sublimatory in a theology of the good, acknowledges itself as repressive in a philosophy of the true. Of the Thomistic "one's own" there remains only the meaning of "unity" with the knowing subject who has forgotten the appetite for the good . . .

If we reread Aquinas we shall again discover that the thinking subject is a subject thinking the other, and as such it is analogous to the subject loving the other. Ratio diligendi and Ego cogito: both bear the scar of a narcissism that aspires to tear itself away from—or unite itself with—the other, as from or with its own good. Aquinas, however, reveals the delight of alienation at the very moment that he posits its impossibility in the citadel of one's own: of the good. The splitting between lovers, as the one within each lover, is immediately erased by the machinery of judgment pertaining to meaning and to one's own good. "Because the relation (of charitable work) to its effect is the good established in the other. From that standpoint, and to the extent that we consider the other's good as our own, because of the union in love, we take delight in the good that, for our sake, befalls to others, mainly to our friends, as we do in our own good."[30] Would amor sui as kernel of Thomistic love be the angelical treatment of alienation—a recommended paranoia, deemed necessary, and thus necessarily successful?

Lacan must have known something about this, since drives, for him, were already signifying. Lacan a Thomist? As Marx was a Hegelian? The one without God, the other without absolute Spirit? To no avail whatsoever, for the sake of the impossible, and with every chance of failure. For, if there is no Creator, where would this appetabilis good come from that would cause us to love? Whence the signifying "naturality" of desire and love drives? By brushing the Creator aside, we eliminate the foundation of the good. But how then might we aim at "our own good"? The cure has therefore no other meaning than an absurd one or an individual choice at every moment. Just as in "lucid" love. Ratio diligendi: I love my truth as you make me understand it.

Salvation: Hypostasis of "One's Own"

At this point one will appreciate the epistemological and psychological importance of Aquinas' valorization of amor sui. For nature to have been "created," or as some might say today, more modestly, for it to be "signifying," was not sufficient for one to propose an ontology based on positive values such as the good and the true. Without necessarily building, without yet building subjectivity, it was necessary to provide human experience with an access to that ontology. Hypostasis of "one's own," a gathering of universal good within the amatory appetite and the true within judgment, such as the intelligent being knows them, became a key point in the theological construct. Psychologically speaking, thus founding the participation of one's own in the universal good by way of love implied a normalization of narcissism—by removing guilt, thanks to an idealizing adjustment. Provided one acknowledges oneself as participating in, united with, or suitable to the supreme good, that absolute Third Party, love of self, far from being a deadly end or a disastrous snare, may turn out to be our way to salvation. To jouissance.

Apparently courtly literature does not share this inspiration. The disastrous passion of the troubadours—and what did they find [trobar] save the rapture of impossible possession of the good that could never be their own?—seems closer to Abelard's humiliation, than to Bernard's ecstatic glorification or Aquinas' logically identifying the self with the good. As I pointed out, however, poetic incantation is in itself an equivalent of the joi that these nostalgic bards extolled, thereby pointing to the jouissance of the one who speaks the amatory sharing of his ideal. The troubadour's bliss is "performative," it resides in the signifier based on the mezura that is mindful of the "lovers' good," of "lor benananssa" (Marcabrun) as much as of the harmony of his song, words and music combined. It is a signifier that relates esoteric exhilaration (Rambaud III and Arnaut Daniel) or is more directly gripped by the erotic or even pornographic act (Guilhelm IX and Bertran de Born);[31] it may be a respectful or nostalgic song that remains essentially oriented toward the contemplation of its relationship (unity-union) with the Other: the idealized Lady is the Other who idealizes me. The statement of this assuming link constitutes the troubadour as such: as lover and/or as poet. His existence has no other reality than that of the statement wherein he joys. It matters little if the Lady really exists or not, if he possessed her or merely contemplated her melancholically: he joys in speaking a unity-union that is dreamed, real, impossible—imaginary. Incantation is the only guarantor of his being and at the same time his only claim to glory.

A late text, whose writing is contemporary with the works of Thomas Aquinas, clarifies in naive and metaphorical fashion this relief of narcissism that had been attempted by theology as well as courtly lyricism.

Guillaume de Lorris' Romance of the Rose tells how the protagonist, a lyrical young man in pursuit of love (a kind of belated troubadour in the middle of the thirteenth century) looks at himself in Narcissus' spring at a crucial moment in his adventure. The death site that the Spring of Narcissus constitutes for Ovid's character becomes, thanks to the reductive as well as amplifying powers of the twin crystals it contains, a Spring of Love—a site for transformation and renewal. Indeed, the lyrical hero, on the basis of what he sees and beyond his own image, becomes transformed into the more and more reasoning character of the narrative that is to follow—he becomes a novelistic hero. A lover who is an adventurer and a philosopher. For Ovid's lethal water reveals to him, an inhabitant of Christendom, that beyond death there exists a Rose: an Object, a Goal of his amatory quest, external to himself and yet sprung out of the refraction of his own image. Might love be a beyond of one's own image that assumes it before metamorphosing it through reduction and amplification? A Coney Island of one's own? A beyond of narcissism, but which takes it into account? Born of lyrical desire with no object other than one's own self, which moreover is unaware of itself as own, narcissism may have access to another beginning with the moment when it is wounded by the sight of the Rose and the arrows of the God of Love. Henceforth the Lover gives up lyrical (auto-) celebration and embarks upon the novelistic conquest of his amatory garden. A narrative and logical conquest of a territory that is less the plain of subjective internality than a complex, social, and relational geography.[32] Poetry belongs to Narcissus, the Lady and the narrative belong to the love of object. And never the former without the latter. The relief of amor sui through courtly poetry and the novelistic literature it subsequently influenced marks out the gossamer that links them, if not with theological inspiration and finality, at least with the same obsession that the Middle Ages seem to have experienced during successful moments of grace—to preserve narcissism while preserving love and goodness, the love of the good. Can we accomplish as much today? For the love of what good? And narcissism remains, unattended and painful.

Spinoza's Joy: Amor Intellectualis

This survey, which stops here abruptly with Thomas Aquinas on the threshold of modern philosophy, has no other aim than to point out, on the near side of the grandiose Cartesian project, a few waverings of philosophical and theological discourse in the shadow of monotheism, which set forth a dynamics of appetency ahead of—or as much as—a systematics of knowledge. One might have followed, with Descartes, Spinoza, Kant, and Hegel, the surviving manifestations of that amatory appetite that, as a feverish pull toward the One and appropriation of the good, becomes distorted into esthetics or morality, into sublimity or jouissance . . . Let us leave them in abeyance—arbitrarily, temporarily, as in a loving passion that is satisfied, that is spoken, only in part . . .

Nevertheless, before tackling a few myths that govern our loving imagination, let us open the pages of that atheist who, while positing that God is the true being, defined the true as understanding itself—Spinoza (1632-1677). Thus partaking, as a part of infinity, of his Deus sive natura, human understanding is fully aware of its being in love with that divine infinity. Does not Spinoza's human spirit, the concept of body existing as action, discover by means of the third kind of knowledge that "Blessedness consists in love towards God"?[33]

The intellectual love of the mind towards God is the very love with which He loves Himself, not insofar as He is infinite, but insofar as He can be manifested through the essence of the human mind, considered under the form of eternity; that is to say, the intellectual love of the mind towards God is part of the infinite love with which God loves Himself.[34]

It is obvious for Spinoza that God does not love, properly speaking, for there is no object external to him, but he loves himself, and it is by partaking of that truth that understanding reaches its goal—salvation. Therefore Spinoza will not define love, as Descartes had done, as the "will of the lover to join the loved object," but as follows: "Love is joy with the accompanying idea of an external cause."[35] If there is will, it is here understood as a satisfaction (acquiescentia), as a step toward joy and bliss and not as optional decision or as desire. But what needs to be stressed is the setting of the "external cause," in love with itself, both object and motor of love according to Spinoza. For it reveals how the amatory impulse of joy is an identification of one's own self with God (sive Natura): without a Cartesian falling back toward a mastering subjectivity, but through jubilatory submersion of understanding in an object or an infinite cause that is joyously in love with itself. "Hence we clearly understand that our salvation, or blessedness, or liberty consists in a constant and eternal love towards God, or in the love of God towards men. . . . For insofar as it is related to God, it is joy . . . accompanied with the idea of Himself."[36]

If there is only knowledge, it is a love—an intellectual love, amor intellectualis—that relates the self (whose body should not be forgotten) to God. Only thus may knowledge lead to the ethical horizon: to the joy of salus. Between Aquinas' ratio diligendi and Spinoza's amor intellectualis it would seem that a real transformation has taken place, that of theology into ethics. This does not suggest removing love from logic when the latter does not give up bliss—jouissance. A discreet jouissance with Aquinas, a radiant, innovative one with Spinoza.

Everything else is mere imagination, shadows, and fables that unfold the deadlocks of love—its delights.

Note that Plato uses this term when, discoursing on love, he posits that if the loving soul perceives an omoioma, an imitation, of things from the celestial world that resemble those here below, the soul is outside itself (Phaedrus, 250 a). Assimilation, transformation (omoiosis), and even what represents (omoiotikos), are thus, from the origins and in Christianity in a very accentuated manner, linked to love. It is the same relation of omoiosis that governs the relations between the divine Names, as well as, as seen in Rom. 6:5, the relations between God and creatures. All medieval thought is dominated by the enigma and the difficulty of omoiosis which, under the Aristotelian name of analogia, preoccupies theology (let us recall Duns Scotus and Saint Thomas, cf. further on, pp. 167-184 but also Eustache de Saint-Paul, Suarez, etc.). If it appears essentially as the privileged mediation of the finite/infinite relation, analogy is precisely the opposite of a logical, rational relation aiming to establish a foundation or a cause. Better: the theological doctrine of analogy eliminated the search for causality since it substituted itself for it. One can follow, however, the slow erosion of this notion, which led to the advent of modern rationalism, passing through an internal evolution within theology up to what J.-L. Marion has been able to call a "white theology" (cf. J.-L. Marion, On Descartes' White Theology, PUF, 1981). To the theological relation of an omoiiosis constitutive of creatures in love (not in reason) and necessarily incommensurable (since the Word homologizes the infinity of God to the finitude of the creature), thus leading to the unthinkable, is progressively substituted the search for a proportionality. Aristotle's analogy, a particular case of metaphor, finds its privileged application in mathematics (Poetics, 21, 1457 b, 6-9). The pivot of analogy would be a certain presumed unicity, the common point of view that allows the unification of inclinations. (Cf. further on p. 253 sq.) One will pass through analogia entis (analogy in being) and through analogia fidei (analogy in the faith of God revealed by Jesus). Duns Scotus, and Suarez who refers to him, posits as the basis of analogy the principle of being as immediately perceptible and common to God and creatures: but he turns univocity and indistinction to the concept, to the "conceptus entis"... a path that will lead to Descartes. Saint Thomas, for his part, would defend against a God-cause the similitude: "it produces according to its strength effects that remain similar to it, not univocally but analogically" (I Sententiarum, d.2, q.2, solutio). The analogues will then be thought as relating to a common perfection and yet unequally possessed (per prius in God, and per posterius in creatures). Finally, God as causa sui, causa sive ratio, in Descartes will be the last compromise between homologizing amorous theology and nascent rationality that emerges from analogy to give a foundation to cogitation, a foundation that is nevertheless still gaping, infinite, unthinkable. One will read, on this evolution and its pitfalls, Marion's fine book. Let us retain, for our purpose, the decisive role played, in this movement, by the precision of omoioma as analogy. Pauline thought, which includes on this point a Platonic echo, while being theocentric, presupposes, by the notion of resemblance-semblance-image-representation, a relation at once differentiating and unifying identities within the divine Trinity, but also between the creature and the Creator. This notion of semblance—of homology—is, properly speaking, inconceivable, illogical, unthinkable, outside of the agape that is its condition. Let us say that it concerns the subjects of enunciation in their amorous bond, and not signifieds or signifiers that are formally analogical or metaphorical, which, for their part, are thinkable starting from the cause-effect relation centered definitively on the ego cogito.
